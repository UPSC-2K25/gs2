Delimitation

1. Meaning and Constitutional Purpose

Delimitation is the process of fixing or redrawing the boundaries of territorial constituencies from which representatives are elected. It seeks to ensure that constituencies broadly reflect population distribution and that the value of representation remains reasonably balanced.

In a representative democracy, population changes over time through migration, urbanisation and demographic transition. Delimitation is therefore connected with political equality, federal balance, democratic representation and the distribution of legislative seats.

Delimitation is not merely a cartographic exercise. Decisions about constituency boundaries can influence political representation, the balance between regions, the representation of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and the relationship between population growth and political power.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Article 81: Provides for the composition of the Lok Sabha and the constitutional framework for allocation of seats among States and territorial constituencies.
  • Article 82: Provides for readjustment after each census, subject to constitutional provisions and parliamentary law.
  • Article 170: Deals with the composition of State Legislative Assemblies and readjustment of constituencies.
  • Article 330: Provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha.
  • Article 332: Provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Article 327: Empowers Parliament to legislate on elections, including delimitation of constituencies, subject to the Constitution.
  • Article 329(a): Bars courts from questioning the validity of laws relating to delimitation of constituencies or allotment of seats made or purporting to be made under Article 327 or Article 328.

Delimitation must be understood alongside the constitutional commitment to universal adult suffrage under Article 326 and equality under Article 14.

3. Constitutional Freeze on Seat Readjustment

The Constitution originally contemplated periodic readjustment of representation following each census. However, concerns arose that States which successfully controlled population growth could lose relative representation, while States with higher population growth could gain seats. This led to constitutional amendments that froze aspects of seat allocation.

42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

The 42nd Amendment froze the allocation of Lok Sabha seats among States and the total number of seats in State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of the 1971 Census, with the freeze originally intended to continue until after the 2000 Census. The measure was linked to the national population-control policy and sought to avoid penalising States for successful family-planning efforts.

84th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001

The 84th Amendment extended the freeze on inter-State allocation of Lok Sabha seats and the total number of seats in State Legislative Assemblies until the first census taken after 2026. It nevertheless permitted readjustment of territorial constituencies within States, subject to the constitutional framework and the 1991 Census basis then specified.

87th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

The 87th Amendment substituted the 2001 Census for the 1991 Census as the basis for readjustment of constituencies within States, without altering the freeze on the total allocation of seats among States.

Important distinction: The freeze on the allocation of seats among States is not the same as a complete ban on redrawing constituency boundaries within States. The 2001–2008 delimitation exercise readjusted constituency boundaries and reserved seats using the 2001 Census, while the inter-State allocation freeze continued.

4. Delimitation Commission: Legal Basis and Composition

Parliament has enacted specific Delimitation Commission laws to carry out delimitation exercises. A Delimitation Commission is constituted under the relevant Act and is intended to function as an independent statutory body for the exercise.

The composition under the Delimitation Commission framework has included:

  • A serving or retired Judge of the Supreme Court, nominated by the President, as Chairperson;
  • The Chief Election Commissioner or an Election Commissioner nominated by the CEC; and
  • The State Election Commissioners of the concerned States as ex officio members.

Associate members representing the concerned legislatures may be associated with the Commission under the governing law. They participate in the process but do not have the same final decision-making status as the Commission members.

The exact statutory composition and procedure must be read with the particular Delimitation Commission Act applicable to the exercise.

5. Functions and Principles of Delimitation

  • Determine the number and territorial extent of constituencies in accordance with the applicable constitutional and statutory framework.
  • Consider population distribution and seek, as far as practicable, equality of representation.
  • Take account of geography, administrative boundaries, communication, public convenience and other relevant factors.
  • Identify constituencies to be reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes under constitutional and statutory requirements.
  • Invite public suggestions and objections through the prescribed process.
  • Publish proposals and conduct public sittings where required.
  • Finalise orders and publish them in the manner prescribed by law.

Population equality is an important objective, but delimitation must also account for territorial integrity, administrative convenience, geographical realities and constitutional safeguards.

6. Process of Delimitation

  1. Constitution of Commission: The Commission is established under the relevant Delimitation Act.
  2. Use of census data: The Commission works with the census reference specified by the Constitution and the applicable Act.
  3. Preparation of proposals: Draft constituency boundaries and reservation arrangements are prepared.
  4. Publication: Proposals are made public through the prescribed process.
  5. Public consultation: Suggestions and objections are invited, and public sittings may be conducted.
  6. Consideration of objections: The Commission considers representations and relevant statutory criteria.
  7. Final orders: The Commission publishes its final delimitation orders as provided by law.
  8. Implementation: The orders take effect according to the applicable statute and are used for subsequent elections.

7. Legal Status and Judicial Review

Article 329(a) restricts judicial challenges to laws relating to delimitation and allotment of seats. Delimitation Commission laws have also provided that the Commission's orders have the force of law and are not to be questioned in courts in the manner specified by the statute.

The purpose is to provide finality and prevent prolonged litigation over constituency boundaries from disrupting the electoral process. However, the constitutional restriction should not be treated as a general licence for every conceivable action to escape constitutional scrutiny. The precise scope of review depends on the nature of the challenge, the constitutional provision and the applicable law.

In Meghraj Kothari v. Delimitation Commission (1967), the Supreme Court considered the finality attached to delimitation orders and the constitutional bar under Article 329(a).

8. Reservation and Delimitation

Delimitation is closely linked to the reservation of legislative seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.

  • Article 330 provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha.
  • Article 332 provides for reservation in State Legislative Assemblies.
  • The number of reserved seats is determined with reference to the constitutional and statutory criteria, including population proportions.
  • The territorial location of reserved constituencies is determined through the delimitation process.

The 106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023 inserted provisions for reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, including Articles 330A and 332A, and provided for commencement after the constitutionally specified census and subsequent delimitation process under Article 334A. The operation of women's reservation is therefore linked to the future delimitation exercise contemplated by the amendment.

9. Delimitation Exercises in India

India has undertaken delimitation exercises at different stages of its constitutional development. The broad historical pattern includes:

  • Early delimitation exercises following the 1951, 1961 and 1971 Censuses;
  • The constitutional freeze introduced by the 42nd Amendment in 1976;
  • The extension of the inter-State seat allocation freeze through the 84th Amendment in 2001;
  • The 2002–2008 delimitation exercise, based on the 2001 Census for constituency readjustment within States, under the applicable constitutional and statutory framework; and
  • Subsequent exercises for specific jurisdictions where Parliament enacted separate arrangements.

The delimitation exercise for Jammu and Kashmir following the reorganisation of the former State was conducted under the applicable statutory framework and had a distinct legal context. It should not be conflated with the general future exercise linked to the first census after 2026.

10. The Post-2026 Delimitation Debate

The constitutional freeze on inter-State allocation of Lok Sabha seats and the total number of seats in State Legislative Assemblies is linked to the first census taken after 2026. Any future exercise will raise important questions about representation, federal balance and population policy.

Key issues

  • Population imbalance: States have experienced different rates of population growth and demographic transition.
  • Federal representation: A purely population-based redistribution may alter the relative influence of States in the Lok Sabha.
  • Demographic performance: States that achieved lower fertility rates may perceive a reduction in their relative political weight as unfair.
  • Equality of vote: Large differences in constituency population can weaken the principle of equal representation.
  • Urbanisation: Rapidly growing cities may be inadequately represented if constituency boundaries do not reflect changing settlement patterns.
  • Women's reservation: The implementation framework under Article 334A links reservation to the relevant census and delimitation process.
  • Administrative capacity: Boundary changes can affect voter registration, polling logistics and public understanding of constituencies.

11. Arguments in Favour of Delimitation

  • Promotes equality in the value of votes by reducing population disparities between constituencies.
  • Updates representation to reflect migration, urbanisation and changing population patterns.
  • Improves the representativeness of legislatures.
  • Allows the constitutional framework for reserved constituencies to reflect demographic distribution.
  • Can strengthen accountability by aligning constituencies more closely with actual communities and administrative realities.

12. Concerns and Criticisms

  • Population-based redistribution can create perceived disadvantages for States that have successfully implemented population stabilisation policies.
  • Constituency boundaries may be vulnerable to political controversy if the process lacks transparency or public confidence.
  • Frequent changes can disrupt voter familiarity and local political accountability.
  • Geographical, linguistic and cultural communities may be divided between constituencies.
  • Urban constituencies may face rapid population growth between delimitation exercises.
  • The relationship between population equality and federal equality remains constitutionally and politically contested.

13. Reform Options and the Way Forward

  • Use transparent, publicly explained criteria for constituency boundaries and reservation allocation.
  • Ensure credible and meaningful public consultation, including accessible draft maps and data.
  • Balance population equality with geography, administrative convenience and community continuity.
  • Develop a constitutionally legitimate approach to inter-State representation that recognises both democratic equality and India's federal structure.
  • Consider institutional mechanisms for consultation with States before major changes in the distribution of representation.
  • Improve census quality, timeliness and public access to relevant demographic data.
  • Ensure that delimitation and the implementation of women's reservation proceed within the constitutional framework and with clear public communication.
  • Strengthen safeguards against partisan boundary manipulation and ensure reasoned decision-making.

14. Important Constitutional Distinctions

  • Article 82: Readjustment of Lok Sabha representation following a census, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Article 170: Composition and readjustment of State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Article 329(a): Bar on court challenges to laws relating to delimitation and allotment of seats.
  • Article 330: Reservation for SCs and STs in the Lok Sabha.
  • Article 332: Reservation for SCs and STs in State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Articles 330A and 332A: Women's reservation provisions inserted by the 106th Amendment.
  • Articles 243K and 243ZA: Local-body elections conducted by State Election Commissions, not the ECI's ordinary parliamentary and assembly election machinery.

15. Integrated Constitutional Understanding

Delimitation illustrates the tension between political equality and federal balance. Equal representation requires constituencies to reflect population realities, while India's federal structure raises questions about how population changes should affect the relative representation of States.

A legitimate delimitation exercise must therefore combine constitutional fidelity, transparent procedures, reliable demographic data, public consultation and sensitivity to India's social and federal diversity.
