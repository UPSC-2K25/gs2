Electoral Malpractices

1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Electoral malpractices are unlawful, unethical or improper activities that distort the electoral process, influence voters through illegitimate means or undermine the fairness of political competition.

They can occur at different stages: voter registration, nomination, campaigning, polling, counting and the declaration of results. Their effects extend beyond individual elections because they weaken public trust, political equality, representative legitimacy and the rule of law.

Free and fair elections are an essential feature of constitutional democracy. Electoral malpractices therefore implicate constitutional values such as equality, liberty, fraternity, freedom of expression and the accountability of government.

2. Constitutional and Statutory Framework

  • Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of the laws.
  • Article 19(1)(a): Freedom of speech and expression, including political communication.
  • Article 19(1)(c): Freedom of association, including political association.
  • Article 324: Superintendence, direction and control of elections vested in the ECI for the elections within its jurisdiction.
  • Article 325: Prohibits exclusion from a general electoral roll solely on grounds of religion, race, caste or sex.
  • Article 326: Adult suffrage for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Article 329: Provides constitutional restrictions on judicial interference in electoral matters.
  • Representation of the People Act, 1951: Defines corrupt practices, electoral offences, disqualifications and election-petition remedies.
  • Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961: Prescribes procedures for polling, counting and related election administration.
  • Applicable criminal laws: Penalise conduct such as violence, intimidation, forgery, unlawful assembly and other offences where the statutory ingredients are established.

3. Corrupt Practices under the RPA 1951

Section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, 1951 identifies specified conduct as corrupt practices for the purposes of election petitions. These are not merely political improprieties; they have defined legal consequences under the Act.

Bribery — Section 123(1)

Bribery includes giving or offering gratification to induce a person to stand or not stand as a candidate, withdraw a candidature, vote or refrain from voting, or reward a person for such conduct. Gratification is not confined to cash and may include benefits falling within the statutory definition.

Bribery undermines the free exercise of the franchise by replacing political choice with an inducement.

Undue influence — Section 123(2)

Undue influence involves direct or indirect interference, or attempted interference, with the free exercise of an electoral right. The provision includes specified forms of intimidation and threats, including threats of injury and certain forms of social or spiritual pressure.

Legitimate persuasion and political advocacy must be distinguished from coercion that interferes with a voter's free choice.

Appeals on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language — Section 123(3)

The provision treats specified appeals for votes on prohibited identity grounds as corrupt practices. Its purpose is to protect secular democratic competition and prevent the electoral process from being distorted by appeals that the statute prohibits.

In Abhiram Singh v. C.D. Commachen (2017), the Supreme Court interpreted Section 123(3) broadly in relation to appeals based on the religion, race, caste, community or language of the candidate, agent or elector.

Promotion of enmity — Section 123(3A)

The promotion or attempted promotion of feelings of enmity or hatred between different classes of citizens on grounds specified in the provision, by a candidate or agent or another person with the consent of the candidate or election agent, may constitute a corrupt practice.

Publication of false statements — Section 123(4)

The publication by a candidate or agent, or with the required consent, of a false statement of fact concerning the personal character or conduct of a candidate, where it is reasonably calculated to prejudice that candidate's electoral prospects, may constitute a corrupt practice. The provision is not a general prohibition on criticism of political policies or public performance.

Other corrupt practices

  • Section 123(5): Hiring or procuring vehicles or other conveyances for the free conveyance of electors, subject to the statutory provision and exceptions.
  • Section 123(6): Incurring or authorising expenditure in contravention of Section 77.
  • Section 123(7): Obtaining or procuring assistance from specified government servants for furtherance of a candidate's electoral prospects.
  • Section 123(8): Carrying on or procuring the carrying on of certain prohibited election activities, as specified by the statutory provision.

The precise elements of each corrupt practice, including attribution, consent, intent and statutory exceptions, must be established in accordance with the RPA 1951 and judicial interpretation.

4. Electoral Offences under the RPA 1951

The Act separately identifies electoral offences. A corrupt practice and an electoral offence are not interchangeable concepts: they have different statutory elements, procedures and consequences.

  • Section 125: Promoting enmity between classes in connection with an election.
  • Section 126: Prohibits specified forms of public election campaigning during the period of 48 hours ending with the hour fixed for conclusion of polling in the relevant polling area.
  • Section 126A: Restricts the conduct and dissemination of exit polls during the period specified by law.
  • Section 127: Regulates disturbance at election meetings.
  • Section 127A: Regulates printing and publication of election pamphlets, posters and similar materials, including printer and publisher particulars.
  • Section 128: Protects the secrecy of voting.
  • Section 129: Restricts government servants from acting as election agents, polling agents or counting agents.
  • Section 130: Prohibits specified canvassing and related activities near polling stations.
  • Section 131: Addresses disorderly conduct in or near polling stations.
  • Section 132: Addresses misconduct at polling stations.
  • Section 134: Addresses breaches of official duty in connection with elections.

Other provisions of the Act and applicable criminal laws may apply depending on the conduct involved. The statutory text should be consulted for the exact ingredients, exceptions and penalties.

5. Bribery and Voter Inducement

Voter inducement involves offering money, gifts, goods, services or other benefits to influence electoral choice. It may take place through direct payments, distribution of consumer goods, transport arrangements or promises of personal benefits.

Why it is harmful

  • It undermines the free and informed exercise of the vote.
  • It creates unequal political competition by rewarding candidates with greater financial resources.
  • It encourages patronage and transactional politics.
  • It can weaken accountability by shifting voter attention away from public policy and performance.

Election authorities use expenditure observers, flying squads, static surveillance teams and other monitoring arrangements to detect suspected inducements. Seizure or intervention must follow applicable law and due process; legitimate possession or movement of money and goods should not automatically be treated as proof of an electoral offence.

6. Intimidation, Coercion and Voter Suppression

Intimidation includes threats, violence, coercive pressure or other unlawful conduct designed to influence whether or how a person votes. Voter suppression may also arise through deliberate obstruction, misinformation about polling arrangements or discriminatory exclusion from the electoral process.

Forms and consequences

  • Threats against voters or their families;
  • Violence or coercion near polling stations;
  • Preventing eligible voters from reaching polling locations;
  • Impersonation or fraudulent voting;
  • Social or economic pressure intended to control voting choices;
  • Targeted intimidation of women, minorities or vulnerable communities.

These practices violate the principle that every elector must be able to exercise the franchise freely and secretly. Effective policing, accessible complaint mechanisms, secure polling arrangements and protection of vulnerable voters are essential safeguards.

7. Booth Capturing and Polling Irregularities

Booth capturing refers to unlawful seizure or control of a polling station or polling process to influence the recording of votes. It may involve intimidation, obstruction of polling personnel, forced voting or destruction of election materials.

The RPA 1951 contains provisions addressing booth capturing and related election misconduct. The ECI also has powers under the election framework to respond to serious polling irregularities, including ordering appropriate corrective action or fresh polling where the legal requirements are met.

Polling irregularities can also include impersonation, unauthorised campaigning near polling stations, breaches of ballot secrecy, disorderly conduct and interference with polling personnel.

8. Misuse of Official Machinery

Misuse of official machinery occurs when the ruling party or candidates use government resources, public office or administrative authority to gain an electoral advantage.

  • Use of government vehicles, buildings, staff or funds for partisan campaigning;
  • Use of official publicity or public events to promote a party during the MCC period;
  • Pressure on public servants to support a candidate or party;
  • Selective distribution of public benefits for electoral advantage;
  • Transfer or posting decisions intended to influence the electoral process;
  • Misuse of official information or government communication channels.

The MCC restricts such conduct during the election period. Specific actions may also violate statutory provisions, service rules, criminal law or constitutional principles, depending on the facts.

9. Hate Speech and Communal Polarisation

Hate speech and divisive campaign rhetoric can undermine electoral equality, public order and constitutional fraternity. Election law prohibits specified appeals and conduct, while general criminal law may apply to other forms of incitement or promotion of enmity.

The distinction between robust political criticism and unlawful speech is important. Criticism of government policy, political ideology or public conduct is a legitimate part of democratic debate. The legal assessment of prohibited speech depends on statutory language, context, intent where relevant and judicial interpretation.

In Abhiram Singh (2017), the Supreme Court interpreted Section 123(3) of the RPA 1951 in relation to appeals based on prohibited identity grounds. The decision is significant for the constitutional values of secularism and equal citizenship.

10. Paid News and Disinformation

Paid news occurs when political publicity is presented as independent news coverage without appropriate disclosure of its paid or sponsored character. It can mislead voters and create an unfair advantage for candidates or parties with greater resources.

Disinformation involves the deliberate circulation of false or misleading information. In election periods, it may concern candidate statements, voting dates, eligibility, polling locations, counting procedures or fabricated incidents.

Digital forms

  • Manipulated photographs, videos and audio;
  • Deepfakes and synthetic media;
  • Fake accounts and coordinated amplification;
  • Impersonation of candidates, officials or media organisations;
  • False information about electoral rolls or polling arrangements;
  • Undisclosed paid political advertising and influencer campaigns.

Countermeasures should include source transparency, rapid verification, disclosure of paid content, accessible correction mechanisms and digital literacy. Measures must remain consistent with constitutional protections for political speech and due process.

11. Electoral Expenditure Violations

Expenditure-related malpractice may include concealed spending, false accounting, expenditure beyond prescribed limits where the statutory conditions apply, and the use of intermediaries to disguise campaign costs.

Section 77 of the RPA 1951 concerns the account of election expenses of candidates, while Section 78 provides for lodging the account. Section 10A provides for disqualification in specified circumstances where a candidate fails to lodge the account without good reason or justification.

Section 123(6) treats incurring or authorising expenditure in contravention of Section 77 as a corrupt practice. Attribution and statutory requirements are important; not every expenditure by a supporter automatically becomes a candidate's expenditure for every legal purpose.

12. Electoral Roll Manipulation and Impersonation

Electoral roll manipulation may involve wrongful inclusion, exclusion, duplication or alteration of entries. Impersonation occurs when a person votes or attempts to vote in the name of another elector.

Accurate electoral rolls are necessary for universal adult suffrage. At the same time, administrative safeguards must prevent wrongful deletion or exclusion of eligible voters.

  • Transparent revision and correction procedures;
  • Accessible claims and objections;
  • Verification based on lawful and proportionate requirements;
  • Protection against discriminatory exclusion;
  • Prompt investigation of impersonation and fraudulent registration;
  • Public communication about enrolment deadlines and polling details.

13. Election Disputes and Legal Remedies

Article 329(b) provides that an election to Parliament or a State Legislature may be called in question only by an election petition presented in the manner provided by law. The RPA 1951 establishes the principal statutory framework for such petitions.

Election petition

  • Section 80: Provides that no election shall be called in question except by an election petition presented in accordance with the Act.
  • Section 80A: Provides for the High Court to try election petitions.
  • Section 81: Governs presentation of election petitions and the statutory limitation period.
  • Section 83: Specifies the contents of an election petition.
  • Section 86: Provides for dismissal of an election petition for non-compliance with specified statutory requirements.
  • Section 98: Provides for the High Court's decision on an election petition.
  • Section 100: Specifies grounds on which an election may be declared void.
  • Section 116A: Provides for appeals to the Supreme Court in the circumstances specified by the Act.

Possible outcomes

The High Court may dismiss the petition or grant relief authorised by the statute, including declaring an election void or declaring another candidate duly elected where the statutory requirements are satisfied.

Election petitions require compliance with strict procedural and evidentiary requirements. Allegations of malpractice must be pleaded and proved in accordance with the applicable law.

14. Role of the Election Commission

The ECI supervises elections under Article 324 and uses administrative, monitoring and enforcement mechanisms to protect electoral integrity.

  • Issue instructions and enforce the MCC within its authority.
  • Deploy general, police and expenditure observers.
  • Monitor campaign expenditure and suspected inducements.
  • Coordinate with law-enforcement agencies and election officials.
  • Receive and process complaints through prescribed channels.
  • Take action under the relevant election laws and constitutional powers.
  • Order appropriate corrective measures, including fresh polling where the legal framework permits.
  • Promote voter awareness and provide accessible election information.

The ECI's administrative role must be distinguished from the adjudicatory role of courts in election petitions and criminal proceedings.

15. Role of Citizens, Media and Civil Society

  • Citizens: Report inducements, intimidation and irregularities through lawful channels and verify information before sharing it.
  • Media: Maintain accuracy, disclose paid content and distinguish reporting from political advertising.
  • Civil society: Promote voter awareness, observe electoral processes and document barriers to participation.
  • Political parties: Train campaign workers, enforce internal standards and cooperate with election authorities.
  • Digital platforms: Improve transparency of political advertising and respond to unlawful manipulation in accordance with applicable law.

Citizen vigilance complements, but cannot replace, impartial public institutions and effective legal enforcement.

16. Challenges in Controlling Electoral Malpractices

  • Large geographical scale and complex election logistics;
  • Difficulty of detecting cash, gifts and indirect inducements;
  • Delayed investigation and prosecution;
  • Political pressure and uneven enforcement perceptions;
  • Difficulty attributing digital content and campaign expenditure;
  • Fear of retaliation among voters and witnesses;
  • Weak awareness of legal rights and complaint procedures;
  • Procedural complexity in election petitions;
  • Potential misuse of allegations to suppress legitimate political activity.

17. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Strengthen the independence, capacity and transparency of election administration.
  • Ensure prompt, impartial investigation of serious electoral offences.
  • Improve the speed and accessibility of election dispute resolution while protecting due process.
  • Strengthen expenditure monitoring and disclosure of political finance.
  • Improve protection for voters, election officials and witnesses who report intimidation or fraud.
  • Use technology transparently to detect irregularities, with safeguards against arbitrary decisions.
  • Strengthen public awareness about bribery, impersonation, misinformation and voter rights.
  • Improve coordination between election authorities, police, prosecution agencies and courts.
  • Ensure that enforcement measures are evidence-based, proportionate and consistent with constitutional freedoms.
  • Promote internal party accountability and ethical candidate selection.

18. Important Legal Distinctions

  • Corrupt practice: Conduct defined under Section 123 of the RPA 1951, with consequences in election-petition proceedings.
  • Electoral offence: Conduct made punishable under election legislation.
  • Criminal offence: Conduct punishable under general criminal law or another applicable statute.
  • MCC violation: Breach of election-period guidelines; it may also overlap with statutory offences or corrupt practices.
  • Election petition: Statutory proceeding challenging the validity of an election.
  • Criminal prosecution: Proceedings to establish criminal liability under the applicable law.
  • Fresh poll: Corrective electoral measure available under the relevant statutory framework in specified circumstances.

19. Integrated Constitutional Understanding

Electoral malpractices threaten the foundation of representative democracy because they distort the relationship between voter choice and political power. The constitutional response combines preventive regulation, independent election administration, criminal sanctions, election petitions and public accountability.

Effective electoral integrity requires both enforcement and democratic culture. Laws and institutions must deter coercion, bribery and manipulation, while citizens, political parties and the media must uphold the values of informed choice, equality and peaceful political competition.
