Election Campaigns and Model Code of Conduct

1. Meaning and Democratic Significance of Election Campaigns

Election campaigns are organised activities through which candidates and political parties communicate their programmes, mobilise supporters, criticise opponents and seek electoral support. Campaigns enable voters to compare alternatives and make informed political choices.

Campaign freedoms are protected by constitutional guarantees, particularly freedom of speech and expression and freedom of association. These freedoms operate within the framework of election law, public order, equality and the requirement of free and fair elections.

A democratic campaign should allow robust political criticism while preventing intimidation, bribery, communal hatred, misuse of public resources and deliberate disruption of electoral choice.

2. Constitutional and Statutory Framework

  • Article 19(1)(a): Freedom of speech and expression protects political communication, subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(2).
  • Article 19(1)(c): Freedom of association supports political parties and campaign organisations, subject to Article 19(4).
  • Article 324: Empowers the ECI to supervise and control elections within its constitutional jurisdiction.
  • Article 326: Establishes adult suffrage for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Representation of the People Act, 1951: Regulates corrupt practices, electoral offences, candidate expenditure and election disputes.
  • Conduct of Elections Rules, 1961: Prescribes procedures relevant to election administration and campaign-related compliance.
  • Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968: Regulates party recognition and election symbols.
  • Model Code of Conduct: A set of standards issued by the ECI to guide political parties, candidates and governments during elections.

3. Model Code of Conduct: Meaning and Purpose

The Model Code of Conduct (MCC) is a set of guidelines governing the conduct of political parties, candidates and, in relevant respects, governments during an election period. It seeks to maintain a level playing field, prevent misuse of official machinery and promote peaceful, fair campaigning.

The MCC is not a comprehensive statute enacted by Parliament. It is primarily a consensual code developed through political practice and enforced by the ECI through its constitutional authority under Article 324, along with applicable statutory provisions.

The MCC generally comes into operation when the ECI announces the election schedule and remains applicable until the election process is completed in the relevant context.

4. Main Components of the MCC

General conduct

  • Parties and candidates should avoid activities that aggravate existing differences or create mutual hatred between communities.
  • Appeals to caste or communal feelings for securing votes are prohibited under the relevant election-law framework and MCC guidelines.
  • Criticism should ordinarily relate to policies, programmes, past record and public conduct rather than unverified personal allegations.
  • Places of worship should not be used as platforms for election propaganda.
  • Bribery, intimidation, impersonation and other unlawful inducements are prohibited.

Meetings and processions

  • Parties should inform the local police and administration of public meetings in accordance with applicable requirements.
  • Necessary permissions should be obtained for the use of public spaces, loudspeakers and processions.
  • Campaign activities should not obstruct other parties' meetings or processions.
  • Parties should avoid carrying or displaying articles that may create tension or provoke violence.

Polling day

  • Parties and candidates must comply with restrictions around polling stations.
  • Unauthorised canvassing near polling stations is prohibited under election law.
  • Election agents and authorised representatives must follow prescribed identification and conduct rules.
  • Voters must be able to cast their votes freely and without intimidation.

Polling booths and voter assistance

Political parties may establish permitted election booths subject to statutory and ECI instructions. Such facilities must not become instruments for inducement, intimidation or unlawful influence.

Observers and complaints

Parties and candidates are expected to cooperate with election observers and the election administration. Complaints should be submitted through the prescribed channels with relevant information and supporting evidence.

5. Restrictions on the Party in Power

The MCC places particular emphasis on preventing the ruling party from using public office or government machinery to secure an electoral advantage.

  • Ministers should not combine official visits with electioneering.
  • Official machinery, government vehicles and public resources should not be used for party campaigns.
  • Public funds should not be used for advertisements that unduly project the ruling party's electoral achievements.
  • New financial grants, schemes, concessions or promises that may influence voters are restricted within the applicable MCC framework.
  • Appointments and discretionary actions that may influence voters or distort the electoral process are subject to restrictions.
  • Government transport, personnel and facilities should not be deployed for partisan campaign purposes.

The objective is not to stop routine governance. Essential administration and ongoing public services continue, subject to the MCC and ECI directions.

6. Legal Status and Enforcement of the MCC

The MCC does not operate as a single statute carrying an independent penal provision for every breach. Its enforcement draws on Article 324, election-law provisions, criminal law where applicable and the ECI's administrative directions.

Conduct that violates the MCC may also amount to a statutory offence or corrupt practice. For example, bribery, undue influence, appeals on prohibited grounds and certain forms of campaign misconduct may attract consequences under the Representation of the People Act, 1951 or other applicable law.

The ECI may issue notices, seek explanations, direct corrective action, censure or warn parties and candidates, and take other measures within its constitutional and statutory powers. Criminal prosecution or disqualification, however, must rest on the relevant legal provision and prescribed procedure.

In Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978), the Supreme Court recognised the broad scope of Article 324 in areas not occupied by legislation, while confirming that the Commission's powers must operate consistently with law.

7. Election Campaign Expenditure

Campaign expenditure includes spending on rallies, travel, publicity, advertising, campaign materials, workers and other election-related activities. Regulation seeks to prevent money power from overwhelming political equality.

Candidate expenditure

  • The RPA 1951 and Conduct of Elections Rules regulate candidate election expenses.
  • Candidates are required to maintain accounts in the prescribed manner.
  • They must lodge their election expense statements within the statutory period after the result is declared.
  • Failure to lodge accounts without good reason or justification may lead to disqualification under Section 10A of the RPA 1951.
  • Expenditure limits are prescribed by the ECI within the statutory framework and may vary according to the relevant election and constituency category.

Party and third-party expenditure

Political party expenditure and spending by supporters or other entities raise distinct accounting and attribution questions. Monitoring indirect expenditure is difficult, especially where multiple organisations, digital advertisements or informal campaign networks are involved.

8. Campaigning Through Media

Modern campaigns rely on print, television, radio, online platforms, social media and messaging services. Media access can improve political communication, but unequal resources and misinformation can distort electoral competition.

  • Paid news: Content presented as independent news but paid for or arranged as political publicity without appropriate disclosure.
  • Political advertising: Advertisements intended to influence voting behaviour, subject to applicable certification and disclosure requirements.
  • Social media: Campaigns through platforms, influencers, targeted advertisements and digital networks.
  • Broadcasting access: ECI arrangements provide specified opportunities for recognised political parties to use public broadcasting facilities during elections.
  • Digital transparency: Disclosure of political advertisers, spending and sponsored content is important for public scrutiny.

The ECI has issued instructions concerning political advertisements in electronic media, including pre-certification requirements for specified categories. The precise scope should be read with the current ECI directions.

9. Social Media, Deepfakes and Misinformation

Digital campaigning has expanded political participation but also creates risks through false information, synthetic media, coordinated manipulation, hate speech, impersonation and targeted harassment.

Major concerns

  • Deepfake audio and video may falsely attribute statements or conduct to candidates.
  • Misleading claims can spread rapidly before verification or correction.
  • Micro-targeted advertising can create unequal information environments for different groups of voters.
  • Anonymous or coordinated accounts may conceal the source of campaign messages.
  • Online intimidation can discourage women and marginalised groups from political participation.
  • Content moderation decisions may affect political speech and therefore require transparency and procedural fairness.

Possible responses

  • Clear disclosure of paid political advertisements and their sponsors.
  • Rapid reporting and correction mechanisms for demonstrably false election-related content.
  • Labelling or disclosure of synthetic media in accordance with applicable law and directions.
  • Preservation of relevant digital evidence for lawful investigation.
  • Media literacy and public awareness campaigns.
  • Proportionate platform accountability with safeguards for lawful political expression.

10. Campaigning and Constitutional Values

Campaign regulation involves balancing political expression with the integrity of elections.

  • Free speech: Candidates must be able to criticise governments and public policy.
  • Equality: Public resources should not be used to create an unfair advantage for the party in power.
  • Secularism: Electoral mobilisation must comply with constitutional and statutory restrictions on appeals based on religion and other prohibited grounds.
  • Fraternity: Campaigns should not incite hatred or undermine the dignity and security of communities.
  • Political accountability: Voters should have access to truthful information about candidates, parties and campaign finance.
  • Privacy: Voter data and personal information should not be misused for unlawful or manipulative targeting.

11. Important Judicial Decisions

  • Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978): Recognised the breadth of Article 324 in the absence of legislation occupying the field.
  • Kanwar Lal Gupta v. Amar Nath Chawla (1975): Considered election expenditure and the relationship between candidate and political party spending under the law as it then stood.
  • Common Cause v. Union of India (1996): Addressed disclosure and maintenance of political expenditure accounts.
  • Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002): Recognised the voter's right to information about candidates.
  • People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2003): Reaffirmed candidate disclosure requirements and the connection with informed voting.
  • Abhiram Singh v. C.D. Commachen (2017): Interpreted Section 123(3) of the RPA 1951 concerning appeals for votes on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language.
  • Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2018): Issued directions on disclosure and publicity of criminal antecedents of candidates.

12. Challenges in MCC Implementation

  • Non-statutory character: The MCC is not a comprehensive statutory code, and enforcement relies on multiple legal and administrative sources.
  • Interpretation: Political speech and campaign conduct can involve difficult questions of context and intent.
  • Uneven enforcement perceptions: Delayed or inconsistent action can weaken trust in the ECI.
  • Digital scale: Large volumes of online content are difficult to monitor in real time.
  • Indirect campaigning: Expenditure and messaging by third parties may be difficult to attribute.
  • Government communication: Distinguishing routine governance from campaign-oriented publicity can be challenging.
  • Short campaign cycles: Complaints may arise and spread faster than administrative and legal remedies can respond.

13. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Clarify the legal consequences of serious MCC violations while preserving the flexibility needed for election administration.
  • Publish reasoned decisions and improve consistency in enforcement.
  • Strengthen expenditure monitoring, including digital advertisements and third-party campaign spending.
  • Ensure transparent and timely procedures for complaints against candidates, parties and government officials.
  • Develop clear protocols for deepfakes, manipulated media and online political advertising.
  • Improve coordination between the ECI, law-enforcement agencies and digital platforms.
  • Protect legitimate political criticism and avoid overbroad restrictions on speech.
  • Strengthen voter awareness about misinformation, paid news and campaign inducements.
  • Ensure equal access to public campaign facilities under transparent rules.

14. Important Distinctions

  • MCC: Election-period guidelines governing parties, candidates and governments.
  • Statutory election offence: Conduct expressly prohibited and punishable under election law or another applicable statute.
  • Corrupt practice: Conduct defined under the RPA 1951 that may provide grounds for an election petition and statutory consequences.
  • Candidate expenditure: Expenditure regulated under candidate-specific statutory rules and limits.
  • Party expenditure: Spending by political parties, subject to separate reporting and regulatory requirements.
  • Paid news: Political publicity presented as independent news without appropriate disclosure.
  • Campaign speech: Political expression protected by constitutional freedoms, subject to lawful restrictions.

15. Integrated Constitutional Understanding

Election campaigns are essential to political choice, but their legitimacy depends on fair competition and informed participation. The MCC seeks to protect the electoral environment by regulating campaign conduct and limiting misuse of public office.

Effective regulation requires a careful balance: strong action against bribery, intimidation, hate and misuse of public resources, alongside protection for legitimate criticism, political association and freedom of expression.
