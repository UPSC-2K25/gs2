National Commission for Scheduled Tribes

1. Constitutional Position and Purpose

The National Commission for Scheduled Tribes (NCST) is a constitutional body established under Article 338A. It safeguards the rights and interests of Scheduled Tribes (STs), monitors constitutional and legal protections, and advises on measures for their socio-economic development.

Its mandate must be understood in the context of India's tribal diversity. Scheduled Tribes include communities with varied languages, cultures, livelihoods, settlement patterns and relationships with land and forests. Many face challenges involving displacement, access to public services, exploitation, loss of customary resources and limited political or administrative participation.

  • Constitutional provision: Article 338A.
  • Constitutional status: Independent constitutional commission.
  • Primary constituency: Scheduled Tribes as specified under Article 342.
  • Core functions: Safeguards, complaints, development evaluation, advice and reporting.

2. Historical Evolution

  • Original Constitution: Article 338 provided for a Special Officer for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • 65th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1990: Replaced the single Special Officer arrangement with a multi-member National Commission for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • 89th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003: Inserted Article 338A and separated the earlier combined commission into the National Commission for Scheduled Castes under Article 338 and the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes under Article 338A.
  • Operationalisation: The separate NCSC and NCST became operational in 2004.

The separate institutional arrangement acknowledged the distinct constitutional, geographical, cultural and developmental concerns of tribal communities.

3. Composition and Appointment

Article 338A provides for a Commission consisting of a Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson and three other Members.

  • The Chairperson, Vice-Chairperson and other Members are appointed by the President by warrant under his hand and seal.
  • Their conditions of service and tenure are determined by rules made by the President.
  • The Commission's composition is intended to provide institutional leadership and expertise relevant to tribal rights, administration and development.

The Constitution does not itself prescribe a fixed tenure or a detailed selection committee mechanism. These matters are governed by the applicable rules and appointment framework in force.

4. Functions under Article 338A(5)

  1. Investigate and monitor all matters relating to safeguards provided for Scheduled Tribes under the Constitution, laws or government orders.
  2. Inquire into specific complaints concerning deprivation of rights and safeguards.
  3. Participate in and advise on the planning process for socio-economic development of Scheduled Tribes and evaluate the progress of their development.
  4. Present reports to the President annually and at other times considered appropriate.
  5. Make recommendations concerning measures that should be taken by the Union or a State for effective implementation of safeguards and protection.
  6. Discharge other functions relating to the protection, welfare, development and advancement of Scheduled Tribes as specified by the President, subject to law.

The NCST therefore has both an individual grievance function and a broader mandate to assess whether institutions and development policies are meeting the needs of tribal communities.

5. Investigative Powers under Article 338A(8)

While investigating a matter or inquiring into a complaint under Article 338A(5)(a) or (b), the Commission has the powers of a civil court trying a suit in respect of specified matters, including:

  • Summoning and enforcing the attendance of any person and examining them on oath.
  • Requiring the discovery and production of documents.
  • Receiving evidence on affidavits.
  • Requisitioning any public record or copy from any court or office.
  • Issuing commissions for the examination of witnesses and documents.
  • Any other matter prescribed by the President through rules.

These powers facilitate fact-finding and inquiry. They do not make the NCST a regular court or give all its recommendations the status of binding judicial orders.

6. Reports and Governmental Action

Under Article 338A(5)(d), the Commission submits reports to the President. Under Article 338A(6), the President causes the reports to be laid before each House of Parliament along with a memorandum explaining the action taken or proposed on recommendations relating to the Union and the reasons for non-acceptance, if any.

Under Article 338A(7), where a report concerns a matter relating to a State Government, a copy is forwarded to the Governor. The Governor causes it to be laid before the State Legislature along with a memorandum explaining action taken or proposed and reasons for non-acceptance, if any.

This reporting mechanism creates a formal accountability channel through legislatures. Its practical impact depends on the quality of recommendations, the adequacy of governmental responses and subsequent implementation.

7. Consultation under Article 338A(9)

Article 338A(9) requires the Union and every State Government to consult the Commission on all major policy matters affecting Scheduled Tribes. This gives the NCST a constitutional role in policy deliberation and not merely in post-facto complaint investigation.

Consultation is particularly relevant to policies concerning:

  • Land acquisition, displacement and rehabilitation.
  • Forest rights, minor forest produce and community resource management.
  • Tribal education, health, nutrition and livelihood security.
  • Scheduled Areas, local self-government and customary institutions.
  • Protection from exploitation, trafficking, bonded labour and atrocities.
  • Development planning and the delivery of public services in tribal regions.

8. Constitutional Framework for Tribal Protection

The NCST's work is connected with several constitutional provisions:

  • Article 14: Equality before the law and equal protection of the laws.
  • Article 15(4) and 15(5): Enabling provisions for special measures in favour of socially and educationally backward classes, SCs and STs in specified contexts.
  • Article 16(4), 16(4A) and 16(4B): Enabling provisions concerning reservation in public employment.
  • Article 46: Promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly SCs and STs, and protection from social injustice and exploitation.
  • Article 244: Administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas under the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.
  • Article 275(1): Grants-in-aid from the Union to certain States for promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and raising the level of administration in Scheduled Areas, including grants for approved schemes.
  • Article 330: Reservation of seats for SCs and STs in the Lok Sabha.
  • Article 332: Reservation of seats for SCs and STs in State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Article 335: Claims of SCs and STs to services and posts, consistent with administrative efficiency.
  • Article 342: Specification of Scheduled Tribes by Presidential notification, with Parliament empowered to include or exclude groups by law.

9. Fifth and Sixth Schedule Connections

The Fifth and Sixth Schedules are central to understanding the distinctive constitutional arrangements for tribal areas.

Fifth Schedule

  • Applies to Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than the areas covered by the Sixth Schedule, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Provides for the role of the Governor in relation to Scheduled Areas and the submission of reports to the President.
  • Provides for Tribes Advisory Councils in relevant States to advise on the welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes.
  • Enables regulations concerning restrictions on transfer of tribal land and regulation of money-lending in Scheduled Areas, subject to constitutional requirements.

Sixth Schedule

  • Provides autonomous district and regional councils in specified tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.
  • Confers specified legislative, administrative, judicial and financial powers on autonomous councils.
  • Recognises the importance of local self-government and protection of customary practices within the constitutional framework.

The NCST monitors safeguards and may make recommendations, but it does not itself exercise the powers assigned to Governors, Tribes Advisory Councils or autonomous district councils.

10. Statutory Framework and Key Laws

  • Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006: Recognises individual and community forest rights and provides a framework for claims and their verification.
  • Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996: Extends Part IX of the Constitution to Scheduled Areas with specified modifications and safeguards for Gram Sabhas and local self-government.
  • Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989: Provides penal and procedural safeguards against specified offences and atrocities.
  • Land acquisition and rehabilitation laws: Relevant to displacement, compensation, consultation and rehabilitation of affected tribal communities.
  • Biological Diversity and environmental laws: Intersect with customary use, community resources, conservation and livelihood rights.

11. Identification of Scheduled Tribes

Article 342 governs the specification of Scheduled Tribes. The President may, after consultation with the Governor where the notification relates to a State, specify the tribes or tribal communities, or parts or groups within them, for constitutional purposes.

Once a Presidential notification is issued, Parliament may by law include or exclude a tribe or tribal community from the list. The list cannot be altered by executive action alone.

The NCST does not independently notify a community as a Scheduled Tribe or amend the list. Its institutional role must be distinguished from the constitutional process under Article 342.

12. Major Areas of Institutional Work

  • Land and resource rights: Monitoring alienation of tribal land, displacement, rehabilitation and access to community resources.
  • Forest rights: Reviewing implementation of individual and community rights under the Forest Rights Act, 2006.
  • Self-governance: Examining implementation of PESA, Gram Sabha powers and Fifth and Sixth Schedule institutions.
  • Education: Assessing school access, retention, language barriers, scholarships and higher education participation.
  • Health and nutrition: Examining access to primary care, maternal health, nutrition and culturally appropriate services.
  • Livelihoods: Monitoring access to forest produce, agriculture, skills, employment, markets and social security.
  • Protection from exploitation: Addressing trafficking, bonded labour, displacement-related vulnerability and violence.
  • Representation: Monitoring reservation safeguards in legislatures, public employment and educational institutions.

13. Relationship with Other Institutions

  • NCSC: The NCSC is established under Article 338 and focuses on Scheduled Castes; the NCST is established under Article 338A and focuses on Scheduled Tribes.
  • NCBC: The National Commission for Backward Classes is established under Article 338B and has a separate mandate concerning socially and educationally backward classes.
  • Tribes Advisory Councils: These are provided for under the Fifth Schedule and advise on welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes in relevant States.
  • Autonomous District Councils: Sixth Schedule institutions exercise specified powers in their respective areas; they are not subordinate offices of the NCST.
  • Ministry of Tribal Affairs: The Ministry is part of the executive and administers policies and programmes; the NCST is a constitutional oversight and advisory institution.
  • Courts: Courts adjudicate disputes and issue binding remedies; the NCST investigates, monitors, advises and reports within its mandate.

14. Challenges and Limitations

  • Implementation gap: Constitutional and statutory protections may not translate into effective outcomes where administrative capacity is weak.
  • Land alienation and displacement: Conflicts involving infrastructure, mining, conservation and development projects can affect customary and statutory rights.
  • Weak local institutions: Inadequate devolution and limited capacity can undermine Gram Sabhas and autonomous councils.
  • Access barriers: Remote geography, language differences, low awareness and fear of retaliation can restrict access to complaint mechanisms.
  • Fragmented administration: Responsibilities are distributed across Union ministries, State departments, district authorities and specialised bodies.
  • Data and evaluation: Incomplete disaggregated data can obscure differences between regions and communities.
  • Limited enforceability: Recommendations require governmental follow-up and are not generally equivalent to binding court orders.
  • Institutional capacity: Staffing, field presence, research expertise and timely inquiry affect the Commission's effectiveness.

15. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Strengthen field-level investigation, legal support, research and specialist capacity.
  2. Improve coordination with State governments, district administrations, Gram Sabhas and autonomous councils.
  3. Track implementation of recommendations through time-bound action-taken reporting and public compliance indicators.
  4. Improve monitoring of PESA and Forest Rights Act implementation, including community forest resource rights.
  5. Ensure meaningful consultation under Article 338A(9) before major policies affecting tribal communities are finalised.
  6. Develop reliable, disaggregated data on land, displacement, education, health, livelihoods and access to justice.
  7. Expand accessible complaint channels, local outreach and language-sensitive communication.
  8. Strengthen the capacity and accountability of local self-governing institutions in tribal areas.
  9. Promote development approaches that respect constitutional protections, customary institutions and community participation.

16. Important Distinctions

  • Article 338A vs Article 338: NCST and NCSC respectively; their constitutional mandates are distinct.
  • Article 338A vs Article 338B: NCST concerns Scheduled Tribes; NCBC concerns socially and educationally backward classes.
  • Article 342 vs Article 338A: Article 342 governs identification of Scheduled Tribes; Article 338A establishes the Commission.
  • Fifth vs Sixth Schedule: The Fifth Schedule provides a distinct administrative framework for Scheduled Areas; the Sixth Schedule creates autonomous district and regional councils in specified North-Eastern tribal areas.
  • NCST vs Ministry of Tribal Affairs: The NCST is a constitutional oversight and advisory body; the Ministry is an executive department responsible for policy and programme administration.

17. Integrated Understanding

The NCST demonstrates the constitutional recognition that formal equality alone may not address the distinct vulnerabilities of tribal communities. Effective protection requires a combination of rights, self-governance, land and resource security, culturally appropriate public services and meaningful participation in development decisions.

For GS-II analysis, connect Article 338A with Articles 244, 275(1), 342 and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules; PESA; the Forest Rights Act; local self-government; displacement and rehabilitation; and the wider debate on balancing development with constitutional safeguards.
