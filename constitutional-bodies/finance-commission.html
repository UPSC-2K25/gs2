Finance Commission

1. Constitutional Position and Significance

The Finance Commission is a constitutional body established under Article 280. It recommends the distribution of specified tax revenues between the Union and the States and the principles governing grants-in-aid of State revenues.

It is a central institution of fiscal federalism. The Constitution recognises that the Union and States have different expenditure responsibilities and revenue-raising capacities. The Finance Commission helps address this vertical and horizontal imbalance through an institutional process of assessment and recommendation.

The Commission is constituted periodically by the President and operates independently in the discharge of its assigned functions. Its recommendations are advisory in constitutional form, but they carry substantial institutional and fiscal significance.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Article 280: Provides for constitution of a Finance Commission by the President at the expiration of every fifth year or at such earlier time as the President considers necessary.
  • Article 281: Requires the President to cause the recommendations of the Finance Commission, together with an explanatory memorandum as to action taken, to be laid before each House of Parliament.
  • Article 270: Deals with distribution of specified Union taxes between the Union and the States.
  • Article 271: Provides for surcharge for purposes of the Union, subject to the constitutional provisions.
  • Article 275: Provides for grants-in-aid from the Union to certain States as Parliament may provide by law.
  • Article 282: Enables the Union or a State to make grants for any public purpose, even where the purpose is not one with respect to which the legislature may make laws.
  • Article 293: Governs borrowing by States, including specified conditions where a State has outstanding liabilities to the Union.
  • Article 279: Provides for certification of net proceeds of taxes by the CAG, a function relevant to the fiscal distribution framework.

3. Composition and Appointment

Article 280(1) provides that the Finance Commission shall consist of a Chairperson and four other members appointed by the President.

Article 280(2) authorises Parliament to determine by law the qualifications required for appointment as members and the manner in which they are to be selected.

The Finance Commission (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act, 1951 prescribes the qualifications and other matters relating to the Commission.

Statutory qualifications

  • The Chairperson should have experience in public affairs.
  • Other members may be selected from persons with judicial experience, knowledge of government finance and accounts, experience in financial and administrative matters, or special knowledge of economics, as prescribed by the Act.

The Commission may obtain information, call for evidence and undertake consultations necessary to formulate its recommendations.

4. Constitutional Functions under Article 280

The President refers specified matters to the Finance Commission. Article 280(3) identifies its principal functions:

  1. Recommend the distribution between the Union and the States of the net proceeds of taxes that are to be, or may be, divided between them, and the allocation among the States of their respective shares.
  2. Recommend the principles governing grants-in-aid of the revenues of States out of the Consolidated Fund of India.
  3. Recommend measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  4. Recommend measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Municipalities, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  5. Advise on any other matter referred to the Commission by the President in the interests of sound finance.

These functions connect national tax-sharing arrangements with local government finance and the broader objective of balanced development.

5. Vertical and Horizontal Devolution

Vertical devolution

Vertical devolution concerns the division of the divisible pool of Union taxes between the Union and all States collectively.

It addresses the difference between the expenditure responsibilities assigned to the States and their own revenue-raising capacity. The Commission assesses the financial requirements of both levels of government and recommends an appropriate share for the States.

Horizontal devolution

Horizontal devolution concerns the distribution of the States' collective share among individual States.

States differ in population, income levels, geographical conditions, demographic patterns, fiscal capacity, forest cover, development needs and cost of providing public services. The Commission uses an appropriate set of criteria to recommend distribution among them.

Horizontal criteria seek to balance equity, need, efficiency and incentives. No single criterion can fully capture the diverse fiscal and developmental circumstances of Indian States.

6. The Divisible Pool of Taxes

The divisible pool is the portion of Union tax revenues that is shared with the States under the constitutional framework. Article 270 is central to this arrangement.

Not every Union receipt is automatically part of the divisible pool. Constitutionally excluded categories include specified cesses and surcharges under the relevant provisions.

The distinction matters because a growing reliance on cesses and surcharges can affect the effective share of States in the Union's gross tax revenues, even where the nominal devolution percentage remains unchanged.

Article 279 assigns the CAG the function of ascertaining and certifying net proceeds of taxes. This certification should be distinguished from the Finance Commission's recommendations on distribution.

7. Criteria for Horizontal Distribution

Finance Commissions have used different criteria and weightings over time. Criteria are not permanently fixed by the Constitution; they are assessed and recommended by each Commission.

  • Income distance: Seeks to provide greater support to States with lower per-capita income or fiscal capacity.
  • Population: Recognises the scale of public service and development responsibilities associated with population.
  • Area: Recognises the higher costs of service delivery and administration in geographically large States.
  • Forest and ecology: Recognises ecological assets, conservation responsibilities and related constraints.
  • Demographic performance: Seeks to recognise States' performance in managing demographic change, depending on the methodology adopted.
  • Tax and fiscal effort: May reward efforts to mobilise own revenues and improve fiscal management.

The weights and definitions of these criteria have changed between Commissions. A sound analysis should identify the relevant Commission and award period rather than treating one formula as permanent.

8. Grants-in-Aid

Article 275 provides for grants-in-aid from the Union to States that Parliament may determine to be in need of assistance. Finance Commissions recommend principles and allocations within their terms of reference.

Grants can address needs that tax devolution alone may not adequately meet, including fiscal capacity gaps, specific service requirements and regionally concentrated challenges.

Common forms of grants considered in fiscal federalism

  • Revenue deficit grants: Support States where assessed revenue expenditure exceeds revenue receipts after taking account of devolution and other relevant resources.
  • Local body grants: Support Panchayats and Municipalities in accordance with the constitutional framework and Commission recommendations.
  • Disaster management grants: Support disaster risk reduction, response and preparedness under the applicable institutional arrangements.
  • Sector-specific or State-specific grants: May address identified service delivery or developmental requirements, where included in the Commission's recommendations.

Article 275 grants should be distinguished from grants under Article 282. The former are constitutionally structured grants-in-aid; the latter provide broader enabling authority for public-purpose grants.

9. Local Government Finance

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments gave constitutional status to Panchayats and Municipalities. However, local governments continue to face significant resource and capacity constraints.

Article 280(3)(bb) and Article 280(3)(c) connect the Union Finance Commission's work with the augmentation of State Consolidated Funds to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities, based on State Finance Commission recommendations.

State Finance Commissions

  • Article 243-I provides for a State Finance Commission to review the financial position of Panchayats.
  • Article 243-Y extends the State Finance Commission framework to Municipalities.
  • State Finance Commissions recommend principles for distribution of State revenues, assignment of taxes and grants to local bodies, and measures to improve their financial position.

Timely constitution of State Finance Commissions, acceptance of recommendations and predictable transfers are essential for effective local self-government.

10. Fiscal Federalism and the GST Framework

The introduction of the Goods and Services Tax changed the indirect tax structure and created a continuing need for Union-State coordination.

The GST Council under Article 279A is a separate constitutional institution. It makes recommendations on GST-related matters, including rates, exemptions and model laws. It is not a committee of the Finance Commission.

The Finance Commission considers broader questions of tax devolution, grants and fiscal relations. The GST Council focuses on the GST framework and cooperative decision-making in that field.

The two institutions are complementary: one addresses the broader distribution of public resources, while the other facilitates coordination in the GST system.

11. Finance Commission and Other Fiscal Institutions

  • Finance Commission: Constitutional body under Article 280; recommends tax devolution and grants-in-aid principles.
  • GST Council: Constitutional body under Article 279A; recommends matters concerning GST.
  • Planning Commission: Former executive institution, replaced by NITI Aayog; it did not have the constitutional status of the Finance Commission.
  • NITI Aayog: Executive policy institution that promotes cooperative federalism and policy coordination; it does not replace the Finance Commission's constitutional fiscal role.
  • CAG: Constitutional auditor with a specific certification role under Article 279 and broader public audit responsibilities.
  • State Finance Commission: Constitutional mechanism under Articles 243-I and 243-Y for local government finance at the State level.

12. Recommendations and Their Status

Article 281 requires the President to lay the Finance Commission's recommendations before Parliament together with an explanatory memorandum as to the action taken on them.

The recommendations are not automatically equivalent to legislation. Their implementation takes place through the relevant constitutional, budgetary and administrative processes.

The explanatory memorandum enables Parliament to examine which recommendations have been accepted, modified or not accepted and the reasons for the government's position.

The Commission's recommendations therefore carry significant constitutional weight while preserving the accountability of the elected executive and legislature for fiscal decisions.

13. Fiscal Responsibility and Public Finance

Finance Commissions operate within a wider public finance framework involving the Union and State budgets, fiscal responsibility legislation, public debt, tax administration and expenditure priorities.

  • Fiscal sustainability requires attention to revenue mobilisation, expenditure quality and debt management.
  • Predictable transfers support State planning and public service delivery.
  • Transparent criteria reduce uncertainty and support cooperative federalism.
  • Grants can address regional disparities but should be designed to avoid excessive fragmentation and administrative complexity.
  • Fiscal incentives should balance revenue effort, expenditure efficiency and equity.

14. Major Challenges

  • Vertical imbalance: States carry substantial expenditure responsibilities while important tax bases are concentrated at the Union level.
  • Horizontal disparities: Differences in income, geography, population and development needs complicate fair distribution.
  • Cesses and surcharges: Their treatment outside the divisible pool can affect the effective resources available to States.
  • Conditional transfers: Centrally designed schemes and conditions may reduce State flexibility in expenditure priorities.
  • Data quality: Differences in data availability and measurement can affect the reliability of distribution criteria.
  • Demographic tensions: States at different stages of demographic transition may have different views on population-based criteria.
  • Local government weakness: Delayed State Finance Commissions and limited own-source revenue constrain local bodies.
  • Fiscal stress: Debt, committed expenditure and limited revenue space affect the capacity of States to deliver services.
  • Climate and disaster risks: Increasing environmental and disaster-related costs require resilient fiscal arrangements.
  • Implementation gaps: Recommendations may not achieve intended outcomes without effective monitoring and institutional follow-up.

15. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Strengthen the transparency and quality of data used for devolution and grants.
  • Maintain a fair balance between equity, need, efficiency and incentives in horizontal distribution.
  • Improve predictability of Union transfers and reduce avoidable uncertainty in State finances.
  • Review the fiscal impact of cesses and surcharges on the effective divisible pool.
  • Strengthen State Finance Commissions through timely constitution, professional support and legislative follow-up.
  • Improve the own-source revenue capacity of Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Link grants to measurable outcomes while avoiding excessive conditionality.
  • Encourage transparent fiscal reporting and responsible debt management.
  • Strengthen cooperative forums for Union-State consultation on fiscal issues.
  • Integrate climate resilience, disaster preparedness and changing demographic needs into fiscal assessments.

16. Important Distinctions

  • Vertical versus horizontal devolution: Vertical concerns the Union-State division; horizontal concerns distribution among States.
  • Tax devolution versus grants: Devolution distributes the States' share of specified taxes; grants provide additional support under constitutional and legal arrangements.
  • Article 275 versus Article 282: Article 275 concerns grants-in-aid to States; Article 282 enables grants for any public purpose.
  • Finance Commission versus GST Council: The former addresses broad fiscal distribution; the latter makes recommendations on GST matters.
  • Finance Commission versus State Finance Commission: The former deals with Union-State fiscal relations and local body augmentation; the latter examines State-local government fiscal relations.
  • Recommendation versus implementation: Article 281 requires presentation of recommendations and an action-taken memorandum; implementation proceeds through the relevant governmental and legislative processes.

17. Integrated Understanding

The Finance Commission is a constitutional mechanism for reconciling national fiscal capacity with the diverse needs of India's States. It promotes equity, fiscal stability and cooperative federalism through tax devolution and grants.

Its effectiveness depends on credible data, transparent criteria, predictable transfers, State-level fiscal responsibility and empowered local governments. Fiscal federalism is not merely a question of distributing money; it is also about enabling each level of government to discharge its constitutional responsibilities effectively.
