Goods and Services Tax Council

1. Constitutional Position and Purpose

The Goods and Services Tax Council (GST Council) is a constitutional body established under Article 279A through the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016. It provides a forum for the Union and the States to jointly discuss and recommend important aspects of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) framework.

The Council is a major institution of cooperative federalism because GST restructures indirect taxation by bringing several Union and State taxes within a common national framework while preserving a constitutionally recognised role for both levels of government.

  • Constitutional provision: Article 279A.
  • Constitutional amendment: 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016.
  • Chairperson: Union Finance Minister.
  • Institutional purpose: Harmonisation and coordination of GST policy between the Union and States.

2. Background and Evolution of GST

Before GST, India's indirect tax structure involved multiple Union and State levies. The system included central excise duty, service tax, State VAT, entry taxes and several other taxes. The multiplicity of taxes and differing State systems contributed to cascading, compliance costs and barriers to a common market.

  • 2000: The Union Government initiated the process of examining a nationwide value-added tax structure and constituted an Empowered Committee of State Finance Ministers.
  • 2006: The Union Budget announced an intended introduction of GST from a future date, initiating a more structured reform process.
  • 2011: The 115th Constitutional Amendment Bill was introduced but did not become law.
  • 2014: The Constitution (122nd Amendment) Bill was introduced.
  • 2016: The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act received assent and provided the constitutional foundation for GST and the GST Council.
  • 2017: GST was implemented from 1 July 2017 following enactment of the central and State GST laws.

The GST Council was designed to provide an ongoing institutional mechanism for resolving policy questions arising from this major change in India's fiscal federal structure.

3. Constitutional Framework

  • Article 246A: Confers legislative power on Parliament and State Legislatures to make laws with respect to GST imposed by the Union or the State. Parliament has exclusive power to make laws with respect to GST where the supply of goods or services, or both, takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Article 269A: Provides for levy and collection of GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce by the Government of India and for apportionment between the Union and States in the constitutionally specified manner.
  • Article 279A: Establishes the GST Council and specifies its composition, functions and decision-making framework.
  • Article 286: Restricts the power of States to impose tax on supplies taking place outside the State or in the course of import into or export out of India, subject to the constitutional framework.
  • Article 366(12A): Defines GST as a tax on supply of goods or services or both, except taxes on the supply of alcoholic liquor for human consumption.
  • Article 366(26A): Defines services for constitutional purposes in the GST framework.

4. Composition of the GST Council

Article 279A(2) provides for the following composition:

  • Chairperson: Union Finance Minister.
  • Union representative: Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance.
  • State representatives: The Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or any other Minister nominated by each State Government.

The State representatives choose one among themselves to be the Vice-Chairperson of the Council for such period as they may decide.

The constitutional composition creates a joint forum in which the Union and States participate in tax policy deliberation. It does not make the Council a third legislative chamber.

5. Decision-Making and Voting

Article 279A(9) provides a special weighted voting arrangement:

  • The vote of the Central Government has a weightage of one-third of the total votes cast.
  • The votes of all State Governments taken together have a weightage of two-thirds of the total votes cast.
  • A decision requires a majority of not less than three-fourths of the weighted votes of the members present and voting.

Under Article 279A(8), one-half of the total number of members of the Council constitutes the quorum for a meeting.

The weighted voting arrangement is intended to prevent unilateral decision-making by either the Union or a single State. It encourages negotiated outcomes between the Union and the States collectively.

6. Functions and Recommendations under Article 279A(4)

The GST Council makes recommendations to the Union and the States on important GST matters, including:

  1. Taxes, cesses and surcharges levied by the Union, States and local bodies that may be subsumed in GST.
  2. Goods and services that may be subjected to, or exempted from, GST.
  3. Model GST laws, principles of levy, apportionment of GST on inter-State supplies and principles governing the place of supply.
  4. Threshold limits of turnover below which goods and services may be exempted from GST.
  5. Rates of GST, including floor rates with bands.
  6. Special rates for a specified period to raise additional resources during natural calamities or disasters.
  7. Special provisions with respect to certain States, including those in the North-Eastern region, Himalayan States and other constitutionally specified categories.

Article 279A(4) also permits the Council to recommend any other matter relating to GST as it may decide.

7. Special Provisions and Institutional Flexibility

  • Article 279A(5): The GST Council recommends the date on which GST may be levied on petroleum crude, high-speed diesel, motor spirit (petrol), natural gas and aviation turbine fuel.
  • Article 279A(6): The Council is guided by the need for a harmonised structure of GST and the development of a harmonised national market for goods and services.
  • Article 279A(7): The Council may establish a mechanism to adjudicate disputes arising out of its recommendations or their implementation between the Union and one or more States, between the Union and States on one side and one or more States on the other, or between two or more States, subject to the constitutional provision.
  • Article 279A(10): The Council may determine its procedure for performing its functions.
  • Article 279A(11): The Council may decide on the modalities to resolve disputes arising from its recommendations or their implementation.

8. GST Structure and the Council's Policy Role

The GST system operates through a combination of Union and State legislation:

  • Central GST (CGST): Levied by the Union on intra-State supplies under the central law.
  • State GST (SGST): Levied by States on intra-State supplies under their respective State laws.
  • Union Territory GST (UTGST): Levied in Union Territories without legislatures under the relevant law.
  • Integrated GST (IGST): Levied by the Union on inter-State supplies and imports, with apportionment under the constitutional and statutory framework.

For an ordinary intra-State taxable supply, CGST and the applicable SGST or UTGST are generally levied together. For an inter-State supply, IGST applies under the relevant provisions.

The Council's recommendations influence rates, exemptions, compliance design and administrative coordination. The actual levy and collection operate through legislation and statutory rules.

9. GST Council and Legislative Powers

The Council is a recommendation-making constitutional body. It does not itself enact tax laws. Parliament and State Legislatures exercise legislative powers under Article 246A.

This distinction is essential to understanding the relationship between cooperative federalism and legislative sovereignty within the Constitution.

In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022), the Supreme Court held that recommendations of the GST Council have persuasive value and are not binding on Parliament and State Legislatures in the exercise of their GST legislative powers. The Court emphasised the cooperative and consultative nature of the GST framework.

The judgment does not make the Council irrelevant. Its recommendations remain institutionally important for coordination, harmonisation and the development of a common market.

10. GST Council and Fiscal Federalism

GST altered the distribution of indirect taxation powers and created a system of shared policy development.

Cooperative federalism

  • Union and State representatives deliberate together on tax policy.
  • Weighted voting provides a formal mechanism for joint decisions.
  • Common rules and rates can reduce fragmentation of the domestic market.
  • States participate in shaping policy even though the Union administers important elements of the IGST framework.

Competitive and fiscal concerns

  • States may differ in their revenue needs, economic structures and fiscal capacities.
  • Uniform rates may have different distributional consequences across States.
  • States have less independent flexibility over several indirect tax instruments that were subsumed into GST.
  • Revenue dependence and compensation arrangements influence State fiscal autonomy.

11. GST Compensation and Revenue Concerns

The 101st Constitutional Amendment provided for a statutory compensation framework to address potential revenue losses of States arising from the introduction of GST.

  • The Goods and Services Tax (Compensation to States) Act, 2017 provided for compensation to States for a specified transition period.
  • The compensation framework used a prescribed base-year revenue and a guaranteed annual growth assumption under the statute.
  • A compensation cess was levied on specified supplies to finance the compensation mechanism.
  • Revenue shortfalls, borrowing arrangements and the pandemic period raised important issues of fiscal federalism and Centre-State trust.

The original statutory compensation period was five years from the date of GST implementation, ending in June 2022. The subsequent continuation of compensation cess collections for specified purposes should be distinguished from an extension of the original State compensation guarantee.

The compensation experience demonstrates that institutional trust depends not only on the constitutional design of the Council but also on predictable revenue arrangements and timely fulfilment of statutory commitments.

12. GST Council and Other Institutions

  • GST Network (GSTN): Provides the technology and information-technology infrastructure for GST registration, returns, payments and related processes.
  • Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs: Administers central indirect tax laws, including central GST and customs functions, within its statutory mandate.
  • State tax administrations: Administer State GST laws and perform registration, assessment, audit and enforcement functions under the applicable framework.
  • GST Council Secretariat: Supports the functioning, meetings and administrative coordination of the Council.
  • GST Appellate Tribunal: Provides a statutory appellate mechanism for disputes under GST law.
  • Finance Commission: Makes recommendations on tax devolution and grants under Article 280; it is distinct from the GST Council's role in GST policy coordination.

13. Achievements and Significance

  • Created a common institutional forum for Union-State indirect tax coordination.
  • Supported the development of a more integrated national market for goods and services.
  • Enabled common deliberation on tax rates, exemptions, thresholds and compliance rules.
  • Provided an institutional process for addressing differences between the Union and States.
  • Encouraged greater use of digital tax administration and common compliance systems.
  • Demonstrated the possibility of negotiated policy-making in a complex federal structure.

14. Challenges and Criticisms

  • State fiscal autonomy: States have concerns about the reduced flexibility to independently alter important indirect tax rates and bases.
  • Revenue uncertainty: Revenue shortfalls and differences in State economic structures complicate fiscal planning.
  • Decision-making pressure: The need for consensus may slow decisions, while strong central influence may generate concerns about balance.
  • Complexity: Multiple rates, exemptions, classifications and compliance requirements can increase administrative and business costs.
  • Compliance burden: Small businesses may face difficulties with returns, reconciliations, digital systems and working-capital constraints.
  • Inverted duty structures: Differences between input and output tax rates can create refund and working-capital issues.
  • Dispute resolution: Classification, valuation, place-of-supply and input-tax-credit disputes can generate litigation.
  • Centre-State trust: Predictability in revenue transfers, data sharing and consultation remains important for institutional credibility.
  • Exclusion of certain taxes: The continued treatment of specified petroleum products outside the operative GST levy framework affects the completeness of the common tax base.

15. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Strengthen the Council as a forum for genuine consultation and evidence-based consensus-building.
  2. Improve transparency through publication of agendas, minutes, explanatory notes and follow-up on major decisions, subject to appropriate confidentiality.
  3. Enhance predictability in revenue-sharing and statutory payment arrangements.
  4. Simplify rate structures and reduce unnecessary exemptions while protecting essential and vulnerable consumption.
  5. Improve GST compliance systems for micro, small and medium enterprises.
  6. Strengthen the GSTN platform, grievance redressal, data security and system reliability.
  7. Improve coordination between Union and State tax administrations and reduce overlapping compliance requirements.
  8. Strengthen dispute resolution and clarify recurring classification and input-tax-credit issues.
  9. Use impact assessments to evaluate the effects of rate changes on consumers, businesses and State revenues.
  10. Continue dialogue on the treatment of remaining major indirect tax areas within the constitutional framework.

16. Important Distinctions

  • Article 246A vs Article 279A: Article 246A confers GST legislative power; Article 279A establishes the GST Council.
  • GST Council vs GSTN: The Council is a constitutional policy forum; GSTN is the technology infrastructure supporting tax administration.
  • GST Council vs Finance Commission: The GST Council recommends GST policy measures; the Finance Commission recommends tax devolution and grants under Article 280.
  • CGST vs SGST: CGST is levied by the Union and SGST by the State on intra-State supplies, subject to the statutory framework.
  • IGST vs CGST: IGST applies to inter-State supplies and imports; CGST is part of the intra-State tax structure.
  • Recommendation vs legislation: The GST Council recommends; Parliament and State Legislatures enact laws under their constitutional powers.
  • GST compensation period vs cess collection: The original State compensation guarantee ended in June 2022; continued cess collection for specified purposes is a separate statutory matter.

17. Integrated Understanding

The GST Council is a significant experiment in institutionalised fiscal federalism. It attempts to reconcile a common national market with the constitutional position of States as important tax authorities.

Its effectiveness depends on meaningful consultation, mutual trust, transparent decision-making, predictable revenue arrangements and respect for the legislative powers of Parliament and State Legislatures. The Council is therefore both a tax-policy institution and a test of India's cooperative federalism.
