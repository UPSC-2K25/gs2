Comptroller and Auditor General of India

1. Constitutional Position and Significance

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India (CAG) is an independent constitutional authority responsible for auditing the accounts of the Union and the States and performing other audit functions prescribed by the Constitution and law.

The CAG is often described as the guardian of the public purse because independent audit enables legislatures and citizens to examine how public money has been raised, authorised, spent and accounted for.

The CAG does not ordinarily decide government policy or approve expenditure before it occurs. Its central role is independent audit and reporting, which supports legislative financial control and public accountability.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Article 148: Establishes the office of the CAG and provides for appointment, removal and service safeguards.
  • Article 149: Provides that Parliament shall prescribe the duties and powers of the CAG in relation to the accounts of the Union, States and other authorities or bodies.
  • Article 150: Provides that the form of accounts of the Union and States shall be prescribed by the President on the advice of the CAG.
  • Article 151: Provides for audit reports of the CAG to be submitted to the President or Governor, as applicable, for placement before the relevant legislature.
  • Article 279(1): Provides that the net proceeds of taxes shall be ascertained and certified by the CAG, whose certificate is final.

The principal legislation governing the CAG's duties, powers and conditions of service is the Comptroller and Auditor-General's (Duties, Powers and Conditions of Service) Act, 1971.

3. Appointment and Tenure

Under Article 148(1), the CAG is appointed by the President by warrant under the President's hand and seal.

The CAG holds office for six years or until attaining the age of 65 years, whichever is earlier, subject to the constitutional and statutory framework.

The office-holder may resign by writing under their hand addressed to the President.

Removal safeguard

Article 148(1) provides that the CAG shall not be removed from office except in like manner and on like grounds as a Judge of the Supreme Court. This protects the office from arbitrary removal by the executive.

The removal process requires the constitutionally prescribed parliamentary procedure for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, involving special majorities in both Houses of Parliament.

4. Independence and Service Safeguards

  • Removal protection: The CAG has the same removal safeguards as a Supreme Court Judge.
  • Security of service conditions: The conditions of service cannot be varied to the CAG's disadvantage after appointment.
  • Financial independence: Administrative expenses of the CAG's office, including salaries, allowances and pensions of persons serving in that office, are charged upon the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Post-retirement restriction: Article 148(4) prohibits the CAG from being eligible for further office under the Government of India or any State Government after ceasing to hold office.
  • Constitutional status: The office is established by the Constitution rather than by an ordinary executive order.

These safeguards are intended to protect the independence and credibility of public audit. Institutional independence also requires adequate staff, technical capacity, timely access to records and freedom to report audit findings without improper interference.

5. Duties and Powers under Article 149

Article 149 provides that Parliament shall prescribe the duties and powers of the CAG in relation to the accounts of the Union and States and other authorities or bodies. The CAG's principal statutory framework is the 1971 Act.

The CAG's responsibilities include:

  • Audit of expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India and the Consolidated Funds of States and Union Territories having legislatures, as provided by law.
  • Audit of transactions relating to the Contingency Funds and Public Accounts within the statutory framework.
  • Audit of trading, manufacturing, profit and loss accounts, balance sheets and other subsidiary accounts kept by government departments.
  • Audit of receipts and expenditure of bodies or authorities where the applicable law or other legal provisions confer audit responsibility.
  • Audit of specified government companies and corporations in accordance with the Companies Act and other applicable legislation.
  • Certification of specified accounts and financial statements where required by law.
  • Preparation and submission of audit reports to the constitutional authorities specified in Article 151.

6. Types of Audit

Financial audit

Financial audit examines whether financial statements and accounts present information fairly and comply with applicable accounting requirements. It supports confidence in the reliability of public financial reporting.

Compliance audit

Compliance audit examines whether transactions, expenditure and administrative actions conform to applicable laws, rules, regulations, orders and established procedures.

Performance audit

Performance audit examines whether government programmes and activities are conducted with due regard to economy, efficiency and effectiveness.

  • Economy: Acquiring resources at the appropriate quality and cost.
  • Efficiency: Using resources to achieve maximum output with minimum avoidable waste.
  • Effectiveness: Achieving intended objectives and outcomes.

Performance audit does not mean that the CAG substitutes its policy preferences for those of the elected government. It evaluates implementation and outcomes against appropriate criteria and evidence.

7. Audit of Government Expenditure

Public expenditure is subject to constitutional, legislative and administrative controls. The CAG's audit helps determine whether expenditure was authorised, properly accounted for and implemented in accordance with applicable requirements.

Regularity and propriety

Audit may examine whether expenditure conforms to legal and financial rules and whether public funds have been used with due regard to propriety and accountability.

Appropriation audit

Appropriation audit examines whether money was spent for the purposes and within the limits authorised by the legislature through appropriation legislation and other applicable provisions.

Excess expenditure

Where expenditure exceeds the authorised grant or appropriation, the excess is subject to the constitutional and legislative process for regularisation, including examination by the Public Accounts Committee.

8. Audit of Receipts and Revenue

Audit of receipts examines whether government revenue has been assessed, collected and accounted for in accordance with law and established procedures.

It may examine tax administration, non-tax revenue, recoveries, exemptions, concessions, arrears and systems designed to prevent leakage.

Receipt audit is important because public accountability concerns not only how money is spent but also whether revenue due to the public exchequer is properly assessed and collected.

9. Audit of Public Sector Enterprises and Other Bodies

The CAG's audit jurisdiction extends to specified public sector enterprises and other bodies under the Constitution, the 1971 Act and relevant legislation.

Government companies

The Companies Act, 2013 provides for the CAG's role in relation to government companies. Under Section 139(5), the CAG appoints the statutory auditor for a government company or government-controlled company within the statutory definition. The CAG may issue directions on the manner in which the company's accounts are to be audited and conduct supplementary or test audit as provided by Section 143.

Statutory corporations

Audit arrangements for statutory corporations depend on the legislation governing each corporation. Some are audited directly by the CAG, while others have specific statutory audit arrangements involving the CAG.

Grants and bodies receiving public funds

Audit of certain bodies and authorities receiving grants or loans from government may be undertaken under the conditions and thresholds prescribed by the 1971 Act and other applicable legal provisions.

10. Article 150: Form of Accounts

Article 150 provides that the accounts of the Union and States shall be kept in such form as the President may, on the advice of the CAG, prescribe.

This provision gives the CAG an important constitutional role in the accounting framework of government. It helps promote consistency, transparency and reliable financial reporting across public institutions.

The CAG's advisory role concerning the form of accounts should be distinguished from the constitutional responsibility of the executive to prepare and maintain government accounts under the applicable arrangements.

11. Article 151: Audit Reports

Article 151 provides the constitutional route for submission and legislative placement of CAG reports.

  • Reports relating to the accounts of the Union are submitted to the President.
  • The President causes the reports to be laid before each House of Parliament.
  • Reports relating to the accounts of a State are submitted to the Governor.
  • The Governor causes the reports to be laid before the State Legislature.

This process connects independent audit with legislative accountability. Audit reports provide an evidentiary basis for legislative scrutiny of government expenditure and administration.

12. CAG and Parliamentary Financial Control

The CAG is closely connected with the financial oversight work of Parliament, particularly through the Public Accounts Committee (PAC).

Public Accounts Committee

The PAC examines appropriation accounts, finance accounts and CAG audit reports referred to it. It scrutinises whether public money was spent in accordance with legislative authorisation and applicable financial rules.

Committee on Public Undertakings

The Committee on Public Undertakings examines reports and accounts of public undertakings within its remit and considers CAG reports relating to them.

Estimates Committee

The Estimates Committee examines estimates and may suggest economies, improvements in organisation and administrative efficiency. It is not a substitute for the CAG's audit function.

The CAG supplies independent audit evidence; parliamentary committees examine the findings and may recommend corrective action. The CAG does not itself impose legislative or political accountability.

13. CAG and the Financial Accountability Cycle

  1. Budget authorisation: Parliament or the State Legislature authorises expenditure through the constitutional budget process.
  2. Execution: The executive collects revenue and spends public funds in accordance with law and authorisation.
  3. Accounting: Transactions are recorded in government accounts.
  4. Audit: The CAG examines accounts, transactions and performance within its jurisdiction.
  5. Reporting: Audit findings are submitted to the President or Governor under Article 151.
  6. Legislative scrutiny: Reports are considered by the legislature and relevant committees.
  7. Corrective action: Departments may be required to respond, explain irregularities and take remedial measures.

This cycle links legislative control over public finance with executive responsibility and independent audit.

14. Article 279 and Certification of Net Proceeds

Article 279(1) provides that the net proceeds of a tax or duty, or part of a tax or duty, in an area shall be ascertained and certified by the CAG, whose certificate is final.

This provision is important for fiscal federalism because the distribution of certain tax revenues between the Union and States depends on the determination of net proceeds under the constitutional framework.

The role under Article 279 should be distinguished from the Finance Commission's role under Article 280. The Finance Commission recommends principles of distribution, while Article 279 assigns the CAG a specific certification function.

15. Judicial and Institutional Perspective

The CAG's constitutional role is closely connected with legislative control over public finance and the principle that public expenditure must be accountable.

Judicial decisions concerning audit jurisdiction, government companies and public authorities must be read with the relevant constitutional provisions and statutory framework. The exact scope of audit over a body depends on the legal basis for that audit, rather than on the mere fact that the body performs a public function.

The CAG's reports are important accountability documents, but they are not judicial findings of criminal guilt or automatic determinations of individual liability. Questions of legal liability, recovery or criminal responsibility are decided through the competent legal and administrative processes.

16. Importance of the CAG in Governance

  • Legislative control: Helps legislatures scrutinise expenditure and revenue administration.
  • Transparency: Makes financial and administrative information available for public scrutiny.
  • Fiscal discipline: Identifies irregularities, waste, unauthorised expenditure and control weaknesses.
  • Performance improvement: Highlights implementation gaps and opportunities to improve public programmes.
  • Federal accountability: Supports scrutiny of public finances at Union and State levels.
  • Public trust: Independent audit strengthens confidence that public resources are subject to oversight.
  • Anti-corruption framework: Audit findings can identify risks and refer matters to appropriate authorities, though the CAG is not an investigating or prosecuting agency.

17. Challenges Facing the CAG

  • Audit delays: Delayed completion and submission of reports can reduce their usefulness for timely corrective action.
  • Resource constraints: Shortages of specialised personnel and technology can affect audit coverage.
  • Complex public spending: Public-private partnerships, digital procurement, complex financial instruments and autonomous bodies create new audit challenges.
  • Follow-up deficiencies: Audit recommendations may not lead to prompt corrective action.
  • Information access: Delayed, incomplete or poorly organised records can hinder audit.
  • Scope disputes: Disagreements may arise over the CAG's jurisdiction in relation to particular entities or transactions.
  • Performance measurement: Assessing outcomes in complex social programmes requires clear criteria and reliable data.
  • Public interpretation: Audit observations may be presented as final findings of wrongdoing even when they require further examination.
  • Changing technology: Digital government and data-intensive programmes require modern audit tools and specialist expertise.

18. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Strengthen the independence and transparency of the appointment process while preserving constitutional safeguards.
  • Ensure adequate staffing, technical expertise and modern audit infrastructure.
  • Develop data analytics and risk-based audit methods for complex public expenditure.
  • Improve timely access to records and digital government systems.
  • Strengthen follow-up mechanisms on audit observations and recommendations.
  • Improve coordination between the CAG, PAC, Committee on Public Undertakings and government departments.
  • Expand capacity for performance audits while maintaining clear, evidence-based audit criteria.
  • Clarify audit arrangements for publicly funded bodies and complex public-private structures through appropriate legal frameworks.
  • Publish reports and accessible summaries to improve public understanding.
  • Ensure that audit findings are considered fairly and that departments receive an opportunity to respond through established procedures.

19. Important Distinctions

  • CAG and PAC: The CAG conducts independent audit; the PAC examines audit reports and accounts on behalf of the legislature.
  • CAG and Finance Commission: The CAG audits and performs specified certification functions; the Finance Commission recommends principles of fiscal distribution.
  • Audit and investigation: Audit examines accounts, transactions and performance; investigation into criminal offences is conducted by competent investigative agencies.
  • Audit and policy-making: The CAG evaluates financial and administrative performance but does not replace the elected government's policy-making authority.
  • Financial audit and performance audit: Financial audit examines financial reporting; performance audit examines economy, efficiency and effectiveness.
  • Article 150 and Article 151: Article 150 concerns the form of accounts; Article 151 concerns submission and legislative placement of audit reports.
  • Article 279 and Article 280: Article 279 concerns certification of net proceeds; Article 280 concerns the Finance Commission.

20. Integrated Understanding

The CAG is a central pillar of legislative financial control. Its independent audit enables Parliament and State Legislatures to examine the use of public resources and strengthens accountability across government.

Its effectiveness depends on institutional independence, professional capacity, timely reporting and meaningful legislative follow-up. Audit is most valuable when its findings lead to improved financial management, better programme implementation and stronger public trust.
