1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Parliamentary committees are smaller groups of members constituted or appointed by one or both Houses of Parliament to examine matters in detail, scrutinise government functioning, review expenditure, consider legislation and assist the legislature in discharging its responsibilities.

The committee system supplements the work of the full House. Parliament has limited time for detailed examination of every policy, expenditure item and legislative proposal. Committees provide a more specialised forum for scrutiny, evidence gathering and deliberation.

Committees are generally established under the Rules of Procedure of the Houses or through resolutions and specific statutory or parliamentary arrangements. They are not, as a general category, constitutional bodies created directly by the Constitution.

Their institutional significance is linked to:

  • Legislative scrutiny and improvement of Bills.
  • Financial accountability and examination of public expenditure.
  • Oversight of ministries, departments and public institutions.
  • Detailed examination of complex policy issues.
  • Cross-party deliberation and informed parliamentary decision-making.

2. Constitutional and Procedural Framework

The Constitution does not provide a single comprehensive chapter establishing the parliamentary committee system. Its operation is supported by constitutional provisions and parliamentary rules.

Provision Relevance
Article 105Privileges and powers of Parliament and its members, relevant to parliamentary proceedings and committee work.
Article 118Power of each House to make rules regulating its procedure and conduct of business.
Article 119Parliament may regulate by law procedure in Parliament in relation to financial business.
Article 122Courts are restricted from questioning parliamentary proceedings on the ground of alleged procedural irregularity, subject to constitutional limits.
Article 75(3)Collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha, within which parliamentary oversight operates.

The detailed structure, membership, functions and procedures of committees are governed principally by the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business of the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha, together with decisions of the presiding officers and established parliamentary practice.

3. Classification of Parliamentary Committees

Parliamentary committees can broadly be classified according to their duration and the nature of their work.

Based on duration

  • Standing committees: constituted periodically or on a continuing basis. They are generally reconstituted at regular intervals and perform recurring parliamentary functions.
  • Ad hoc committees: constituted for a specific purpose or inquiry. They cease to exist after submitting their report or completing the assigned task.

Based on function

  • Financial committees.
  • Department-related standing committees.
  • Committees examining Bills and legislative matters.
  • Committees dealing with privileges, petitions and ethics.
  • Housekeeping and administrative committees.
  • Joint parliamentary committees and other ad hoc investigative committees.

4. Financial Committees: Overview

Financial committees are central to Parliament's control over public finance. They examine expenditure, audit findings, public undertakings and the implementation of financial authorisations.

Committee Membership Principal function
Public Accounts Committee 22: 15 from Lok Sabha and 7 from Rajya Sabha Examines public expenditure and CAG-related audit findings.
Estimates Committee 30, all from Lok Sabha Examines estimates and suggests economy, efficiency and administrative improvement.
Committee on Public Undertakings 22: 15 from Lok Sabha and 7 from Rajya Sabha Examines the accounts, reports and functioning of selected public undertakings.

These three committees are often described as the principal financial committees of Parliament. Their functions are complementary but distinct.

5. Public Accounts Committee

The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) examines how public money authorised by Parliament has been collected, spent and accounted for. It is a major instrument of legislative financial control.

Composition

  • Consists of 22 members: 15 elected by the Lok Sabha and 7 elected by the Rajya Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • A minister is not eligible to be elected as a member of the committee.
  • The chairperson is appointed by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
  • By established convention, the chairperson has generally been selected from the opposition, although this is not a constitutional requirement.

Functions

  • Examines the Appropriation Accounts of the Union Government.
  • Examines the Finance Accounts of the Union Government.
  • Considers reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General relating to public expenditure.
  • Examines whether money was legally available and applied to the purpose for which Parliament authorised it.
  • Considers cases of excess expenditure and the circumstances in which expenditure exceeded the authorised amount.
  • Examines financial irregularities, weaknesses in internal control and failures of financial administration highlighted by audit.

Relationship with the CAG

The Comptroller and Auditor General provides the audit foundation for much of the PAC's work. The CAG's reports are laid before Parliament under Article 151. The PAC examines relevant audit findings and calls upon departments and officials to explain deficiencies.

The CAG is often described as the friend, philosopher and guide of the PAC because audit expertise assists the committee in examining financial administration.

Limitations

  • The PAC is primarily an ex post facto oversight body; it examines expenditure after it has occurred.
  • It does not ordinarily determine government policy or substitute its own policy preferences for those of the executive.
  • Its recommendations are generally advisory and are not equivalent to judicial orders.
  • Its effectiveness depends on the quality of audit reports, departmental cooperation and follow-up action.

6. Estimates Committee

The Estimates Committee examines government expenditure estimates and suggests ways to improve economy, efficiency and administrative organisation.

Composition

  • Consists of 30 members, all elected from the Lok Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually by proportional representation through the single transferable vote.
  • A minister cannot be elected as a member.
  • The chairperson is appointed by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha.

Functions

  • Examines estimates included in the budget.
  • Suggests economies consistent with policy and administrative efficiency.
  • Recommends improvements in organisation, administration and management.
  • Considers whether money is being used efficiently and whether alternative policies or methods may produce better results.
  • May suggest the form in which estimates should be presented to Parliament to facilitate scrutiny.

The committee generally examines selected estimates and does not review every ministry's entire budget in the same manner every year.

Unlike the PAC, which mainly examines expenditure after it has been incurred, the Estimates Committee focuses on estimates and the efficiency of proposed or continuing expenditure.

7. Committee on Public Undertakings

The Committee on Public Undertakings examines the functioning and accountability of selected public sector undertakings (PSUs) and the use of public resources within them.

Composition

  • Consists of 22 members: 15 from the Lok Sabha and 7 from the Rajya Sabha.
  • Members are elected annually through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • A minister cannot be a member.
  • The chairperson is appointed by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha.

Functions

  • Examines reports and accounts of selected public undertakings.
  • Examines reports of the CAG concerning public undertakings.
  • Considers whether public undertakings are managed in accordance with sound business principles and prudent commercial practices.
  • Examines efficiency, autonomy, financial performance and accountability.
  • Reviews whether public undertakings are fulfilling their stated objectives and statutory responsibilities.

The committee's examination is subject to the limits prescribed by its rules. It does not ordinarily examine matters of major government policy or detailed day-to-day administration that fall outside its mandate.

9. Department-Related Standing Committees

Department-Related Standing Committees (DRSCs) provide systematic parliamentary scrutiny of the work of ministries and departments. They were introduced in 1993 and expanded in 2004.

There are 24 DRSCs, each linked to specified ministries and departments. Their purpose is to enable detailed examination of government activity beyond the time available during ordinary House proceedings.

Composition

  • Each committee has 31 members.
  • 21 members are drawn from the Lok Sabha and nominated by the Speaker.
  • 10 members are drawn from the Rajya Sabha and nominated by the Chairman.
  • Ministers are not eligible to be members of these committees.
  • Members are generally appointed for a term not exceeding one year, subject to the applicable rules and reconstitution.

Functions

  • Consider Demands for Grants of the ministries and departments assigned to them.
  • Examine Bills referred to them by the presiding officer or House under the applicable procedure.
  • Examine annual reports of ministries and departments.
  • Consider policy documents and other matters referred to them.
  • Submit reports containing observations and recommendations to Parliament.

DRSCs generally do not examine day-to-day administration or matters that are primarily within the jurisdiction of other parliamentary committees. Their reports are valuable instruments of legislative scrutiny, but their recommendations are generally not binding on the government.

10. Examination of Demands for Grants

One of the most important functions of DRSCs is the examination of Demands for Grants of the ministries and departments assigned to them.

  • They examine proposed expenditure and the priorities reflected in budget demands.
  • They consider whether allocations are adequate and aligned with stated objectives.
  • They may examine programme implementation, utilisation and administrative capacity.
  • They submit reports to Parliament before the relevant Demands for Grants are voted upon.

These reports help members make informed decisions during the budget process. However, the committees do not themselves vote on or authorise the Demands for Grants; that authority belongs to the Lok Sabha.

11. Committees on Legislative Matters

Parliament uses committees to examine legislative proposals, delegated legislation and specific policy questions. Some committees are standing committees; others are constituted for a particular Bill or issue.

Select Committee

A Select Committee is appointed by one House to examine a Bill referred to it. Its membership is drawn from that House. It studies the Bill, may seek evidence or receive representations and submits recommendations for consideration by the House.

Joint Committee

A Joint Committee consists of members of both Houses and is constituted to examine a Bill or a specific matter. Its composition and terms of reference depend on the resolution or procedure through which it is established.

Committee on Subordinate Legislation

This committee examines whether powers to make rules, regulations, orders and other forms of delegated legislation have been exercised within the limits of the authority conferred by Parliament.

It may examine whether delegated legislation:

  • Is consistent with the Constitution and the parent Act.
  • Contains matters that should properly have been dealt with in the parent legislation.
  • Imposes taxes, fees or other charges without adequate statutory authority.
  • Operates retrospectively without proper authority.
  • Appears to involve an unusual or unexpected use of delegated powers.

12. Committee on Government Assurances

The Committee on Government Assurances examines assurances, promises and undertakings given by ministers on the floor of the House and reviews the extent to which they have been fulfilled.

Its work helps ensure that ministerial statements during parliamentary proceedings are not merely rhetorical commitments but are followed by administrative action and reporting.

The committee strengthens executive accountability by tracking commitments made in response to questions, debates and other parliamentary proceedings.

13. Committee on Petitions

The Committee on Petitions examines petitions presented to the House or referred to it under the applicable rules.

  • It considers grievances and representations submitted through the parliamentary petition process.
  • It may examine matters of public importance raised by petitioners within its jurisdiction.
  • It may seek information from ministries or other authorities.
  • It submits reports and recommendations to the House.

The petition mechanism provides citizens with a formal route to bring public grievances and concerns to parliamentary attention, subject to procedural requirements.

14. Committee of Privileges

The Committee of Privileges examines questions involving breach of parliamentary privilege or contempt of the House referred to it.

Article 105 recognises the privileges and powers of Parliament and its members. The committee assists the House by examining facts, considering relevant rules and conventions and making recommendations.

The committee's work must be understood in the context of the need to protect the functioning and independence of Parliament while respecting constitutional rights and the rule of law.

15. Committee on Ethics

The Committee on Ethics examines matters relating to ethical conduct and standards among members of the relevant House.

Its responsibilities may include:

  • Considering complaints or matters referred to it concerning members' conduct.
  • Examining alleged violations of the House's ethical standards.
  • Recommending appropriate action under the applicable rules and procedures.
  • Promoting ethical conduct, transparency and public confidence in parliamentary institutions.

Ethical oversight is distinct from the criminal justice process. Parliamentary disciplinary procedures and criminal proceedings may operate under different legal standards and jurisdictions.

17. Joint Parliamentary Committees

A Joint Parliamentary Committee (JPC) is a committee constituted with members from both Houses to investigate or examine a specified matter. It is generally established through a motion adopted by Parliament or through the prescribed parliamentary process.

A JPC may be assigned terms of reference relating to a particular policy issue, transaction, administrative matter or alleged irregularity. Its composition, powers and duration depend on the resolution establishing it.

Functions and significance

  • Examines a matter in detail on behalf of Parliament.
  • May call for documents and records within its authorised powers.
  • May examine witnesses and obtain evidence according to its terms of reference and applicable procedure.
  • Submits a report to Parliament with findings and recommendations.

A JPC is an instrument of parliamentary investigation and scrutiny. It is not a court and does not exercise judicial power. Its conclusions do not automatically determine criminal or civil liability.

18. Powers and Limitations of Parliamentary Committees

Committees exercise powers and functions under the Constitution, parliamentary rules, resolutions and their terms of reference. They may be authorised to call for evidence, records and persons relevant to their work.

However, their powers are not unlimited:

  • Their jurisdiction is confined by their terms of reference and the rules governing them.
  • They cannot exercise powers that belong to courts or other constitutional authorities merely by virtue of being committees.
  • Their recommendations are generally advisory and require government or House action for implementation.
  • They cannot ordinarily substitute their views for the legislative decision of the House.
  • They must respect constitutional limits, parliamentary privileges and applicable procedural safeguards.

19. Committee Reports and Government Action

Committee reports provide Parliament with detailed findings and recommendations. Their practical impact depends on how effectively the House, government and relevant ministries respond.

  • Reports are presented or laid before the relevant House in accordance with procedure.
  • Recommendations may call for policy changes, administrative correction, improved financial control or legislative amendments.
  • Ministries may be asked to provide responses or action-taken statements.
  • Committees may examine the extent to which recommendations have been accepted and implemented.

In many cases, committee recommendations are not legally binding. Their influence arises from parliamentary authority, public scrutiny, evidence-based analysis and the expectation that the executive will explain its response.

20. Importance of Parliamentary Committees

  • Detailed scrutiny: enable examination of complex legislation, policies and expenditure beyond the time available in the full House.
  • Executive accountability: require ministries and departments to explain decisions and implementation outcomes.
  • Financial control: strengthen oversight of public money, audit findings and public undertakings.
  • Cross-party deliberation: provide a setting for members from different parties to examine issues in a less adversarial environment.
  • Expert input: allow committees to obtain information and representations from specialists and stakeholders, subject to procedure.
  • Legislative quality: help identify drafting problems, implementation risks and unintended consequences in proposed legislation.
  • Continuity: maintain scrutiny of government departments and policy areas across parliamentary sessions.

21. Challenges in the Committee System

  • Limited referral of Bills: not all Bills are referred to committees, reducing opportunities for detailed examination.
  • Time constraints: committees may have limited time to examine complex Bills, budgets and policy questions.
  • Vacancies and attendance: irregular attendance or changes in membership can affect continuity and depth of scrutiny.
  • Information asymmetry: ministries may possess greater technical and administrative information than members and committee staff.
  • Research capacity: limited independent research and specialist support can constrain evidence-based examination.
  • Implementation gaps: recommendations may not receive adequate or timely government action.
  • Public awareness: committee work is often less visible than debates in the full House.
  • Confidentiality and transparency: balancing candid deliberation with appropriate public access remains a continuing concern.

22. Reforms for Strengthening Parliamentary Committees

  • Refer a larger proportion of important Bills to the relevant committees for detailed scrutiny.
  • Provide sufficient time for examination of Bills, budgets and policy matters.
  • Strengthen research, legal, financial and technical support for committees.
  • Improve attendance, continuity and participation by members.
  • Ensure timely submission of ministry responses and action-taken reports.
  • Improve access to credible data and independent expert evidence.
  • Enhance transparency through timely publication of reports and permissible supporting material.
  • Strengthen the follow-up mechanism for recommendations that are accepted but not implemented.
  • Protect the non-partisan and deliberative character of committee examination.
  • Develop a more systematic parliamentary calendar for committee work.

23. Important Distinctions

Concepts Distinction
Standing vs ad hoc committeeStanding committees perform continuing or recurring functions; ad hoc committees are constituted for a specific task.
PAC vs Estimates CommitteePAC examines expenditure and audit findings; Estimates Committee examines estimates and suggests economy and efficiency.
PAC vs Committee on Public UndertakingsPAC focuses on public accounts and expenditure; the latter focuses on selected public undertakings.
DRSC vs JPCDRSCs are continuing departmental oversight committees; JPCs are constituted for specified matters or inquiries.
Select Committee vs Joint CommitteeA Select Committee is drawn from one House; a Joint Committee includes members of both Houses.
Committee report vs lawA report contains findings and recommendations; it does not itself become legislation.
Committee scrutiny vs judicial reviewCommittees exercise parliamentary oversight; courts exercise judicial powers under the Constitution and law.

24. Integrated Understanding

Parliamentary committees deepen representative democracy by converting the broad authority of the legislature into sustained and specialised scrutiny. They help Parliament examine public expenditure, legislative proposals, administrative performance and policy implementation.

The financial committees protect legislative control over public money. Department-related standing committees provide continuing oversight of ministries. Other committees safeguard privileges, ethics, petitions and procedural integrity, while select committees and JPCs examine particular legislative or investigative questions.

Their effectiveness depends on adequate time, reliable information, independent research support, cross-party deliberation and meaningful follow-up by the executive. A stronger committee system can improve legislative quality and accountability without displacing the authority of Parliament as a whole.
