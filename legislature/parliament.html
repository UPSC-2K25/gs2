1. Constitutional Position and Institutional Significance

Parliament is the Union legislature and a central institution of India's constitutional democracy. It represents the people, deliberates on public policy, makes laws, authorises public expenditure and holds the Union executive accountable.

Article 79 provides that Parliament consists of the President and two Houses: the Council of States (Rajya Sabha) and the House of the People (Lok Sabha). The President is an integral constitutional component of Parliament, although not a member of either House and not entitled to participate in their ordinary proceedings.

India follows a bicameral Union legislature. The two Houses have different compositions and certain distinct powers, but ordinarily participate jointly in the legislative process.

2. Constitutional Framework

Part V, Chapter II of the Constitution (Articles 79–122) deals with Parliament. Important provisions include:

  • Article 79: Constitution of Parliament.
  • Article 80: Composition of the Council of States.
  • Article 81: Composition of the House of the People.
  • Article 82: Readjustment of constituencies after each census, subject to constitutional provisions and applicable law.
  • Article 83: Duration of the Houses.
  • Article 84: Qualifications for membership.
  • Articles 85–87: Sessions, prorogation, dissolution and presidential addresses.
  • Articles 89–98: Presiding officers and Secretariat.
  • Articles 99–100: Oath, voting and quorum.
  • Articles 101–104: Vacation of seats, disqualifications and penalties.
  • Articles 105–106: Powers, privileges and salaries of members.
  • Articles 107–111: Legislative procedure and presidential assent.
  • Articles 112–117: Union financial procedure.
  • Articles 118–122: Rules, language, judicial non-interference in parliamentary proceedings and related matters.

3. Composition of the Rajya Sabha

Article 80 provides for a maximum strength of 250 members: up to 238 representatives of States and Union Territories, and 12 members nominated by the President.

  • Representatives of States are elected by the elected members of their Legislative Assemblies through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote.
  • Representation of States is not equal; it broadly reflects population, as allocated by the Constitution and law.
  • Representatives of Union Territories are chosen in the manner Parliament prescribes by law.
  • The President nominates 12 members with special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service.

The Rajya Sabha is a continuing House. It is not subject to dissolution, and approximately one-third of its members retire every second year. A member ordinarily serves a six-year term.

Constitutional significance: The Rajya Sabha provides continuity, represents the States at the Union level and offers an additional forum for legislative scrutiny and deliberation.

4. Composition of the Lok Sabha

Article 81 provides for the composition of the Lok Sabha through directly elected representatives of territorial constituencies. The constitutional framework provides for up to 530 representatives of States and up to 20 representatives of Union Territories, subject to the provisions governing representation.

  • Members representing States are directly elected by the people on the basis of adult suffrage.
  • Parliament regulates the representation of Union Territories by law.
  • The Constitution originally provided for nominated Anglo-Indian representation under Article 331. The special representation provision ceased to operate after the constitutional amendment that ended this arrangement.
  • The present constitutional framework does not provide for presidential nomination of members to the Lok Sabha.

The normal term of the Lok Sabha is five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved. While a National Emergency is in operation, Parliament may extend its term by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time, subject to the constitutional limit after the Emergency ends.

6. Membership: Qualifications and Disqualifications

Qualifications — Article 84

  • Must be a citizen of India.
  • Must make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation.
  • Must be at least 30 years old for the Rajya Sabha and 25 years old for the Lok Sabha.
  • Must possess other qualifications prescribed by Parliament by law.

Disqualifications — Article 102

  • Holding an office of profit under the Union or a State, subject to statutory exceptions.
  • Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
  • Being an undischarged insolvent.
  • Not being a citizen of India, voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign State, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
  • Any disqualification imposed by or under a law made by Parliament.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 prescribes additional grounds, including specified criminal convictions, corrupt practices and certain electoral offences.

Under Article 103, questions concerning disqualification under Article 102(1) are decided by the President, who must obtain and act according to the opinion of the Election Commission.

Questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule are decided by the Speaker or Chairman, subject to judicial review.

7. Vacation of Seats and Parliamentary Membership

Articles 101–104 regulate vacation of seats and related matters.

  • A person cannot simultaneously be a member of both Houses of Parliament or of Parliament and a State Legislature, subject to the applicable legal procedure.
  • A seat may become vacant if a member incurs a disqualification, resigns in the prescribed manner, or is absent without permission for 60 days of House sittings, subject to constitutional rules for calculating the period.
  • A resignation must be addressed to the Speaker or Chairman, as applicable. The presiding officer may refuse to accept it if satisfied that it is not voluntary or genuine.
  • Article 104 provides for a penalty for sitting or voting before taking the required oath or when not qualified or disqualified.

8. Presiding Officers of Parliament

Speaker of the Lok Sabha

Articles 93–97 deal with the Speaker and Deputy Speaker and related provisions.

  • The Lok Sabha elects its Speaker and Deputy Speaker from among its members.
  • The Speaker presides over sittings, maintains order, interprets and applies procedural rules, and regulates debates.
  • The Speaker decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110; the decision is constitutionally significant and is endorsed by the Speaker's certification.
  • The Speaker presides over a joint sitting of both Houses under Article 108.
  • The Speaker has a casting vote in the event of an equality of votes.
  • The Speaker's office continues after dissolution of the Lok Sabha until immediately before the first meeting of the newly elected Lok Sabha.

The Speaker may be removed by a resolution of the Lok Sabha passed by a majority of all the then members of the House, after at least 14 days' notice. The Speaker is entitled to speak and participate in proceedings on such a resolution but cannot preside over them.

Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

  • Under Article 89, the Vice-President of India is the ex officio Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
  • The Rajya Sabha elects a Deputy Chairman from among its members.
  • The Chairman presides over sittings and performs procedural and disciplinary functions.
  • The Chairman is not a member of the Rajya Sabha and ordinarily does not vote, but may exercise a casting vote in case of a tie.
  • The Chairman may be removed from office as Vice-President through the procedure prescribed by Article 67(b); the resolution must originate in the Rajya Sabha and be agreed to by the Lok Sabha.

Institutional importance: Presiding officers are expected to protect the rights of members, ensure orderly debate, safeguard minority participation and uphold the authority of the House. Their impartiality is important for parliamentary legitimacy.

9. Sessions, Sittings and Parliamentary Calendar

Article 85 empowers the President to summon each House from time to time. No more than six months may intervene between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next.

  • Summoning: Calling a House to meet.
  • Adjournment: Suspending a sitting or proceedings to a specified time.
  • Adjournment sine die: Ending a sitting without specifying the date of the next sitting.
  • Prorogation: Terminating a session of the House by the President.
  • Dissolution: Ending the life of the Lok Sabha. The Rajya Sabha is not dissolved.

Parliament traditionally meets in Budget, Monsoon and Winter sessions, although the Constitution does not prescribe these three session names or fixed dates.

Under Article 87, the President addresses both Houses assembled together at the commencement of the first session after each general election to the Lok Sabha and at the commencement of the first session of each year.

10. Quorum, Voting and Decision-Making

  • Article 100: Questions are ordinarily decided by a majority of members present and voting, unless the Constitution provides otherwise.
  • The presiding officer ordinarily does not vote in the first instance but exercises a casting vote in the event of equality.
  • The quorum to constitute a meeting of either House is one-tenth of the total number of members of the House.
  • If there is no quorum, the presiding officer must adjourn the House or suspend the meeting until there is a quorum.
  • Some constitutional decisions require special majorities or other prescribed voting thresholds.

Parliamentary decision-making combines majority rule with procedural protections, debate, committee scrutiny and constitutional limitations.

11. Legislative Powers and Functions

Parliament exercises legislative authority within the constitutional distribution of powers and subject to judicial review.

Ordinary Bills

  • An ordinary Bill may ordinarily be introduced in either House.
  • Both Houses must pass the Bill in the same form, subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • If the Houses disagree in specified circumstances, Article 108 permits a joint sitting, subject to its limitations.
  • After passage, the Bill is presented to the President under Article 111.

Money Bills

  • Article 110 defines a Money Bill by reference to specified financial matters, including taxation, borrowing, the Consolidated Fund and appropriation.
  • A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the President's recommendation.
  • The Speaker certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
  • The Rajya Sabha must return a Money Bill with recommendations within 14 days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations.
  • If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within 14 days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Lok Sabha.
  • A joint sitting is not available for a Money Bill.

Financial Bills

Financial Bills are not all Money Bills. Article 117 distinguishes financial Bills that contain matters specified in Article 110 from other financial legislation. Their introduction and passage are governed by different constitutional requirements. The classification of a Bill matters because it determines the role of the Rajya Sabha and the procedure for enactment.

Constitution Amendment Bills

Under Article 368, a Constitution Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House. Each House must pass it separately by the prescribed special majority. There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve a disagreement over a Constitution Amendment Bill. Ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures is also required for amendments affecting the specified federal provisions.

12. Financial Control over the Executive

Parliament's control over public finance is central to representative government. Article 112 requires the President to cause the Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the Union Budget, to be laid before both Houses.

  • Demand for Grants: Under Article 113, demands for grants are submitted to the Lok Sabha. The Lok Sabha may assent, refuse or reduce a demand.
  • Appropriation Bill: Article 114 requires legislative authorisation for withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Finance Bill: Gives effect to the government's taxation proposals, subject to constitutional procedures.
  • Vote on Account: Allows advance authorisation for expenditure for part of a financial year, pending completion of the full budget process.
  • Supplementary and additional grants: Article 115 provides for further legislative authorisation when the amount authorised is insufficient or a new service arises.
  • Excess grants: Article 115 also provides for regularisation where expenditure exceeds the amount previously authorised.
  • Charged expenditure: Certain expenditure is charged on the Consolidated Fund and is not submitted to vote, although it may be discussed.

The Rajya Sabha can discuss the Budget and other financial matters, but the Lok Sabha has the decisive role in voting on demands for grants.

13. Oversight and Accountability of the Executive

Parliament exercises continuous scrutiny over the government through debate, questions, motions, committees and financial oversight.

  • Question Hour: Enables members to seek information and explanations from ministers. Questions may be classified as starred, unstarred or short-notice under House rules.
  • Zero Hour: An Indian parliamentary practice through which members raise matters of urgent public importance. It is not expressly mentioned in the Constitution.
  • Calling Attention: Enables a member to draw a minister's attention to a matter of urgent public importance under applicable rules.
  • Adjournment Motion: Seeks to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance and involves interruption of normal business, subject to admissibility rules.
  • No-confidence Motion: Tests whether the Council of Ministers enjoys the confidence of the Lok Sabha.
  • Censure Motion: Expresses disapproval of a specified government policy or action.
  • Short-duration discussion: Provides an opportunity to discuss matters of public importance without necessarily putting a motion to vote.
  • Half-an-hour discussion: Allows further clarification on matters arising from answers to questions, subject to rules.

These instruments help convert the principle of collective responsibility under Article 75(3) into regular legislative scrutiny.

14. Parliamentary Committees

Committees enable detailed examination of legislation, expenditure, administration and policy. They help Parliament scrutinise complex matters beyond the time available in full House sittings.

Types of Committees

  • Standing Committees: Continuing committees constituted under parliamentary rules or other arrangements.
  • Ad hoc Committees: Formed for a specific purpose and generally cease to exist after submitting their report or completing the assigned task.

Important Financial Committees

  • Public Accounts Committee: Examines appropriation accounts, finance accounts and reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General relating to public expenditure.
  • Estimates Committee: Examines estimates and suggests economies, administrative improvements and alternatives in public expenditure.
  • Committee on Public Undertakings: Examines the reports and accounts of specified public sector undertakings and CAG reports concerning them.

Department-related Standing Committees

These committees examine the demands for grants of ministries and departments, Bills referred to them, annual reports and long-term policy documents. They provide specialised scrutiny and facilitate cross-party deliberation.

Other Important Committees

  • Committee on Privileges.
  • Committee on Petitions.
  • Committee on Subordinate Legislation.
  • Committee on Government Assurances.
  • Business Advisory Committee.
  • Rules Committee.
  • Committee on Ethics.

Committee reports are generally recommendatory, but they can influence legislation, public expenditure and executive conduct. Their effectiveness depends on timely referral, access to information, attendance and follow-up action.

15. Special Powers of the Rajya Sabha

Although the Lok Sabha has a predominant role in financial matters and executive accountability, the Rajya Sabha has special powers that reflect its federal and continuing character.

  • Article 249: If the Rajya Sabha passes a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest, Parliament may legislate on a matter in the State List. Such a resolution remains in force for a period not exceeding one year at a time and may be renewed.
  • Article 312: The Rajya Sabha may pass a resolution supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting declaring it necessary or expedient in the national interest to create one or more All India Services common to the Union and States. Parliament may then provide for their creation by law.
  • Vice-President's removal: Under Article 67(b), a resolution to remove the Vice-President must originate in the Rajya Sabha and be agreed to by the Lok Sabha.

These powers prevent the Rajya Sabha from being merely a revising chamber and give it a distinct role in India's federal structure.

16. Joint Sitting of Parliament

Article 108 provides for a joint sitting in specified situations of disagreement between the Houses over an ordinary Bill.

A joint sitting may be called when:

  • One House rejects a Bill passed by the other House.
  • The Houses finally disagree on amendments to a Bill.
  • More than six months elapse without the other House passing the Bill, excluding periods specified by the Constitution.

The President may notify the intention to summon a joint sitting, subject to the constitutional conditions. The Speaker of the Lok Sabha ordinarily presides. If the Speaker is absent, the Deputy Speaker or another person specified by Article 118 and the applicable rules presides.

A joint sitting is not available for:

  • Money Bills.
  • Constitution Amendment Bills.

Joint sittings are intended to resolve legislative deadlock, but they do not replace the ordinary requirement of bicameral deliberation.

17. Parliamentary Privileges

Article 105 provides for the powers, privileges and immunities of Parliament, its members and committees. Article 194 deals with corresponding privileges of State Legislatures.

  • Freedom of speech in Parliament, subject to the Constitution and rules and standing orders regulating parliamentary procedure.
  • Immunity from court proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament or a parliamentary committee, subject to constitutional limits.
  • Protection in relation to authorised publication of parliamentary proceedings and reports.
  • Other powers, privileges and immunities as defined by Parliament by law, and, until so defined, those recognised under the constitutional framework.

Privileges protect the independence and functioning of Parliament. They are not intended to place members above the law or to create a general exemption from ordinary criminal law.

Important judicial development: In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), the Supreme Court held that parliamentary privilege does not confer immunity from prosecution for bribery connected with a vote or speech in the legislature. The decision overruled the contrary position on this issue in P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998).

18. Parliamentary Control over Delegated Legislation

Parliament frequently authorises the executive to make rules, regulations, schemes and notifications under an enabling Act. This is known as delegated or subordinate legislation.

Delegation is necessary where technical detail, administrative flexibility or frequent adjustment is involved. However, essential legislative policy and standards must remain within the authority of the legislature.

Parliament exercises oversight through:

  • Laying requirements that require rules or regulations to be placed before one or both Houses.
  • Examination by the Committee on Subordinate Legislation.
  • Questions, debates and scrutiny of the parent legislation.
  • Judicial review where delegated legislation exceeds statutory authority or violates the Constitution.

19. Parliamentary Control over Constitutional and Institutional Processes

Parliament participates in several important constitutional processes beyond ordinary law-making.

  • Constitutional amendments: Exercises constituent power under Article 368, subject to the basic structure limitation and, where required, State ratification.
  • Impeachment of the President: Either House may initiate the process under Article 61, subject to the prescribed special majority and procedural requirements.
  • Removal of judges: Both Houses participate in the constitutional process under Articles 124 and 217, subject to the required special majorities and statutory procedure.
  • Removal of specified constitutional functionaries: Parliament has roles in procedures concerning the Comptroller and Auditor General, Chief Election Commissioner and other authorities under relevant constitutional provisions.
  • Emergency approval: Both Houses have constitutionally prescribed roles in approving and reviewing proclamations of National Emergency and President's Rule.
  • Creation of All India Services: Parliament may legislate under Article 312 after the prescribed Rajya Sabha resolution.

20. Parliamentary Sovereignty and Constitutional Supremacy

India does not follow the model of unlimited parliamentary sovereignty associated with the traditional British constitutional doctrine. Parliament exercises extensive legislative and constituent powers, but these powers operate within a written and supreme Constitution.

  • Legislative competence is distributed through Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule.
  • Fundamental Rights constrain ordinary legislation under Article 13.
  • Constitutional amendments are subject to Article 368 and the basic structure doctrine.
  • Judicial review enables courts to examine the constitutionality of legislative and executive action.
  • Federalism and constitutional procedures limit unilateral alteration of specified federal arrangements.

The Indian model combines representative legislative authority with constitutional supremacy, federalism, separation of powers and judicial review.

21. Anti-Defection Law and Parliamentary Functioning

The Tenth Schedule, introduced by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985 and modified by the 91st Amendment Act, 2003, provides for disqualification on grounds of defection.

  • A member may be disqualified for voluntarily giving up membership of the political party or voting or abstaining contrary to a party direction, without obtaining condonation within the prescribed period.
  • Independent members are disqualified if they join a political party after election.
  • Nominated members may join a political party within six months of taking their seat; joining after that period may attract disqualification.
  • The merger exception applies where at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger, subject to the Schedule.
  • The presiding officer decides disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.

The law seeks to promote political stability and prevent opportunistic defections. At the same time, concerns arise about legislative independence, party control and delays in deciding petitions.

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the basic validity of the anti-defection framework while recognising judicial review of the presiding officer's decision. In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Court emphasised the need for timely decisions and suggested that Parliament consider an independent mechanism for adjudication.

22. Parliamentary Functioning: Contemporary Challenges

  • Disruptions and loss of working time: Repeated interruptions reduce time available for legislation, debate and scrutiny.
  • Declining deliberative depth: Important Bills may receive limited debate or insufficient examination.
  • Weak committee scrutiny: Not all Bills are referred to committees, limiting detailed examination and stakeholder consultation.
  • Executive dominance: Strong party discipline and government control of legislative business can weaken independent scrutiny.
  • Anti-defection constraints: Party whips may restrict members' ability to express independent views beyond confidence and financial matters.
  • Ordinance route: Frequent or inadequately justified use of ordinances may weaken ordinary legislative deliberation.
  • Representation concerns: Gender, social, regional and other dimensions of representation remain important questions for parliamentary democracy.
  • Criminalisation and money power: Electoral integrity and the quality of political representation require continuous attention.
  • Legislative complexity: Increasingly technical laws require adequate research, consultation and expert scrutiny.
  • Accountability gaps: Limited follow-up on committee recommendations and audit findings can weaken oversight.

23. Reforms for a More Effective Parliament

  • Ensure adequate sitting days and a predictable parliamentary calendar.
  • Strengthen pre-legislative consultation and publish explanatory notes and impact assessments for major Bills.
  • Refer a larger proportion of significant Bills to parliamentary committees for detailed examination.
  • Improve access to independent research, technical expertise and legislative support for members.
  • Strengthen Question Hour, financial scrutiny and follow-up on committee recommendations.
  • Ensure timely and impartial decisions on disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule.
  • Review the scope of party whips to protect both government stability and legitimate legislative independence.
  • Improve transparency in legislative drafting, amendments, voting records and committee functioning, subject to legitimate confidentiality.
  • Promote constructive debate, civil conduct and consensus on major national and federal questions.
  • Strengthen the participation of women and under-represented communities in representative institutions.

25. Important Judicial Decisions

  • Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007): Parliamentary privileges and internal proceedings are not wholly immune from judicial review; constitutional illegality and substantive unconstitutionality may be examined.
  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): Upheld the anti-defection framework, subject to judicial review of decisions under the Tenth Schedule.
  • Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020): Emphasised timely adjudication of disqualification petitions and discussed the need for an independent adjudicatory mechanism.
  • Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd. (2019): Examined the constitutional limits and classification issues concerning Money Bills, reinforcing the importance of proper constitutional procedure.
  • Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024): Held that legislators cannot claim privilege-based immunity from prosecution for bribery connected with their legislative vote or speech.
  • In re: Article 370 (2023): Discussed constitutional processes and the role of representative institutions in the context of the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir.

26. Conceptual Distinctions for Revision

  • Adjournment vs Prorogation: Adjournment suspends a sitting; prorogation terminates a session.
  • Prorogation vs Dissolution: Prorogation ends a session; dissolution ends the Lok Sabha itself.
  • Money Bill vs Financial Bill: Every Money Bill is a financial Bill, but not every financial Bill is a Money Bill.
  • Ordinary Bill vs Constitution Amendment Bill: Ordinary Bills may be subject to a joint sitting in specified circumstances; Constitution Amendment Bills require separate passage by each House and cannot be resolved through a joint sitting.
  • Rajya Sabha vs Lok Sabha: Both participate in ordinary legislation, but the Lok Sabha has decisive powers over demands for grants and government confidence.
  • Parliamentary privilege vs personal immunity: Privileges protect legislative functioning; they do not create a general exemption from criminal law.
  • Question Hour vs Zero Hour: Question Hour is governed by formal rules; Zero Hour is a parliamentary practice rather than an express constitutional provision.
  • Committee scrutiny vs House debate: Committees provide detailed examination; the House retains the constitutional authority to decide legislative and financial matters.

27. Integrated Understanding

Parliament must be understood not merely as a law-making institution but as the central representative forum connecting popular sovereignty, federal representation, executive accountability and constitutional government.

The Lok Sabha expresses the democratic mandate through direct election and exercises decisive control over the Council of Ministers and public expenditure. The Rajya Sabha provides continuity, represents the States and possesses special powers under Articles 249 and 312. Parliamentary committees, privileges and procedural rules support effective scrutiny, while judicial review ensures that parliamentary authority remains within constitutional limits.

The effectiveness of Parliament therefore depends on more than formal constitutional powers. It also requires meaningful debate, adequate sitting time, informed members, effective committees, transparent procedures and respect for constitutional conventions.
