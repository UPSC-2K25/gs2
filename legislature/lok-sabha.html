1. Constitutional Position and Institutional Significance

The Lok Sabha, or House of the People, is the directly elected lower House of the Parliament of India. It is the principal institution through which citizens participate in national legislative representation and exercise democratic control over the Union executive.

India follows a parliamentary system in which the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. Consequently, the House has a central role not only in legislation but also in determining whether the government retains the confidence of the elected representatives.

The constitutional framework is primarily contained in Part V, Chapter II of the Constitution, particularly Articles 79–122. Its functioning is also governed by parliamentary rules, statutes, constitutional conventions and established practices.

2. Composition under Article 81

Article 81 provides for the composition of the House of the People.

  • Not more than 530 members are chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies in the States.
  • Not more than 20 members represent the Union Territories, in the manner provided by Parliament by law.
  • The constitutional maximum under Article 81 is therefore 550 elected members.

The former provision allowing the President to nominate up to two members of the Anglo-Indian community was discontinued by the Constitution (104th Amendment) Act, 2019. The present Lok Sabha has 543 elected seats.

Parliament may regulate representation of Union Territories by law. The allocation of seats and territorial constituencies must operate within the constitutional framework of population-based representation and delimitation.

3. Territorial Constituencies and Representation

Members representing States are elected from territorial constituencies. The Constitution seeks to ensure, as far as practicable, uniformity in the scale of representation among States and between constituencies within a State.

Article 81 links representation to population, subject to constitutional qualifications and the delimitation framework. The expression population is tied to the census specified by the Constitution for the relevant constitutional purpose.

Article 82 provides for readjustment of the allocation of seats and division of States into territorial constituencies after each census, subject to the constitutional provisions governing the timing and extent of readjustment.

The constitutional freeze on readjustment of the allocation of seats among States and the related delimitation arrangements must be read with the amendments to Articles 81, 82 and 170. The relevant provisions refer to the first census taken after the year 2026; the actual exercise depends on the constitutional and statutory process that follows publication of the relevant census figures.

Delimitation is intended to maintain representative equality, but it also raises questions concerning population control, federal balance and the distribution of political representation among States.

4. Reservation of Seats

Article 330 provides for reservation of seats in the Lok Sabha for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes. The allocation is based on the constitutional criteria relating to their population and representation.

  • Reserved constituencies enable representation of historically disadvantaged communities.
  • Reserved seats do not create separate electorates; voters in the constituency participate in the election under the applicable electoral framework.
  • Article 334 governs the duration of reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.

The 104th Constitutional Amendment extended SC and ST political reservation for a further period of ten years from the relevant constitutional commencement date, taking the extension to 2030. It also discontinued the special representation of the Anglo-Indian community by nomination.

The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 provides for reservation of approximately one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies, including within the SC/ST reserved-seat framework. Its commencement for operational purposes is linked to the constitutionally prescribed census and subsequent delimitation exercise. It should not be treated as an already implemented reservation in the present composition of the House.

5. Election of Members

Lok Sabha members are elected through direct elections based on universal adult suffrage under Article 326.

  • Citizens who satisfy the constitutional and statutory conditions are entitled to be registered as electors.
  • The ordinary electoral method is the first-past-the-post system: the candidate securing the highest number of valid votes in a constituency is elected.
  • The election process is governed principally by the Representation of the People Act, 1950, the Representation of the People Act, 1951, and rules made under them.
  • The Election Commission of India supervises, directs and controls elections under Article 324.

Direct territorial representation connects each elected member to a defined constituency. However, the first-past-the-post system does not guarantee that a party's share of seats will correspond proportionately to its share of votes across the country.

6. Qualifications for Membership

Article 84 prescribes the constitutional qualifications for membership of Parliament.

  • The person must be a citizen of India.
  • The person must make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation before an authorised person.
  • The minimum age for membership of the Lok Sabha is 25 years.
  • The person must possess such other qualifications as may be prescribed by or under a law made by Parliament.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 provides additional qualifications and requirements, including those relating to electoral registration and nomination.

Qualification for membership must be distinguished from eligibility to vote. The Constitution prescribes a higher minimum age for contesting a Lok Sabha election than for registration as an elector.

7. Disqualifications and Vacation of Seats

Article 102 identifies constitutional grounds for disqualification from membership of either House of Parliament.

  • Holding an office of profit under the Union or a State, subject to constitutional exceptions.
  • Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
  • Being an undischarged insolvent.
  • Not being a citizen of India, voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign State, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
  • Any disqualification imposed by or under a law made by Parliament.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 provides statutory grounds, including specified criminal convictions, corrupt practices and other electoral disqualifications.

The Tenth Schedule separately provides for disqualification on grounds of defection. Questions concerning disqualification under Article 102(1) are decided by the President in accordance with Article 103, after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission. Decisions under the Tenth Schedule are made by the presiding officer, subject to judicial review.

Article 101 deals with vacation of seats, including circumstances involving dual membership, resignation and prolonged absence without permission.

8. Duration and Dissolution

Article 83(2) provides that the Lok Sabha continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved. The expiry of the five-year period itself operates as dissolution.

During a Proclamation of Emergency, Parliament may extend the term of the Lok Sabha by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time. Such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Proclamation has ceased to operate.

The President may dissolve the Lok Sabha under the constitutional parliamentary framework. In ordinary circumstances, dissolution takes place on the advice of the Council of Ministers, while constitutional conventions and judicial principles become relevant in exceptional situations involving government formation or loss of confidence.

Dissolution ends the life of the House and affects pending legislative business. The fate of pending Bills depends on the constitutional rules governing the particular House and the stage reached by the Bill.

9. Presiding Officer: Speaker and Deputy Speaker

Article 93 requires the Lok Sabha to choose two members as Speaker and Deputy Speaker as soon as may be. Their offices are central to the orderly and impartial conduct of House proceedings.

The Speaker:

  • Presides over sittings and maintains order and decorum.
  • Interprets and applies the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business.
  • Decides questions relating to the admissibility of motions, resolutions and amendments.
  • Certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110.
  • Exercises powers relating to parliamentary committees and the business of the House.
  • Decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.

Under Article 94, the Speaker and Deputy Speaker vacate office if they cease to be members, may resign, and may be removed by a resolution of the Lok Sabha passed by a majority of all the then members of the House. At least fourteen days' notice is required for such a resolution.

Article 96 provides that the Speaker or Deputy Speaker cannot preside while a resolution for their removal is under consideration, although the Speaker may participate and vote in the first instance.

Article 100 provides for voting in the House and the presiding officer's casting vote in the event of an equality of votes. The Speaker ordinarily does not vote in the first instance.

10. Legislative Powers and Functions

The Lok Sabha participates in law-making on matters within Parliament's legislative competence. Its legislative role is exercised with the Rajya Sabha, subject to the special procedures and powers assigned to each House.

  • Ordinary Bills may generally be introduced and passed in either House.
  • A Bill ordinarily becomes law only after passage by both Houses and presidential assent.
  • In the event of a deadlock over an ordinary Bill, Article 108 provides for a joint sitting in specified circumstances.
  • Money Bills can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha, subject to the constitutional procedure in Articles 109 and 110.
  • Constitutional Amendment Bills must be passed separately by each House under Article 368; there is no joint sitting for resolving disagreement over such a Bill.

Legislative competence is governed by Articles 245–246 and the Seventh Schedule, along with the other relevant constitutional provisions. The Lok Sabha cannot legislate beyond Parliament's constitutional authority.

11. Financial Powers and Control over Public Expenditure

The Lok Sabha has a particularly significant position in financial matters. Parliamentary control over taxation, public expenditure and the Consolidated Fund of India is a central feature of responsible government.

  • Under Article 109, a Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha and only on the President's recommendation.
  • The Rajya Sabha may make recommendations on a Money Bill but must return it within fourteen days. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject those recommendations.
  • Under Article 113, demands for grants are submitted to the Lok Sabha. The House may assent to, refuse or reduce a demand, subject to constitutional procedure.
  • Appropriation legislation under Article 114 authorises withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Article 117 governs Financial Bills and imposes distinct requirements depending on their nature.

The Lok Sabha's financial authority is not unlimited. Constitutional requirements concerning the President's recommendation, parliamentary authorisation and the Consolidated Fund must be followed.

Financial scrutiny is supported by parliamentary committees, especially the Public Accounts Committee, Estimates Committee and Committee on Public Undertakings.

12. Control over the Council of Ministers

Article 75(3) provides that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This is the foundation of the House's political control over the Union executive.

The Lok Sabha may hold the government accountable through:

  • Questions and supplementary questions.
  • Question Hour and other opportunities for seeking information.
  • Zero Hour and matters raised by members under House procedures.
  • Adjournment motions and other instruments of parliamentary discussion.
  • Calling attention, short-duration discussions and debates.
  • Cut motions during consideration of demands for grants.
  • No-confidence motions and confidence motions.
  • Parliamentary committee examination of government action and expenditure.

A no-confidence motion, if adopted, indicates that the Council of Ministers no longer enjoys the confidence of the House and must ordinarily resign. The Rajya Sabha does not exercise an equivalent power to dismiss the Union Council of Ministers.

13. Electoral and Constituent Functions

The Lok Sabha participates in several constitutional processes beyond ordinary legislation.

  • It participates with the Rajya Sabha and elected members of State Legislative Assemblies in the election of the President under Article 54.
  • It participates with the Rajya Sabha in the election of the Vice-President under Article 66.
  • It participates in the impeachment process of the President under Article 61.
  • It participates in the removal process for constitutional functionaries where the Constitution prescribes parliamentary action by both Houses.
  • It participates in constitutional amendments under Article 368, with each House required to pass the Bill separately by the prescribed special majority.

The Lok Sabha alone cannot complete processes for which the Constitution requires approval by both Houses or by other constitutional bodies.

14. Special Position in Relation to the Rajya Sabha

Although the two Houses are generally co-equal in ordinary legislative matters, the Lok Sabha enjoys specific advantages in the parliamentary system.

  • The Union Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3).
  • Money Bills can originate only in the Lok Sabha.
  • The Lok Sabha has the decisive role in approving demands for grants.
  • It can remove the government through a no-confidence motion.
  • In a joint sitting under Article 108, the numerical strength of the Lok Sabha generally gives it an advantage, although the procedure is constitutionally defined and does not apply to every category of Bill.

The Rajya Sabha, however, has its own special powers under Articles 249 and 312. It can authorise Parliament to legislate on a State List matter in the national interest and can authorise the creation of one or more All India Services. These powers reflect the federal role of the Council of States.

15. Parliamentary Privileges and Immunities

Article 105 protects the freedom of speech in Parliament, subject to the Constitution and the rules and standing orders regulating parliamentary proceedings.

Members enjoy immunity from court proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament or a committee thereof, within the constitutional scope of the provision.

Other privileges are determined by the Constitution, laws and the law of parliamentary privilege. These protections exist to secure the independent functioning of Parliament, not to place members above the law in matters unrelated to their parliamentary functions.

The Supreme Court's decision in Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024) clarified that legislative privilege does not confer immunity from prosecution for bribery connected with a vote or speech in the House.

16. Parliamentary Committees and Legislative Scrutiny

Parliamentary committees enable detailed examination of legislation, expenditure, administration and public policy. They help overcome the limitations of floor proceedings, including limited time and the volume of government business.

Important committees associated with Lok Sabha functioning include:

  • Public Accounts Committee: examines appropriation accounts and reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General.
  • Estimates Committee: examines estimates and suggests improvements in economy, efficiency and administrative organisation.
  • Committee on Public Undertakings: examines the functioning and accounts of selected public sector undertakings.
  • Department-related Standing Committees: examine demands for grants, Bills, annual reports and policy documents of ministries and departments.
  • Committee on Petitions, Committee of Privileges and other subject-specific committees: perform specialised scrutiny functions.

Committee recommendations generally have persuasive rather than legally binding force. Their effectiveness depends on the quality of scrutiny, access to information, attendance, follow-up and the government's response.

17. Limitations and Challenges in Lok Sabha Functioning

  • Frequent disruptions and adjournments reduce the time available for deliberation and scrutiny.
  • Declining legislative scrutiny can weaken the quality of law-making.
  • Rushed passage of Bills may limit consultation with stakeholders and committee examination.
  • Party discipline and the anti-defection framework may constrain independent deliberation by members.
  • Executive dominance over the legislative agenda can weaken Parliament's oversight role.
  • Limited time for private members' business restricts opportunities for non-government legislative initiatives.
  • Unequal representation of women and other groups remains a concern, notwithstanding constitutional reservation measures.
  • Criminalisation of politics, money power and opaque political finance can affect representative accountability.
  • Insufficient research support and technical expertise can affect members' ability to scrutinise complex legislation and budgets.

18. Reforms for a More Effective Lok Sabha

  • Ensure a predictable parliamentary calendar and adequate sitting days.
  • Strengthen committee scrutiny of important Bills and major public expenditure.
  • Provide adequate time for debate and meaningful participation by members across parties.
  • Improve research, legislative drafting and non-partisan technical support for members.
  • Strengthen transparency in legislative processes, committee work and government responses.
  • Improve the quality and accessibility of parliamentary data and digital records.
  • Encourage greater representation and participation of women and marginalised communities.
  • Promote constructive conduct while protecting the rights of opposition members to question and scrutinise government action.
  • Review the operation of anti-defection provisions to balance government stability with legislative deliberation.

Reform should preserve the central principle of parliamentary democracy: an effective government must be matched by an effective legislature capable of deliberation, accountability and representation.

20. Important Distinctions

  • Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha: The Lok Sabha represents the people directly; the Rajya Sabha represents the States and Union Territories through its constitutional electoral arrangements.
  • Ordinary Bill and Money Bill: An ordinary Bill may originate in either House; a Money Bill can originate only in the Lok Sabha.
  • Confidence and no-confidence motions: A confidence motion tests whether the government commands support; a no-confidence motion seeks to establish that it does not.
  • Prorogation and dissolution: Prorogation terminates a session; dissolution ends the life of the Lok Sabha.
  • Qualification and disqualification: Qualification determines eligibility to become a member; disqualification removes or prevents membership on specified grounds.
  • Reservation and nomination: Reserved constituencies are filled through elections under the relevant rules; nomination is a distinct constitutional method of selecting members.

21. Integrated Understanding

The Lok Sabha combines three essential democratic functions:

  • Representation: it provides a direct institutional link between citizens and the Union legislature.
  • Legislation and finance: it participates in law-making and exercises a decisive role in taxation, grants and public expenditure.
  • Executive accountability: it determines whether the Council of Ministers continues to enjoy the confidence of the elected House.

Its effectiveness depends not merely on constitutional powers but also on the quality of debate, committee scrutiny, opposition participation, responsible party conduct and public accountability. A strong Lok Sabha is therefore indispensable to representative democracy and constitutional government.
