1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

The budget is the government's annual financial statement setting out its estimated receipts and expenditure for a financial year. It translates public policy priorities into resource allocation and provides the legislature with an instrument to scrutinise executive financial decisions.

In India, the budget is not merely an accounting exercise. It is connected with legislative control over taxation, expenditure, borrowing, public debt and the Consolidated Fund of India.

The constitutional framework seeks to ensure that public money is raised and spent only through legally authorised procedures, subject to legislative scrutiny and audit.

2. Constitutional Framework

The principal constitutional provisions governing Union financial administration include:

  • Article 112: Annual Financial Statement.
  • Article 113: Procedure in Parliament with respect to estimates.
  • Article 114: Appropriation Bills.
  • Article 115: Supplementary, additional or excess grants.
  • Article 116: Votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants.
  • Article 117: Special provisions relating to Financial Bills.
  • Article 110: Definition of Money Bills.
  • Article 109: Special procedure for Money Bills.
  • Article 266: Consolidated Funds and Public Accounts.
  • Article 267: Contingency Fund.
  • Article 148: Comptroller and Auditor-General of India.
  • Article 151: Audit reports.

For States, Articles 202–207 provide the corresponding framework for annual financial statements, grants, appropriation and financial legislation.

3. The Three Public Funds

3.1 Consolidated Fund of India

Article 266(1) provides for the Consolidated Fund of India. It includes:

  • All revenues received by the Union Government.
  • Loans raised by the government through borrowing.
  • Repayments of loans advanced by the government.

No money can be withdrawn from the Consolidated Fund except under appropriation made by law in accordance with the Constitution.

3.2 Public Account of India

Under Article 266(2), other public money received by or on behalf of the government is credited to the Public Account. It includes provident funds, small savings and certain deposits.

These amounts are generally held by the government in a trustee or banker-like capacity. Their withdrawal does not follow the same parliamentary appropriation procedure as expenditure from the Consolidated Fund.

3.3 Contingency Fund of India

Article 267 provides for a Contingency Fund to enable advances for unforeseen expenditure pending legislative authorisation.

The Union Contingency Fund is placed at the disposal of the President. Expenditure advanced from it is subsequently recouped through the appropriate parliamentary authorisation.

4. Annual Financial Statement

Article 112 requires the President to cause an Annual Financial Statement to be laid before both Houses of Parliament for every financial year.

It presents estimated receipts and expenditure of the government and distinguishes expenditure charged upon the Consolidated Fund from other expenditure.

The distinction is important because charged expenditure is not submitted to the vote of Parliament, although it may be discussed.

Examples of charged expenditure include:

  • Emoluments and allowances of the President.
  • Salary and allowances of the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha.
  • Salary and allowances of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha.
  • Debt charges for which the Union Government is liable.
  • Salaries, allowances and pensions of Supreme Court judges.
  • Salary and allowances of the Comptroller and Auditor-General.
  • Amounts required to satisfy court judgments, decrees or awards against the Union.
  • Other expenditure declared by the Constitution or Parliament to be so charged.

5. Preparation of the Union Budget

Budget preparation is an executive process involving the Ministry of Finance, other ministries and departments. The Department of Economic Affairs and Department of Expenditure play important roles in coordinating the process.

  1. Budget circular and instructions: Ministries receive instructions for preparing estimates of receipts and expenditure.
  2. Departmental estimates: Ministries assess ongoing commitments, new schemes, programme requirements and likely expenditure.
  3. Revenue estimates: Expected tax and non-tax receipts are assessed.
  4. Scrutiny and consultations: The Ministry of Finance examines proposals and consults relevant departments and stakeholders.
  5. Resource allocation: Available fiscal resources are matched with competing expenditure priorities.
  6. Cabinet approval: The final budget proposals are considered and approved through the executive process before presentation.
  7. Presentation: The Finance Minister presents the budget in Parliament.

The precise internal timetable and consultation process may vary. The constitutional requirement is the presentation of the Annual Financial Statement and observance of the prescribed legislative procedures.

6. Presentation and General Discussion

The Union Budget is presented by the Finance Minister in the Lok Sabha. The Annual Financial Statement is laid before both Houses.

The general discussion provides members an opportunity to examine the government's economic and fiscal policy, revenue assumptions, expenditure priorities and development strategy.

At this stage, the House discusses the budget as a whole. Detailed voting on individual demands for grants takes place later in the Lok Sabha.

The Rajya Sabha may participate in the general discussion, but it does not vote on demands for grants.

7. Demands for Grants

Under Article 113, expenditure estimates other than charged expenditure are submitted to the Lok Sabha in the form of demands for grants.

  • Each demand ordinarily relates to a ministry or department.
  • Demands are presented for the consideration of the Lok Sabha.
  • Only the Lok Sabha can vote on demands for grants.
  • Charged expenditure is not submitted to voting, though it can be discussed.
  • Expenditure cannot be incurred from the Consolidated Fund merely because it appears in the budget estimates.

The constitutional arrangement reinforces the principle that the directly elected House exercises primary control over public expenditure.

8. Cut Motions

A cut motion is a parliamentary device through which a member seeks to reduce the amount of a demand for grant. It enables members to scrutinise government expenditure and express disagreement with policy or administration.

8.1 Policy Cut

A policy cut proposes reduction of the demand to Re 1. It indicates disapproval of the policy underlying the demand.

8.2 Economy Cut

An economy cut proposes reduction by a specified amount. It seeks savings or challenges particular items of expenditure.

8.3 Token Cut

A token cut proposes reduction by Rs 100. It is generally used to raise a specific grievance within the government's responsibility.

Cut motions are subject to admissibility rules and the Speaker's decision. Their passage would have serious political implications because the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.

9. Guillotine

Parliament has limited time to scrutinise all demands for grants. When the allocated discussion period ends, the Speaker puts the remaining demands to vote without further detailed discussion. This procedure is known as the guillotine.

It enables the budget process to be completed within the parliamentary calendar, but it can reduce the opportunity for detailed legislative scrutiny of expenditure.

10. Appropriation Bill

Article 114 provides for an Appropriation Bill after demands for grants have been voted upon by the Lok Sabha.

The Bill seeks legislative authorisation for withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund of India to meet:

  • Grants voted by the Lok Sabha.
  • Expenditure charged upon the Consolidated Fund.

No amendment may be proposed to an Appropriation Bill that would vary the amount of a grant made or alter its destination, or vary the amount of charged expenditure. The Speaker's decision on the admissibility of an amendment is final under Article 114.

Once the Appropriation Act is enacted, the executive receives legal authority to withdraw the specified amounts from the Consolidated Fund.

11. Finance Bill and Taxation Proposals

The Finance Bill gives legislative effect to the government's taxation proposals and other financial measures requiring legislation.

A Finance Bill may qualify as a Money Bill under Article 110 if it contains only matters specified in that Article and matters incidental to them. However, not every Bill described as a Finance Bill is necessarily a Money Bill.

Article 117 distinguishes different categories of Financial Bills and prescribes special requirements concerning presidential recommendation and the legislative procedure.

The constitutional classification of a Bill depends on its contents and the relevant provisions, not merely on its title.

12. Role of the Rajya Sabha

The Rajya Sabha participates in the general discussion on the budget but does not vote on demands for grants.

An Appropriation Bill and a Money Bill are subject to Article 109. The Rajya Sabha may make recommendations within fourteen days, but the Lok Sabha may accept or reject them.

For Financial Bills that are not Money Bills, the ordinary bicameral legislative procedure may apply, subject to the specific requirements of Article 117.

Thus, the financial powers of the two Houses are not identical. The Constitution gives the Lok Sabha a predominant position in relation to public expenditure and Money Bills.

13. Vote on Account, Vote of Credit and Exceptional Grant

13.1 Vote on Account

Article 116 permits the Lok Sabha to authorise an advance grant for a part of the financial year pending completion of the full budget process.

It ensures continuity of government expenditure when the regular budget has not yet been fully approved.

13.2 Vote of Credit

A vote of credit may be granted for meeting an unexpected demand upon the resources of India where the scale or indefinite character of the demand makes ordinary details difficult to specify.

13.3 Exceptional Grant

An exceptional grant relates to a special purpose that does not form part of the current service of any financial year.

14. Supplementary, Additional and Excess Grants

Article 115 provides mechanisms for obtaining further legislative authorisation when the original budgetary provision is insufficient or expenditure requirements arise beyond the original estimates.

  • Supplementary grant: Required when the amount authorised for a service is insufficient for the current financial year.
  • Additional grant: Required when expenditure arises on a new service not contemplated in the original budget.
  • Excess grant: Required when expenditure on a service exceeds the amount originally authorised.

Excess grants are examined by the Public Accounts Committee before being submitted to the Lok Sabha for regularisation.

15. Parliamentary Committees and Budget Scrutiny

15.1 Departmentally Related Standing Committees

Departmentally Related Standing Committees examine demands for grants of the ministries and departments allocated to them.

Their examination may consider:

  • Budgetary allocations and expenditure priorities.
  • Progress of schemes and programmes.
  • Implementation capacity and administrative constraints.
  • Utilisation of funds and performance outcomes.
  • Need for improved allocation or expenditure efficiency.

Their reports assist Parliament in informed scrutiny. However, the reports are recommendatory and do not replace the formal voting procedure in the Lok Sabha.

15.2 Public Accounts Committee

The Public Accounts Committee examines government expenditure after it has occurred, with particular reference to appropriation accounts, finance accounts and reports of the CAG.

15.3 Estimates Committee

The Estimates Committee examines estimates and suggests improvements in economy, efficiency and administrative organisation.

15.4 Committee on Public Undertakings

The Committee on Public Undertakings examines the accounts, reports and performance of specified public sector undertakings, within its prescribed mandate.

16. Role of the Comptroller and Auditor-General

The CAG is a constitutional authority under Article 148. The CAG audits public expenditure and reports on the financial accountability of the Union and States.

The CAG's reports are submitted to the President or Governor, as applicable, and are laid before the appropriate legislature under Article 151.

The audit process supports legislative oversight by identifying:

  • Expenditure without proper authorisation.
  • Irregular or excessive expenditure.
  • Weaknesses in financial management.
  • Deficiencies in programme implementation.
  • Issues relating to economy, efficiency and effectiveness.

The CAG does not itself impose legislative sanctions or decide political responsibility. Its audit findings inform the legislature and its committees.

17. Budgetary Process in the States

Article 202 requires the Governor to cause an Annual Financial Statement to be laid before the State Legislature.

The broad stages resemble the Union process:

  1. Preparation of estimates by State departments.
  2. Consolidation and scrutiny by the Finance Department.
  3. Approval through the State executive process.
  4. Presentation of the Annual Financial Statement.
  5. General discussion in the Legislature.
  6. Consideration and voting on demands for grants by the Legislative Assembly.
  7. Enactment of the Appropriation Act.
  8. Legislation relating to taxation and other financial proposals.
  9. Audit and legislative scrutiny of expenditure.

Under Article 203, demands for grants are submitted to the Legislative Assembly. The Legislative Council, where one exists, does not vote on demands for grants.

Articles 204–207 provide the corresponding provisions relating to appropriation and financial legislation.

18. Gender Budgeting and Outcome Budgeting

18.1 Gender Budgeting

Gender budgeting examines how public revenue and expenditure affect women and men differently. It seeks to integrate gender considerations into policy design, resource allocation and evaluation.

It is a budgetary approach rather than a separate constitutional fund or independent constitutional procedure.

18.2 Outcome Budgeting

Outcome budgeting links financial allocations with measurable outputs and outcomes. It shifts attention from the amount spent to the results achieved.

18.3 Performance Budgeting

Performance budgeting connects expenditure with activities and measurable performance indicators, strengthening the relationship between resources and administrative results.

19. Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management

Fiscal responsibility frameworks seek to promote prudent public finance through discipline in borrowing, deficits and debt management.

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003 provides a statutory framework for fiscal management at the Union level.

Its objectives include:

  • Promoting fiscal discipline.
  • Improving transparency in fiscal operations.
  • Reducing excessive borrowing and unsustainable deficits.
  • Strengthening medium-term fiscal planning.
  • Improving intergenerational equity in public finance.

Fiscal targets and escape provisions have evolved through amendments and policy decisions. Their implementation must balance fiscal sustainability with economic conditions and developmental needs.

20. Transparency and Public Participation

Budget transparency enables citizens, legislators and civil society to understand how public resources are raised, allocated and spent.

Important elements include:

  • Publication of budget documents and fiscal statements.
  • Disclosure of expenditure and scheme performance.
  • Accessible information on subsidies and tax expenditures.
  • Social audits and community monitoring where applicable.
  • Use of digital platforms for tracking public expenditure.
  • Legislative scrutiny and independent audit.

The Right to Information framework and public financial management systems can strengthen accountability, but disclosure must be accompanied by effective scrutiny and corrective action.

21. Challenges in the Budgetary Process

  • Limited legislative scrutiny: Time constraints can restrict detailed examination of demands for grants.
  • Guillotine procedure: Large portions of expenditure may be approved without individual discussion.
  • Weak committee follow-up: Recommendations may not always translate into corrective executive action.
  • Unrealistic estimates: Poor revenue or expenditure projections can undermine fiscal planning.
  • Underutilisation of allocations: Administrative delays and weak implementation capacity can prevent intended outcomes.
  • Supplementary demands: Repeated additional authorisations may indicate weaknesses in initial budgeting.
  • Off-budget liabilities: Certain fiscal obligations may reduce transparency if not adequately disclosed.
  • Outcome measurement: Spending data may not adequately reflect service quality or social impact.
  • Centre-State coordination: Fiscal transfers, centrally sponsored schemes and State priorities require effective coordination.

22. Reforms for a More Accountable Budget Process

  • Provide adequate time for legislative scrutiny of budget proposals.
  • Strengthen the research and technical capacity of parliamentary committees.
  • Improve follow-up on committee and CAG recommendations.
  • Publish accessible information on allocations, releases and actual expenditure.
  • Improve forecasting and reduce persistent deviations between estimates and actuals.
  • Strengthen outcome and performance measurement.
  • Disclose contingent liabilities and off-budget obligations transparently.
  • Improve coordination between Union, State and local government finances.
  • Use technology to track expenditure and improve public access to fiscal information.
  • Balance fiscal discipline with inclusive development and counter-cyclical policy needs.

24. Important Distinctions

Concepts Distinction
Charged expenditure and voted expenditure Charged expenditure is not voted upon; other expenditure is submitted as demands for grants.
Appropriation Bill and Finance Bill The former authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund; the latter gives effect to taxation and other financial proposals.
Vote on account and interim budget A vote on account is a constitutional mechanism for temporary expenditure authorisation; an interim budget is a political and budgetary presentation that may include broader estimates and proposals.
Supplementary and excess grant A supplementary grant addresses insufficiency in authorised provision; an excess grant regularises expenditure already incurred beyond the authorised amount.
Consolidated Fund and Public Account The Consolidated Fund contains government revenues, borrowings and loan recoveries; the Public Account contains other public money held by the government.
Demand for grant and appropriation A demand for grant is considered by the Lok Sabha; appropriation provides the legal authority for withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund.
Money Bill and Financial Bill A Money Bill must satisfy Article 110; Financial Bills are governed by Article 117 and may have a different legislative procedure.

25. Integrated Constitutional Understanding

The budgetary process connects executive policy-making with legislative authorisation and post-expenditure accountability.

  1. The executive prepares estimates and formulates expenditure and revenue proposals.
  2. The Annual Financial Statement communicates the government's estimated financial position.
  3. The Lok Sabha examines and votes on demands for grants.
  4. Appropriation legislation authorises withdrawals from the Consolidated Fund.
  5. Financial legislation gives effect to applicable taxation proposals.
  6. The CAG audits public expenditure and reports to the appropriate legislature.
  7. Parliamentary committees examine audit findings and government financial management.

The effectiveness of this system depends not only on constitutional rules but also on the quality of legislative scrutiny, transparency, audit follow-up and administrative capacity.
