1. Meaning and Constitutional Foundation

Legislative procedure refers to the constitutional and parliamentary rules through which a legislature conducts its business, considers proposals, deliberates, votes and enacts laws.

In India, the legislative process is governed by the Constitution, rules of procedure of each House, parliamentary conventions and established practices. It gives institutional expression to representative democracy, deliberation, accountability and constitutional limitations on law-making.

Important constitutional provisions include Articles 107–111, 118–122, 196–201 and 208–212.

2. Types of Bills

2.1 Ordinary Bills

An Ordinary Bill deals with matters other than those subject to the special constitutional procedures applicable to Money Bills and certain Financial Bills. It may generally be introduced in either House of Parliament, subject to constitutional requirements.

2.2 Money Bills

Article 110 defines a Money Bill. It must contain only provisions dealing with matters specified in Article 110(1), or matters incidental to those specified matters.

These matters include taxation, government borrowing, custody and withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund, appropriation, charged expenditure and specified public financial matters.

The Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110(3). The Speaker's certificate is endorsed when the Bill is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha and presented to the President under Article 110(4).

2.3 Financial Bills

Article 117 deals with Financial Bills. They are not all Money Bills. Their classification depends on their provisions and the constitutional requirements applicable to them.

  • Article 117(1): A Bill containing matters specified in Article 110 along with other matters. It cannot be introduced in the Rajya Sabha and requires the President's recommendation.
  • Article 117(3): A Bill involving expenditure from the Consolidated Fund of India cannot be passed by either House unless the President has recommended its consideration.

2.4 Constitutional Amendment Bills

Constitutional Amendment Bills are governed by Article 368. They require the prescribed special majority in each House and, for amendments affecting specified federal provisions, ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

There is no provision for a joint sitting to resolve disagreement over a Constitutional Amendment Bill.

3. Introduction of a Bill

The formal introduction of a Bill is known as its First Reading. A Bill may be introduced by a Minister or by a private member.

  • Government Bill: Introduced by a Minister on behalf of the government.
  • Private Member's Bill: Introduced by a member who is not a Minister.

Government Bills generally reflect executive policy priorities. Private Members' Bills provide individual legislators an opportunity to propose legislative changes independently of the government.

Ordinary Bills may generally originate in either House. Money Bills can originate only in the Lok Sabha. Constitutional Amendment Bills may be introduced in either House.

4. Stages of an Ordinary Bill

4.1 First Reading

The Bill is introduced in the House. The introduction is ordinarily followed by publication in the official Gazette, though prior publication may also take place.

4.2 Second Reading

The Second Reading involves consideration of the principles and detailed provisions of the Bill. It is commonly divided into two stages:

  • General discussion: The House discusses the broad objectives and principles of the Bill.
  • Detailed consideration: The Bill may be considered clause by clause, referred to a committee, or otherwise examined according to the applicable rules and decision of the House.

Committee scrutiny can improve legislative quality by enabling detailed examination, expert input and stakeholder consultation.

4.3 Third Reading

At the Third Reading, the House considers whether the Bill should be passed. Debate is generally limited to arguments for or against the Bill as a whole, subject to the applicable rules.

Once passed, the Bill is transmitted to the other House, where the legislative process is repeated.

5. Passage in the Second House

Under Article 107, an Ordinary Bill must ordinarily be agreed to by both Houses of Parliament before it can be presented to the President.

The second House may:

  • Pass the Bill without amendments.
  • Pass it with amendments, which must be agreed to by the originating House.
  • Reject the Bill.
  • Take no action for more than six months, subject to the constitutional rules for calculating the period.

Where the Houses disagree in circumstances specified under Article 108, the President may summon a joint sitting, subject to the constitutional exceptions.

6. Joint Sitting of Parliament

Article 108 provides for a joint sitting to resolve certain deadlocks between the Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha over Ordinary Bills and certain Financial Bills.

A joint sitting may be considered where:

  • The other House rejects a Bill passed by the first House.
  • The Houses finally disagree on amendments to a Bill.
  • More than six months elapse without the other House passing the Bill, subject to the constitutional method of computing the period.

The President may notify the intention to summon a joint sitting, after which neither House proceeds further with the Bill independently.

The joint sitting is presided over by the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, or in the prescribed order by the Deputy Speaker or Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha, or another member determined under the applicable rules.

The Bill is decided by a majority of the total number of members of both Houses present and voting.

6.1 Bills Excluded from Joint Sitting

  • Money Bills follow the special procedure under Article 109.
  • Constitutional Amendment Bills under Article 368 cannot be resolved through a joint sitting.

A joint sitting has been used on three occasions: the Dowry Prohibition Bill, 1961; the Banking Service Commission (Repeal) Bill, 1978; and the Prevention of Terrorism Bill, 2002.

7. Special Procedure for Money Bills

Articles 109 and 110 establish a special legislative procedure for Money Bills.

  1. A Money Bill can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
  2. It requires the President's recommendation before introduction.
  3. After passage by the Lok Sabha, it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha for recommendations.
  4. The Rajya Sabha must return the Bill within fourteen days.
  5. The Lok Sabha may accept or reject any or all recommendations.
  6. If the Rajya Sabha does not return the Bill within fourteen days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Lok Sabha.

The Rajya Sabha cannot amend or reject a Money Bill in the same manner as an Ordinary Bill. Its role is recommendatory.

8. Presidential Assent

Article 111 governs presidential assent to Bills passed by Parliament.

When a Bill is presented to the President, the President may:

  • Give assent to the Bill.
  • Withhold assent.
  • Return a Bill, if it is not a Money Bill, for reconsideration by Parliament.

If Parliament passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendments, and presents it to the President, the President shall not withhold assent.

The Constitution does not prescribe a specific time limit for the President's decision under Article 111. The constitutional position must be understood alongside applicable judicial interpretation.

A Money Bill cannot be returned for reconsideration under Article 111.

9. Legislative Procedure in State Legislatures

Articles 196–201 govern the legislative procedure in State Legislatures.

In a unicameral State, Bills are considered by the Legislative Assembly. In a bicameral State, the Legislature consists of the Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council.

Ordinary Bills may originate in either House of a bicameral State Legislature, subject to constitutional provisions. The Legislative Council has a revising role but cannot permanently block an Ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly.

Article 197 provides that the Legislative Council may delay an Ordinary Bill for a limited period. The Assembly may pass the Bill again, after which the Council's further powers are restricted by the Constitution.

There is no provision for a joint sitting of a State Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council.

10. Money Bills in State Legislatures

Articles 198 and 199 provide the special procedure for Money Bills in States.

  • A Money Bill cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council.
  • The Legislative Assembly may pass the Bill and transmit it to the Council for recommendations.
  • The Council must return the Bill within fourteen days.
  • The Assembly may accept or reject the Council's recommendations.
  • If the Council does not return the Bill within fourteen days, it is deemed passed in the form passed by the Assembly.

Article 200 governs the Governor's assent to State Bills. Article 201 applies where a Bill is reserved for the consideration of the President.

11. Parliamentary Motions and Resolutions

Motions and resolutions enable members to raise matters, seek decisions, express opinions and hold the government accountable. Their detailed operation is governed by the rules and practices of each House.

11.1 Substantive Motion

A substantive motion is a self-contained proposal on which the House is invited to make a decision.

11.2 Substitute Motion

A substitute motion is moved in place of an original motion and proposes an alternative course or decision.

11.3 Subsidiary Motion

A subsidiary motion depends on or relates to another proceeding or motion. It may regulate the manner in which the House considers a question.

11.4 Resolution

A resolution is a formal expression of the opinion or decision of the House. It may be moved by the government or a private member, depending on its nature and applicable rules.

12. Parliamentary Questions

Question Hour is an important instrument of legislative oversight. Members seek information from Ministers about government policy, administration, expenditure and implementation.

12.1 Starred Questions

A starred question requires an oral answer. Supplementary questions may be asked subject to the rules and the Speaker's or Chairman's control.

12.2 Unstarred Questions

An unstarred question receives a written answer. Supplementary questions are not ordinarily permitted.

12.3 Short Notice Questions

A short notice question concerns a matter of urgent public importance and is admitted under the applicable procedural requirements.

Question Hour strengthens ministerial accountability by requiring the executive to provide information to elected representatives.

13. Zero Hour and Other Discussion Mechanisms

Zero Hour is a parliamentary practice that developed in India. It provides members an opportunity to raise matters of urgent public importance after Question Hour, subject to the procedural arrangements of the House.

It is not expressly mentioned in the Constitution and is not equivalent to a formal constitutional motion.

Other discussion mechanisms include:

  • Short-duration discussions.
  • Calling Attention notices.
  • Half-an-hour discussions.
  • Special mentions and matters raised under applicable House procedures.
  • Discussions on matters of public importance.

14. Important Parliamentary Motions

14.1 No-Confidence Motion

A no-confidence motion tests whether the Council of Ministers continues to enjoy the confidence of the Lok Sabha. Its passage requires the Council of Ministers to resign, reflecting Article 75(3).

14.2 Confidence Motion

A confidence motion is moved to demonstrate that the government enjoys the support of the House.

14.3 Censure Motion

A censure motion expresses disapproval of a particular policy, action or failure of the government. Its scope and consequences differ from those of a no-confidence motion.

14.4 Adjournment Motion

An adjournment motion seeks to interrupt normal business to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance. Its admission is subject to strict procedural conditions.

14.5 Privilege Motion

A privilege motion raises an alleged breach of parliamentary privilege or contempt of the House. Its substantive constitutional framework is addressed separately in the Parliamentary Privileges chapter.

15. Legislative Delegation and Subordinate Legislation

Parliament may delegate authority to the executive to make rules, regulations, orders and notifications under an enabling Act.

Delegated legislation is necessary because modern governance involves technical detail, changing circumstances and administrative complexity. However, the legislature must retain control over the essential legislative policy and the limits of delegated authority.

Important safeguards include:

  • The parent Act must define the scope and purpose of delegated authority.
  • Delegated rules must remain within the enabling legislation and the Constitution.
  • Parliamentary committees may scrutinise subordinate legislation.
  • Courts may review delegated legislation for constitutional or statutory invalidity.
  • Publication and consultation requirements may improve transparency and accountability.

16. Parliamentary Committees in the Legislative Process

Committees provide detailed examination of legislative proposals and can improve the quality of law-making.

A Bill may be referred to a Departmentally Related Standing Committee, Select Committee or Joint Committee, according to the applicable procedure and decision of the House.

Committee scrutiny can:

  • Identify drafting inconsistencies and legal ambiguities.
  • Examine constitutional implications.
  • Invite expert and stakeholder views.
  • Assess administrative and financial consequences.
  • Recommend changes to improve implementation.

Committee reports are generally recommendatory. The House retains the authority to decide the legislative proposal.

17. Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business

Article 118 empowers each House of Parliament to make rules for regulating its procedure and conduct of business, subject to the Constitution.

Article 208 provides a corresponding power for State Legislatures.

These rules regulate matters such as:

  • Admission and listing of business.
  • Notice and introduction of Bills.
  • Questions and motions.
  • Amendments and voting.
  • Committee referrals and reports.
  • Maintenance of order and conduct of proceedings.

Procedural rules facilitate orderly deliberation while preserving the constitutional authority and autonomy of each House.

18. Legislative Autonomy and Judicial Review

Articles 122 and 212 protect parliamentary and State legislative proceedings from being questioned in court merely on the ground of an alleged irregularity of procedure.

These provisions do not create an unrestricted immunity from judicial review. Courts may examine substantial constitutional violations and issues involving legislative competence or compliance with mandatory constitutional requirements, subject to established principles.

Important judicial decisions include:

  • Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007): Legislative proceedings and privileges are not wholly beyond judicial review; substantive illegality or unconstitutionality may be examined.
  • Rojer Mathew v. South Indian Bank Ltd. (2019): Examined the scope and constitutional requirements surrounding Money Bills and the legislative process.
  • Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. v. Union of India (2022): Considered the constitutional significance of the GST Council's recommendations and the federal legislative framework.

19. Constitutional Amendment Procedure

Article 368 prescribes the procedure for amendment of the Constitution.

  • A Constitutional Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  • It must be passed separately by each House.
  • It requires a majority of the total membership of each House and a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  • Amendments affecting specified federal provisions also require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.
  • After being duly passed and, where required, ratified, the Bill is presented to the President, who shall give assent.

The special procedure protects constitutional stability while allowing formal constitutional change. The Basic Structure doctrine also limits Parliament's amending power.

20. Legislative Procedure and Democratic Representation

Legislative procedure is not merely a technical process. It shapes the quality of representation and the relationship between the executive, legislature and citizens.

Effective legislative functioning requires:

  • Meaningful participation by members across political parties.
  • Adequate time for debate and scrutiny.
  • Transparent and accessible legislative information.
  • Effective committee examination.
  • Consultation with affected groups and experts.
  • Respect for constitutional limits and institutional autonomy.

21. Challenges in Legislative Functioning

  • Disruptions: Repeated interruptions reduce the time available for deliberation.
  • Limited debate: Important Bills may be passed with insufficient discussion.
  • Low committee referral: Reduced scrutiny can weaken legislative quality.
  • Rushed law-making: Inadequate consultation may create implementation difficulties.
  • Complex legislation: Technical Bills require specialist analysis and adequate research support.
  • Executive dominance: A strong executive majority may reduce independent legislative scrutiny.
  • Partisan polarisation: Political confrontation can displace constructive deliberation.
  • Weak post-legislative review: The actual impact of laws may not be systematically assessed.

22. Reforms to Strengthen Legislative Procedure

  • Ensure adequate time for debate and scrutiny of Bills.
  • Strengthen committee referral and technical research capacity.
  • Publish draft legislation and explanatory materials in accessible formats.
  • Encourage structured consultation with stakeholders and experts.
  • Improve legislative impact assessment and post-legislative evaluation.
  • Strengthen the institutional capacity of the Speaker and presiding officers to maintain orderly proceedings.
  • Promote constructive participation by the opposition and government members.
  • Improve access to legislative records, committee reports and amendments.
  • Ensure that delegated legislation remains within statutory and constitutional limits.

25. Integrated Understanding

The legislative process reflects a balance between efficiency and deliberation. The executive may initiate most government legislation, but Parliament remains the constitutional forum for debate, amendment, approval and scrutiny.

Bicameralism provides an additional layer of review. Committee scrutiny improves technical and institutional examination. Presidential assent completes the ordinary parliamentary legislative process, while judicial review ensures that legislation remains within constitutional limits.

The effectiveness of legislative procedure ultimately depends on meaningful debate, respect for institutional rules, adequate scrutiny and accountability to citizens.
