1. Meaning and Concept

A parliamentary system of government is a form of representative government in which the political executive is drawn from the legislature and remains collectively responsible to the popularly elected House. The executive exercises governmental authority while remaining subject to legislative scrutiny and confidence.

India follows the parliamentary form of government at both the Union and State levels. At the Union level, the President is the constitutional head, while the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers exercise real executive authority. At the State level, the Governor is the constitutional head, while the Chief Minister and Council of Ministers exercise real executive authority.

The central principle is responsible government: the executive must retain the confidence of the elected House and answer to the legislature for its policies and administration.

2. Historical Evolution in India

India's parliamentary institutions developed through constitutional and legislative reforms during British rule, followed by the adoption of a democratic parliamentary framework under the Constitution.

  • Indian Councils Act, 1861: Introduced limited legislative participation through legislative councils.
  • Indian Councils Act, 1892: Expanded legislative councils and allowed limited discussion of budgets and questions.
  • Indian Councils Act, 1909: Expanded legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.
  • Government of India Act, 1919: Introduced dyarchy in provinces and a bicameral legislature at the Centre.
  • Government of India Act, 1935: Provided for provincial autonomy and proposed an All-India Federation. The federal scheme was not implemented.
  • Constituent Assembly: Debated and adopted a parliamentary system based on democratic representation, ministerial responsibility and constitutional limitations on public power.

The Constitution adopted parliamentary government while adapting it to India's written Constitution, federal structure, universal adult franchise, judicial review and constitutional supremacy.

4. Essential Features of the Parliamentary System

A. Nominal and real executive

The constitutional head formally exercises executive functions, ordinarily on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. Actual political leadership and policy direction are exercised by the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers.

B. Majority party or coalition government

The government is ordinarily formed by the party or coalition that can command the confidence of the Lok Sabha. The Prime Minister leads the government and coordinates its political and administrative programme.

C. Collective responsibility

The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha under Article 75(3). At the State level, the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly under Article 164(2).

D. Individual ministerial responsibility

Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President at the Union level and the Governor at the State level, subject to the constitutional framework. In parliamentary practice, a minister who loses the confidence of the Prime Minister or Chief Minister may be asked to resign or may be removed through the applicable constitutional process.

E. Leadership of the Prime Minister

The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers, coordinates government policy, allocates or reshuffles portfolios through the constitutional process and acts as the principal link between the President and the Council of Ministers.

F. Political executive drawn from the legislature

Ministers are generally members of Parliament. A person who is not a member of either House may be appointed a minister but must become a member of Parliament within six consecutive months under Article 75(5). The corresponding rule applies to State ministers under Article 164(4).

G. Leadership and opposition

The parliamentary system recognises both the governing majority and the opposition. The opposition scrutinises government decisions, questions ministers, debates legislation and offers alternative policies.

H. Political accountability

The executive is answerable to Parliament through questions, debates, motions, committee scrutiny, budgetary control and the possibility of losing the confidence of the House.

5. Nominal Executive and Real Executive

The distinction between the nominal and real executive is central to understanding India's parliamentary system.

Dimension Nominal executive Real executive
Union President Prime Minister and Council of Ministers
State Governor Chief Minister and Council of Ministers
Constitutional position Head of the constitutional executive Political leadership of government
Decision-making Ordinarily acts on ministerial advice Formulates and directs government policy
Accountability Not collectively responsible to the elected House Collectively responsible to the elected House

The distinction does not mean that the constitutional head has no constitutional role. The President and Governor perform important constitutional functions, including formal appointments, summoning legislatures and other functions assigned by the Constitution. Their discretion is limited by constitutional provisions, conventions and judicial interpretation.

6. Collective Responsibility

Collective responsibility means that the Council of Ministers functions as a team and is jointly accountable to the elected House for government policy and decisions.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 75(3): Union Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha.
  • Article 164(2): State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.

Implications

  • The Council of Ministers must ordinarily speak with one voice in public after a government decision is taken.
  • Ministers are expected to support Cabinet decisions or resign if they cannot accept them.
  • Defeat of the government on a confidence motion ordinarily requires the Council of Ministers to resign or seek another constitutionally appropriate course.
  • The government is accountable as a whole for its policies and administration.

Importance

  • Ensures political unity in government.
  • Creates a clear line of accountability to the elected House.
  • Prevents ministers from disclaiming responsibility for collective decisions.
  • Strengthens the relationship between executive authority and legislative confidence.

7. Individual Responsibility of Ministers

Individual responsibility refers to the responsibility of each minister for the administration of the ministry or department under their charge.

At the Union level, Article 75(2) provides that ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President. At the State level, Article 164(1) contains the corresponding provision concerning the Governor.

In parliamentary practice, the constitutional pleasure is exercised within the framework of responsible government. A minister's continuation in office is closely linked to the confidence and leadership of the Prime Minister or Chief Minister.

Individual responsibility should be distinguished from collective responsibility: the former concerns the conduct and administration of an individual minister; the latter concerns the government as a whole.

8. The Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers

The Prime Minister is the central political figure in the Union parliamentary executive. The Council of Ministers assists and advises the President under Article 74.

Constitutional position of the Prime Minister

  • Appointed by the President under Article 75.
  • Heads the Council of Ministers.
  • Communicates decisions of the Council to the President.
  • Furnishes information relating to administration and legislative proposals when required under Article 78.
  • Coordinates the work of ministries and departments.
  • Plays a central role in government policy and parliamentary leadership.

Composition of the Council of Ministers

The Council of Ministers comprises ministers of different ranks and responsibilities. In ordinary practice, it includes Cabinet Ministers, Ministers of State and, where appointed, Ministers of State with independent charge.

The Constitution does not prescribe these political categories as separate constitutional offices. They are part of the organisational structure of the Council of Ministers.

91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003

The amendment limited the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, to 15 per cent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha. It also prescribed a minimum of 12 ministers for a State Legislative Assembly under the corresponding provision.

The amendment sought to discourage excessively large ministries and reduce the use of ministerial appointments as political inducements.

9. Cabinet Government and Cabinet Committees

The Cabinet is the smaller and principal decision-making body within the larger Council of Ministers. It provides collective political direction and coordinates major government policies.

Cabinet and Council of Ministers

Dimension Council of Ministers Cabinet
Composition All categories of ministers Smaller body of senior ministers
Role Broader constitutional and political body Principal policy and decision-making body
Meetings Not ordinarily required to meet as a full body regularly Meets regularly to consider government business
Constitutional reference Expressly recognised in Articles 74 and 75 Expressly referred to in Article 352(3)

Cabinet Committees

Cabinet Committees are organisational mechanisms used to consider specific areas of government policy, administration and coordination. They are established and reconstituted according to executive requirements.

They help reduce the workload of the full Cabinet and allow focused examination of complex policy issues. Their decisions operate within the authority and procedures of the government.

10. Relationship Between the Executive and Legislature

The parliamentary system combines executive leadership with legislative accountability. The executive participates in the legislative process while remaining answerable to Parliament.

Executive participation in legislation

  • Ministers introduce and defend government bills.
  • The government ordinarily determines the legislative programme.
  • Ministers participate in debates and answer questions.
  • The executive recommends certain constitutional and procedural actions, including summoning and prorogation.

Legislative control over the executive

  • Questions and supplementary questions.
  • Debates and discussions on government policy.
  • Adjournment motions and other procedural devices.
  • Budgetary scrutiny and control over public expenditure.
  • Parliamentary committee examination.
  • Confidence and no-confidence motions.

This relationship is sometimes described as a fusion of executive and legislative powers, but it does not mean that the two institutions are identical. Parliament retains its legislative, deliberative and oversight functions.

11. Confidence of the House and Government Stability

A parliamentary government must retain the confidence of the elected House. At the Union level, this is the Lok Sabha; at the State level, it is the Legislative Assembly.

Confidence motion

A confidence motion is used to establish whether the government continues to command the support of the House. It may be moved by the government when its majority is in question.

No-confidence motion

A no-confidence motion seeks to determine whether the House continues to support the government. If it is carried, the government is expected to resign because it has lost the confidence of the House.

Other parliamentary motions

  • Censure motion: Expresses disapproval of a specified policy or action. Its consequences depend on parliamentary procedure and political circumstances.
  • Adjournment motion: Raises a matter of urgent public importance under the rules of the House.
  • Privilege motion: Raises an alleged breach of parliamentary privilege.

These motions are not interchangeable. A no-confidence motion directly concerns the government's continued authority to govern.

12. Dissolution, Prorogation and Adjournment

Parliamentary government operates through legislative sessions and constitutional procedures governing the continuation or termination of House proceedings.

Concept Meaning Effect
Adjournment Suspension of a sitting of the House House remains in existence; sitting resumes later
Adjournment sine die Termination of a sitting without fixing the next date House remains in existence
Prorogation Formal termination of a session Ends the session, not the existence of the House
Dissolution Termination of the life of the Lok Sabha or Legislative Assembly Fresh elections are ordinarily required to constitute a new House

The Rajya Sabha is a permanent House and is not subject to dissolution. The Lok Sabha has a normal term of five years, unless sooner dissolved, subject to the constitutional provisions concerning emergencies.

13. Parliamentary System at the State Level

States follow the parliamentary form of government through a constitutional arrangement broadly corresponding to the Union executive.

  • The Governor is the constitutional head of the State.
  • The Chief Minister heads the State Council of Ministers.
  • The Governor appoints the Chief Minister under Article 164.
  • Other ministers are appointed by the Governor on the advice of the Chief Minister.
  • The State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly.
  • The Chief Minister communicates decisions of the Council to the Governor under Article 167.

The Governor ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except in constitutionally recognised areas of discretion. Such discretion is not an unrestricted personal power and must be exercised within constitutional limits.

14. Parliamentary and Presidential Systems: Comparison

Dimension Parliamentary system Presidential system
Executive leadership Prime Minister leads the government President leads the executive
Executive's source of authority Depends on legislative confidence Separate constitutional electoral mandate
Relationship with legislature Executive is drawn from and accountable to legislature Executive and legislature have separate institutional mandates
Tenure Government may fall if it loses legislative confidence President generally has a fixed term, subject to constitutional removal procedures
Removal of executive Loss of confidence can require resignation Removal ordinarily follows impeachment or other specified constitutional processes
Ministerial membership Ministers are generally legislators or must qualify within the prescribed period Executive officers are ordinarily separate from legislative membership
Executive-legislative relationship Fusion with institutional accountability Separation with checks and balances

The United States is a prominent example of a presidential system. India and the United Kingdom follow parliamentary systems, although their constitutional structures differ significantly.

15. Why India Adopted the Parliamentary System

  • Familiarity: Parliamentary institutions had developed in India during the colonial period, creating some administrative and political familiarity.
  • Responsible government: The system makes the executive answerable to elected representatives.
  • Representation: It allows diverse political groups and regional interests to participate in government formation and legislative debate.
  • Flexibility: A government that loses confidence can be replaced without necessarily waiting for the completion of a fixed executive term.
  • Coordination: The executive ordinarily has the support of the legislative majority, facilitating the passage of its legislative programme.
  • Accountability: Regular questions, debates, committees and confidence procedures provide mechanisms for scrutiny.

16. Advantages of the Parliamentary System

  1. Executive accountability: The government must answer to the elected House and retain its confidence.
  2. Legislative scrutiny: Ministers participate in debates and respond to questions about government decisions.
  3. Political flexibility: A government can be replaced when it loses the confidence of the House.
  4. Representation of diversity: Coalition arrangements and legislative participation can accommodate varied political interests.
  5. Coordination: Executive leadership and legislative majority can facilitate policy implementation.
  6. Collective responsibility: The Cabinet is politically accountable as a team rather than as unrelated individual officeholders.

17. Limitations and Criticisms

  1. Political instability: Coalition disagreements or loss of majority support may lead to government instability.
  2. Executive dominance: A strong majority may enable the executive to dominate parliamentary proceedings and reduce effective scrutiny.
  3. Party discipline: Strict party whips can limit independent deliberation by legislators.
  4. Short-term political incentives: Governments may prioritise immediate electoral considerations over long-term reforms.
  5. Cabinet concentration: Important decisions may be concentrated in a small group of senior ministers.
  6. Legislative disruption: Frequent disruptions and inadequate debate can weaken legislative oversight.
  7. Weak committee scrutiny: Reduced referral of bills or inadequate committee examination may affect legislative quality.
  8. Overlapping political and executive roles: Ministers must balance constituency, party and administrative responsibilities.

18. Parliamentary System and Constitutional Supremacy

India's parliamentary system operates within a written Constitution that is supreme. Parliament is a constitutionally constituted law-making body and does not possess unlimited sovereign authority.

  • Parliament must legislate within its constitutional competence.
  • Legislation is subject to Fundamental Rights and other constitutional limitations.
  • Constitutional amendments are subject to Article 368 and the Basic Structure doctrine.
  • Judicial review enables courts to examine the constitutional validity of laws and executive action.
  • The executive is subject to both legislative accountability and constitutional review.

This distinguishes India from the traditional doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty associated with the United Kingdom.

19. Important Judicial Decisions

Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955)

The Supreme Court explained the nature of executive power under the Constitution and the relationship between the executive and legislature. The judgment is important for understanding the constitutional position of the executive in India's parliamentary system.

U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971)

The Supreme Court held that the Council of Ministers does not cease to exist merely because the Lok Sabha has been dissolved. The constitutional requirement of a Council of Ministers under Article 74 continues.

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

The Supreme Court clarified that the President and Governor are constitutional heads and ordinarily exercise their powers on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers, except where the Constitution permits discretion.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The Supreme Court emphasised that the question of whether a ministry enjoys majority support should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House. The judgment is important for federalism, responsible government and limits on the use of Article 356.

Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)

The Supreme Court examined the constitutional limits on the Governor's role in relation to legislative proceedings. The decision is relevant to the relationship between the Governor, Council of Ministers and State Legislature.

20. Contemporary Issues and Reform Priorities

Strengthening legislative deliberation

  • Ensure adequate time for discussion of important bills.
  • Strengthen the role and capacity of parliamentary committees.
  • Improve access to legislative research and technical expertise.
  • Encourage informed debate and scrutiny across party lines.

Improving executive accountability

  • Ensure meaningful responses to parliamentary questions.
  • Strengthen follow-up on committee recommendations.
  • Improve transparency in government decision-making.
  • Make performance information accessible to legislators and citizens.

Strengthening opposition and representation

  • Protect the institutional space for opposition scrutiny.
  • Promote constructive debate and legislative cooperation.
  • Improve opportunities for members to raise constituency and public-interest concerns.

Balancing party discipline and deliberation

Party discipline is necessary for stable government, but excessive control over every legislative vote can weaken deliberation. Reform discussions should consider the relationship between the anti-defection framework, legislative independence and responsible government.

Responsible use of constitutional offices

The constitutional heads at Union and State levels should act within constitutional limits, respect established conventions and avoid unnecessary political conflict with elected governments.

22. Integrated Revision Framework

Understand the parliamentary system through the following connected institutional relationships:

  1. Constitutional head: President at the Union and Governor at the State level.
  2. Political executive: Prime Minister or Chief Minister and the Council of Ministers.
  3. Legislative confidence: Government must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha or Legislative Assembly.
  4. Collective responsibility: The Council of Ministers is jointly accountable to the elected House.
  5. Legislative oversight: Questions, debates, committees, budgetary scrutiny and motions.
  6. Constitutional limitations: Judicial review, federal distribution of powers and Fundamental Rights.

The defining feature of parliamentary government is not merely the presence of a Prime Minister or a legislature. It is the constitutional relationship in which the political executive remains collectively responsible to the elected House.
