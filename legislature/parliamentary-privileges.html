1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Parliamentary privileges are special rights, immunities and exemptions enjoyed by each House of Parliament, its members and committees, which are necessary for the effective discharge of legislative functions.

They protect the independence and authority of the legislature from improper interference. Their purpose is not to place legislators above the law, but to ensure that legislative institutions can deliberate, legislate and scrutinise the executive without intimidation or obstruction.

Privileges must therefore be understood in the context of representative democracy, freedom of speech, separation of powers, institutional autonomy and the rule of law.

2. Constitutional Framework

The principal constitutional provisions are:

  • Article 105: Powers, privileges and immunities of Parliament, its members and committees.
  • Article 194: Powers, privileges and immunities of State Legislatures, their members and committees.
  • Article 361A: Protection for publication of substantially true reports of parliamentary and State legislative proceedings, subject to constitutional conditions.
  • Article 122: Courts not to inquire into parliamentary proceedings on the ground of alleged procedural irregularity.
  • Article 212: Corresponding protection for proceedings of State Legislatures.

Articles 105 and 194 are the central provisions. They recognise legislative privileges while leaving important questions concerning their precise scope to constitutional interpretation and parliamentary law.

3. Historical Evolution

Parliamentary privileges in India developed from the Westminster parliamentary tradition, but their constitutional position is governed by the Indian Constitution.

The Government of India Act, 1935 contained provisions concerning the privileges of legislatures. The Constitution adopted a framework under Articles 105 and 194.

Originally, Articles 105(3) and 194(3) referred to the privileges of the House of Commons of the United Kingdom and its members and committees, until defined by Parliament or the State Legislature, respectively.

The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 removed the reference to the House of Commons. The relevant clauses now refer to the powers, privileges and immunities of the respective legislatures and their members and committees as existing immediately before the commencement of Section 15 of the Amendment Act.

This change removed the automatic constitutional linkage to the privileges of the British House of Commons.

4. Article 105: Privileges of Parliament

Article 105 provides the constitutional basis for parliamentary privileges.

4.1 Freedom of Speech in Parliament — Article 105(1)

Subject to the Constitution and the rules and standing orders regulating parliamentary procedure, there is freedom of speech in Parliament.

This freedom enables members to express views, question the government, criticise policies and participate in legislative deliberation.

It is not identical to the general freedom of speech under Article 19(1)(a). It operates within the institutional framework of Parliament and is subject to constitutional and procedural limitations.

4.2 Immunity from Court Proceedings — Article 105(2)

No member of Parliament is liable to proceedings in any court in respect of anything said or any vote given by the member in Parliament or any committee thereof.

No person is liable in respect of the publication by or under the authority of either House of Parliament of any report, paper, votes or proceedings.

This immunity protects legislative speech and voting within the constitutional setting. It does not create a general immunity for statements made outside parliamentary proceedings.

4.3 Other Powers and Privileges — Article 105(3)

Parliament may define by law the powers, privileges and immunities of each House and of its members and committees. Until so defined, the constitutional provision preserves the privileges recognised by the applicable constitutional framework.

4.4 Ministers and the Attorney-General — Article 105(4)

The provisions relating to parliamentary privileges also apply to persons who have the right to speak in and otherwise take part in the proceedings of a House or its committees by virtue of the Constitution.

This includes Ministers and the Attorney-General of India, subject to the constitutional conditions. It does not confer a general right to vote where no such voting entitlement exists.

5. Article 194: Privileges of State Legislatures

Article 194 provides the corresponding framework for State Legislatures.

  • Article 194(1) recognises freedom of speech in the State Legislature, subject to the Constitution and procedural rules.
  • Article 194(2) provides immunity from court proceedings in respect of speech and votes in the Legislature or its committees and protects authorised publication of proceedings.
  • Article 194(3) concerns other powers, privileges and immunities of the House, its members and committees.
  • Article 194(4) extends the relevant protections to persons entitled to participate in legislative proceedings under the Constitution.

Article 194 is the State-level counterpart of Article 105, although the institutional setting and applicable legislative rules differ.

6. Classification of Parliamentary Privileges

Privileges may broadly be classified into collective privileges enjoyed by the House as an institution and individual privileges enjoyed by members in their legislative capacity.

6.1 Collective Privileges

  • Right to regulate internal proceedings and procedure, subject to the Constitution.
  • Right to control admission to the House and regulate access to its proceedings.
  • Power to publish or authorise publication of reports and proceedings.
  • Power to protect the authority and dignity of the House against contempt and breach of privilege.
  • Power to conduct inquiries through committees and require evidence in accordance with applicable rules.
  • Power to take action against persons responsible for obstruction of legislative functions, within constitutional limits.

6.2 Individual Privileges

  • Freedom of speech within the House, subject to constitutional and procedural limitations.
  • Immunity from court proceedings in respect of speech or votes in the House or its committees.
  • Certain protections relating to attendance and participation in legislative proceedings, as recognised by law and parliamentary practice.

Privileges are functional protections. They are not personal benefits independent of legislative responsibilities.

7. Freedom of Speech in the Legislature

Freedom of speech within the legislature is essential to representative government. It enables members to raise public grievances, scrutinise executive action, debate Bills and express dissent.

However, this freedom operates subject to:

  • The provisions of the Constitution.
  • Rules and standing orders of the House.
  • Directions of the Speaker or Chairman under the applicable rules.
  • Restrictions on discussion concerning the conduct of judges under Articles 121 and 211, except upon a motion for presenting an address to the President as provided in the Constitution.
  • Other constitutional limitations governing legislative proceedings.

Thus, legislative freedom is broad but not unlimited.

8. Immunity from Court Proceedings

Article 105(2) protects members from court proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given in Parliament or its committees. Article 194(2) provides corresponding protection in State Legislatures.

The immunity is designed to ensure that members can participate in legislative proceedings without fear of civil or criminal proceedings arising merely from protected speech or voting.

The protection is not a blanket immunity for all conduct of a legislator. Acts outside the protected legislative setting must be assessed under ordinary law and relevant constitutional principles.

The distinction between protected legislative conduct and independent criminal conduct has been particularly important in judicial interpretation of bribery and corruption allegations.

9. Breach of Privilege

A breach of privilege occurs when an act or omission infringes a privilege of the House or of its members, thereby obstructing or undermining the performance of legislative functions.

Examples may include:

  • Obstructing a member from performing legislative duties.
  • Threatening or intimidating a member because of legislative activity.
  • Publishing materially misleading accounts of proceedings in circumstances that violate applicable privileges or rules.
  • Unauthorised disclosure of protected committee material where it interferes with the House's work.
  • Disobeying a lawful direction or summons of a House or its committee.
  • Deliberately obstructing a parliamentary committee or giving false evidence before it.

Whether a particular act amounts to breach of privilege depends on the relevant privilege, the facts and the applicable constitutional and procedural framework.

10. Contempt of the House

Contempt of the House refers to conduct that obstructs or tends to obstruct the House, its members or committees in the discharge of their functions, or that undermines their authority.

Contempt may occur even where no specific, separately recognised privilege has been infringed, if the conduct obstructs the functioning of the House.

Examples may include:

  • Disrupting or obstructing legislative proceedings.
  • Interfering with witnesses or evidence before a committee.
  • Threatening members or officials in connection with legislative duties.
  • Disobeying a lawful order of the House.
  • Intentionally interfering with the functioning of parliamentary institutions.

11. Breach of Privilege and Contempt: Distinction

Basis Breach of Privilege Contempt of the House
Meaning Violation of a recognised privilege of the House or its members. Conduct obstructing or tending to obstruct the functioning or authority of the House.
Scope Centred on infringement of a specific privilege. Can include conduct that obstructs the House even without infringement of a specific privilege.
Purpose Protects privileges necessary for legislative functioning. Protects the authority and effective functioning of the legislature.
Relationship A breach of privilege may also constitute contempt. Contempt may exist independently of a specific breach of privilege.

12. Procedure for Raising a Privilege Matter

In Parliament, a member may raise a question involving breach of privilege under the applicable rules of procedure.

The process generally involves:

  1. A member gives notice of the alleged breach in accordance with the rules.
  2. The Speaker or Chairman examines the admissibility and procedural requirements.
  3. Where appropriate, the matter may be referred to the Committee of Privileges or dealt with through another permitted procedure.
  4. The Committee examines the facts, may seek evidence and submits a report.
  5. The House considers the report and decides what action, if any, should be taken.

The presiding officer's role is important in filtering matters and ensuring that privilege procedure is not used merely for partisan or personal purposes.

13. Committee of Privileges

The Committee of Privileges examines questions involving breach of privilege referred to it by the House or presiding officer under the applicable rules.

Its functions may include:

  • Examining the facts and circumstances of an alleged breach.
  • Determining whether a privilege or the authority of the House has been affected.
  • Seeking explanations, documents or evidence where appropriate.
  • Considering the seriousness and institutional consequences of the conduct.
  • Recommending appropriate action to the House.

The Committee's report assists the House, but the final decision on the matter rests with the House according to its procedures.

14. Punishments for Breach of Privilege or Contempt

Parliamentary Houses have historically exercised powers to impose sanctions for breach of privilege or contempt, subject to constitutional limitations and judicial review.

Possible measures may include:

  • Admonition or reprimand.
  • Suspension or other disciplinary action against a member under applicable rules.
  • Commitment to custody in cases where the House lawfully exercises such power.
  • Other measures necessary to protect legislative functioning and authority.

The use of privilege powers must be proportionate, procedurally fair and connected to the legitimate functions of the legislature.

15. Publication of Legislative Proceedings

Publication of parliamentary proceedings supports transparency, public accountability and informed democratic participation.

Article 105(2) protects publication by or under the authority of either House of Parliament of reports, papers, votes or proceedings.

Article 361A separately provides protection for publication in a newspaper of a substantially true report of proceedings of either House of Parliament or a State Legislature, unless the publication is proved to have been made with malice. This protection does not apply to reports of proceedings held in camera.

Article 361A also extends the relevant protection to broadcasts and similar forms of reporting, subject to its constitutional conditions.

The protection of authorised publication and the protection of independent reporting must therefore be distinguished.

16. Parliamentary Privileges and Fundamental Rights

Parliamentary privileges must be interpreted in harmony with the broader constitutional framework, including fundamental rights.

Important constitutional relationships include:

  • Article 19(1)(a): General freedom of speech and expression. Legislative speech is separately protected under Articles 105 and 194.
  • Article 14: Legislative privilege powers must be exercised consistently with constitutional principles against arbitrary state action.
  • Article 21: Where liberty is affected, constitutional requirements of fairness and legality remain relevant.
  • Articles 122 and 212: Protect legislative proceedings from judicial inquiry based merely on procedural irregularity, without excluding all substantive constitutional review.

Privileges are not a general licence to disregard constitutional rights. Their scope must be assessed in light of legislative necessity and constitutional supremacy.

17. Judicial Review of Parliamentary Privileges

The relationship between legislative privilege and judicial review has evolved through important judicial decisions.

17.1 M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha (Searchlight Case), 1959

The Supreme Court considered the relationship between legislative privileges and freedom of the press in the context of publication of proceedings of the Bihar Legislative Assembly. The decision recognised the importance of the constitutional privilege framework and the relationship between legislative autonomy and fundamental rights.

17.2 Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 — Keshav Singh Case

The Supreme Court examined the relationship between the privileges of a State Legislature and the jurisdiction of courts. The decision established that legislative privilege is not wholly beyond constitutional scrutiny and that the relationship between the legislature and judiciary must be resolved within the constitutional scheme.

17.3 Raja Ram Pal v. Hon'ble Speaker, Lok Sabha (2007)

The Supreme Court examined the expulsion of members following the cash-for-query controversy. It held that parliamentary privileges and proceedings are subject to judicial review where there is substantive illegality or unconstitutionality, even though courts cannot interfere merely on grounds of procedural irregularity.

17.4 Amarinder Singh v. Special Committee, Punjab Vidhan Sabha (2010)

The Supreme Court examined the scope of legislative privilege and the relationship between privilege powers and the constitutional limits on legislative action. The decision reinforced that privilege cannot be used without a legitimate connection to the functioning of the House.

18. Bribery and Legislative Privilege: Sita Soren Judgment

In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), a seven-judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court reconsidered the scope of legislative immunity in cases involving bribery connected with votes or speeches in the legislature.

The Court overruled the majority ruling in P.V. Narasimha Rao v. State (1998) to the extent that it recognised immunity from criminal prosecution for a legislator who accepted a bribe in connection with a vote in Parliament or a State Legislature.

The Court clarified that:

  • Bribery is not protected by the immunity under Articles 105(2) and 194(2).
  • The offence of bribery is complete upon acceptance of the bribe, subject to the applicable criminal law requirements.
  • The constitutional immunity for speech and voting does not extend to independent criminal conduct merely because it is connected with legislative activity.
  • Legislative privilege must serve the functioning and integrity of the legislature, not protect corruption.

The judgment strengthens legislative integrity and clarifies that constitutional privileges cannot be used as a shield for bribery.

19. Privileges and the Anti-Defection Framework

Parliamentary privileges and the anti-defection law address different constitutional concerns.

The Tenth Schedule regulates disqualification of legislators on specified grounds relating to defection. Articles 105 and 194 protect legislative functioning and speech.

A member's freedom to express views within the House must be understood alongside the constitutional and legal framework governing party discipline and disqualification. Legislative privilege does not eliminate the operation of the Tenth Schedule.

20. Need for Codification of Parliamentary Privileges

Articles 105(3) and 194(3) empower Parliament and State Legislatures to define privileges by law. A comprehensive statutory framework has not been enacted to codify all parliamentary privileges.

The continued reliance on inherited conventions and case-by-case interpretation raises questions about certainty, accountability and the relationship between institutional autonomy and individual rights.

Arguments supporting codification include:

  • Greater clarity about the scope of privileges.
  • Predictability for legislators, citizens and public institutions.
  • Clearer procedural safeguards for persons facing privilege proceedings.
  • Better alignment with fundamental rights and constitutional principles.
  • Reduced uncertainty and risk of inconsistent application.

However, codification must preserve sufficient institutional autonomy to protect legislative functions and respond to new forms of obstruction or interference.

21. Challenges and Concerns

  • Uncodified scope: Uncertainty may arise because many privileges are not exhaustively defined by statute.
  • Potential misuse: Privilege proceedings may be used to target criticism or political disagreement.
  • Overlap with fundamental rights: Balancing institutional autonomy with speech, liberty and equality requires careful interpretation.
  • Procedural fairness: Persons facing privilege allegations require fair notice and an opportunity to respond.
  • Executive-legislative tensions: Disputes may arise when privilege powers are asserted against public officials or other institutions.
  • Judicial-legislative boundaries: Courts and legislatures must respect their respective constitutional functions.
  • Public confidence: Excessive or disproportionate use of privilege powers can weaken institutional legitimacy.

22. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Consider clear statutory codification of privileges through a carefully designed constitutional framework.
  • Define the connection required between an alleged contempt and the functioning of the House.
  • Provide fair procedures, notice, hearing and reasoned decisions in privilege proceedings.
  • Ensure that sanctions are proportionate to the seriousness of the conduct.
  • Distinguish legitimate criticism of the legislature from conduct that obstructs its functioning.
  • Strengthen transparency in privilege committee proceedings, subject to legitimate confidentiality requirements.
  • Encourage institutional dialogue between legislatures and courts.
  • Ensure that privilege powers are exercised to protect legislative independence rather than personal or partisan interests.

26. Integrated Constitutional Understanding

Parliamentary privileges protect the legislature's ability to perform its constitutional functions independently and effectively. They are essential to meaningful debate, representative government and legislative oversight.

At the same time, privileges exist within a constitutional democracy governed by the rule of law. Their exercise must remain connected to legitimate legislative purposes and must respect constitutional limitations, procedural fairness and judicial review where applicable.

The Sita Soren judgment illustrates this balance: legislative independence is protected, but corruption cannot be converted into a privileged legislative act.

A sound privileges framework therefore requires institutional autonomy, legal clarity, fair procedure, proportionality and accountability.
