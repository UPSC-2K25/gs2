1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Defection refers to the abandonment of allegiance to a political party by an elected legislator, or conduct that violates the conditions under which the member was elected or continues to hold membership. In parliamentary democracies, defections can destabilise governments, undermine electoral mandates and encourage political corruption.

The Anti-Defection Law is contained in the Tenth Schedule of the Constitution. It was inserted by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985, and subsequently modified by the Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003.

Its principal objectives are:

  • To discourage political defections motivated by office or personal advantage.
  • To promote stability of elected governments.
  • To protect the integrity of party-based electoral mandates.
  • To reduce the influence of money and political inducements in legislative decision-making.

The law seeks to balance party discipline and government stability against the freedom of legislators to deliberate, dissent and represent their constituents.

2. Historical Background

Frequent defections and changes of political allegiance became a significant concern in Indian politics, particularly during the late 1960s and subsequent decades.

  • The expression "Aaya Ram, Gaya Ram" came to symbolise repeated changes of political allegiance by legislators.
  • Frequent defections contributed to instability in several State governments.
  • Political debates and committee recommendations highlighted the need for a constitutional mechanism to address the problem.
  • The Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985 introduced the Tenth Schedule.
  • The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 removed the protection previously available for a split by one-third of a legislative party and strengthened restrictions relating to ministerial appointments and the size of ministries.

The law was designed as an institutional response to instability, although its operation has raised continuing questions about legislative independence and the role of political parties.

The Tenth Schedule applies to members of both Houses of Parliament and to members of State Legislative Assemblies and Legislative Councils.

4. Grounds of Disqualification: Paragraph 2

Paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule identifies the circumstances in which a member of a House becomes disqualified on grounds of defection.

A. Member belonging to a political party

A member belonging to a political party becomes liable to disqualification if:

  • The member voluntarily gives up membership of that political party; or
  • The member votes or abstains from voting in the House contrary to a direction issued by the political party or a person authorised by it, without obtaining prior permission, and the action is not condoned by the party within fifteen days.

The expression "voluntarily gives up membership" is wider than formal resignation. Conduct and circumstances may establish that a member has abandoned party membership even without submitting a written resignation.

B. Independent member

An elected member who contested as an independent candidate becomes disqualified if the member joins any political party after the election.

C. Nominated member

A nominated member becomes disqualified if the member joins a political party after the expiry of six months from the date on which the member takes their seat.

A nominated member who joins a political party within the constitutionally permitted six-month period is not disqualified merely on that ground.

5. Meaning of Voluntarily Giving Up Membership

The Tenth Schedule does not define every form of conduct that amounts to voluntarily giving up party membership. Judicial interpretation has therefore been important.

In Ravi S. Naik v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court explained that voluntarily giving up membership is not confined to formal resignation. The member's conduct may indicate abandonment of political allegiance.

Relevant circumstances may include:

  • Public statements repudiating the party.
  • Conduct inconsistent with continuing party membership.
  • Participation in activities demonstrating alignment with another political formation.
  • Other evidence that, taken as a whole, establishes voluntary abandonment of party membership.

However, mere criticism of party leadership or disagreement on a policy issue should not automatically be treated as proof of defection. The facts, applicable law and judicial standards must be considered.

6. Whip and Voting Against Party Direction

A whip is a direction issued by a political party to its legislators regarding voting or participation in legislative proceedings. The Tenth Schedule makes violation of a party direction a possible ground for disqualification.

For disqualification under paragraph 2(1)(b), the relevant conditions include:

  • A direction issued by the political party or an authorised person.
  • The member voting or abstaining contrary to that direction.
  • Absence of prior permission for the conduct.
  • Failure of the party to condone the conduct within fifteen days.

The law does not restrict the use of party directions only to confidence motions or Money Bills. This broad scope has generated debate about whether ordinary legislative dissent should carry the same disqualification risk as conduct affecting government stability.

A key reform debate concerns limiting disqualification-triggering whips to votes that directly affect government survival, such as confidence motions and specified financial matters.

7. Merger Exception: Paragraph 4

Paragraph 4 provides an exception where a merger of the original political party takes place and the prescribed legislative-party threshold is satisfied.

A member is protected from disqualification where:

  • The member's original political party merges with another political party; and
  • At least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned agree to the merger.

Members who accept the merger and members who choose to function as a separate group are covered by the protection in paragraph 4, subject to its conditions.

The exception is based on a merger of the original political party and the two-thirds legislative-party threshold. A mere claim of internal division or a faction breaking away is not sufficient.

The merger exception is distinct from the former split exception, which was removed by the Ninety-first Amendment.

8. Removal of the Split Exception: Ninety-first Amendment

Before the Ninety-first Amendment, paragraph 3 of the Tenth Schedule protected a split in a political party where at least one-third of the members of the legislature party formed a separate group.

This exception was widely criticised because it could legitimise organised defections and encourage political fragmentation.

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 omitted paragraph 3. Consequently:

  • A one-third split no longer provides protection from disqualification.
  • The merger exception under paragraph 4 continues to operate.
  • The threshold for the merger exception is two-thirds of the members of the legislature party concerned.

The amendment sought to reduce incentives for engineered splits and strengthen the integrity of the anti-defection framework.

9. Exemption for Presiding Officers: Paragraph 5

Paragraph 5 provides a limited exemption for a member who is elected Speaker or Deputy Speaker of the House of the People or a Legislative Assembly, or Chairman or Deputy Chairman of the Council of States or a State Legislative Council.

The exemption applies when the member gives up membership of their political party because of election to the specified presiding office, or rejoins a political party after ceasing to hold that office, subject to the conditions in the paragraph.

The provision seeks to protect the impartiality and dignity of presiding officers by allowing them to discharge their procedural responsibilities without ordinary party affiliation pressures in the specified circumstances.

10. Authority to Decide Disqualification: Paragraph 6

Paragraph 6 provides that a question of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule is decided by the Chairman or Speaker of the relevant House.

  • For Parliament, the Chairman of the Rajya Sabha or Speaker of the Lok Sabha decides cases concerning members of the respective House.
  • For State Legislatures, the Chairman of the Legislative Council or Speaker of the Legislative Assembly decides cases concerning members of the relevant House.
  • The presiding officer acts as the adjudicating authority under the Schedule.

Where a question arises concerning the presiding officer's own disqualification, the Schedule provides for decision by a member elected by the House for that purpose, in accordance with the applicable provisions.

The original constitutional text sought to exclude judicial jurisdiction through paragraph 7. The Supreme Court subsequently held that the exclusion could not stand because the amendment had not satisfied the special ratification requirement under Article 368(2).

11. Judicial Review of the Presiding Officer's Decision

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule, subject to important qualifications.

The Court held that:

  • The Speaker or Chairman acts as a tribunal while deciding disqualification questions under the Schedule.
  • The decision is subject to judicial review on grounds such as jurisdictional error, violation of constitutional mandates, mala fides, perversity and breach of natural justice.
  • Courts ordinarily do not interfere before the presiding officer has made a decision, except in exceptional circumstances.
  • Paragraph 7 was invalid because excluding judicial review required ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures under Article 368(2).

The judgment preserved the adjudicatory role of the presiding officer while affirming the constitutional importance of judicial review.

12. Procedure and Natural Justice

Paragraph 8 empowers the presiding officer to make rules for giving effect to the provisions of the Tenth Schedule. The rules may regulate the presentation of petitions, verification, evidence and procedure.

Important procedural principles include:

  • A petition must identify the alleged ground of disqualification and provide supporting material.
  • The member concerned must receive a reasonable opportunity to respond.
  • The adjudicating authority should consider relevant evidence and give a reasoned decision.
  • Procedural fairness and natural justice are important safeguards against arbitrary disqualification.
  • The decision remains subject to judicial review within the constitutional framework.

The Tenth Schedule and applicable rules should be read together with the principles developed by the Supreme Court concerning timely adjudication and fairness.

13. Time Limit for Deciding Defection Petitions

The Tenth Schedule does not itself prescribe a fixed general time limit for deciding every disqualification petition. Delays by presiding officers have therefore been a recurring institutional concern.

In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court emphasised the need for timely decisions and indicated that, as a general rule, disqualification petitions should be decided within three months, unless exceptional circumstances justify a different course.

The Court also suggested that Parliament consider creating an independent mechanism to decide disqualification disputes instead of leaving the adjudicatory function exclusively with the Speaker or Chairman.

The judgment is important because prolonged inaction can undermine the purpose of the law and allow disputed members to continue participating in legislative proceedings.

14. Speaker as Adjudicator: Institutional Concerns

The Speaker or Chairman is generally elected through political processes and may have prior or continuing party affiliations. Assigning the adjudicatory function to a presiding officer creates concerns about institutional neutrality.

Key issues include:

  • Possible political bias in deciding petitions involving the ruling party or opposition.
  • Delay in deciding petitions during periods of government instability.
  • Potential strategic use of disqualification proceedings to alter legislative strength.
  • Difficulty in separating the presiding officer's procedural role from the adjudicatory role.
  • Limited confidence in the impartiality of decisions where political consequences are immediate.

The central institutional question is whether a politically elected presiding officer should have exclusive authority to decide disputes that can determine the survival of the government.

15. Resignation and Disqualification

Resignation from legislative membership and disqualification under the Tenth Schedule are legally distinct concepts, although they may arise from related political circumstances.

A member may resign from the House, but resignation does not automatically erase the possibility of proceedings relating to conduct that occurred while the member was a legislator.

In Shrimanth Balasaheb Patil v. Speaker, Karnataka Legislative Assembly (2019), the Supreme Court examined the relationship between resignation and disqualification in the context of political developments in Karnataka. The Court held that the Speaker could not impose a disqualification extending beyond the remainder of the Assembly's term as a punishment for the members' conduct, although disqualification from the current House could have consequences under the Tenth Schedule.

The case illustrates the need to distinguish:

  • Resignation from membership.
  • Disqualification for defection.
  • Eligibility to contest subsequent elections.
  • The legal consequences of a disqualification decision.

16. Disqualification and Ministerial Appointment

The Ninety-first Amendment strengthened the framework against political defections by restricting the appointment of defectors to ministerial office.

Article 164(1B) provides that a member of a State Legislature who is disqualified under paragraph 2 of the Tenth Schedule is also disqualified from being appointed as a minister for the period specified in the Constitution.

Article 75(1B) contains the corresponding restriction for the Union Council of Ministers.

Article 361B further provides that a member disqualified under the Tenth Schedule is disqualified from holding a remunerative political post for the constitutionally specified period.

These provisions seek to prevent defection from becoming a route to immediate political reward.

17. Ninety-first Constitutional Amendment: Wider Reforms

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 introduced changes beyond the Tenth Schedule.

  • Omitted the one-third split exception from the Tenth Schedule.
  • Retained the two-thirds merger exception.
  • Inserted Article 75(1A), limiting the size of the Union Council of Ministers to fifteen per cent of the total strength of the Lok Sabha, subject to a minimum of twelve ministers.
  • Inserted Article 164(1A), limiting the size of a State Council of Ministers to fifteen per cent of the total strength of the Legislative Assembly, subject to a minimum of twelve ministers.
  • Inserted Article 75(1B) and Article 164(1B) to restrict appointment of defectors as ministers.
  • Inserted Article 361B concerning disqualification from remunerative political posts.

The amendment sought to address political instability, excessive ministerial expansion and incentives for defection.

19. Strengths and Achievements

  • Discourages opportunistic changes of party allegiance.
  • Reduces the likelihood of governments being destabilised through individual defections.
  • Encourages legislators to respect the party mandate under which they were elected.
  • Provides a constitutional procedure for addressing specified forms of defection.
  • Supports political stability in parliamentary institutions.
  • Removes the former one-third split exception, reducing incentives for engineered fragmentation.

The law has become a significant part of India's electoral and parliamentary framework, particularly in situations involving narrow legislative majorities and coalition governments.

20. Criticisms and Limitations

A. Restriction on legislative freedom

Broad party directions may discourage legislators from exercising independent judgment even on ordinary policy matters.

B. Weakening of deliberative democracy

When members are expected to vote according to party instructions on a wide range of issues, legislative debate may become less meaningful.

C. Executive dominance

Strong party control over legislators may strengthen the executive's hold over the legislature and weaken independent scrutiny.

D. Partisan adjudication

The Speaker's political background may create doubts about neutrality in disputes involving the ruling party.

E. Delays

Undue delay in deciding petitions may allow members whose status is disputed to continue exercising legislative powers.

F. Loopholes and engineered mergers

The merger exception may be used strategically in political negotiations, especially where the two-thirds threshold is achievable.

G. Limited distinction between dissent and defection

The law does not adequately distinguish between dissent on ordinary legislation and conduct that threatens government survival.

H. Party leadership concentration

Strong control by party leadership may reduce the representative autonomy of legislators and weaken internal party democracy.

21. Reform Proposals

  • Limit the scope of whips: consider restricting disqualification-triggering party directions to confidence motions, no-confidence motions, Money Bills and other specified matters directly affecting government survival.
  • Independent adjudication: consider an independent tribunal or constitutional authority to decide defection petitions.
  • Time-bound decisions: establish a clear statutory or constitutional deadline, with limited and reasoned extensions.
  • Transparent proceedings: require reasoned orders, accessible records and procedural fairness.
  • Strengthen intra-party democracy: encourage transparent candidate selection, internal elections and meaningful consultation with elected representatives.
  • Clarify merger rules: ensure that the exception is applied only where the constitutional conditions of an actual merger are met.
  • Protect legislative dissent: distinguish legitimate policy disagreement from conduct that amounts to defection.
  • Improve judicial consistency: develop clear standards for review of jurisdiction, delay, natural justice and procedural fairness.

23. Relationship with Parliamentary Democracy

The Anti-Defection Law creates a tension between two important principles of parliamentary democracy:

  • Collective political responsibility and party discipline: elected governments need a stable legislative majority to implement policies and remain accountable to the House.
  • Representative independence and deliberation: legislators must be able to debate, scrutinise and express the interests of their constituencies.

A balanced anti-defection framework should prevent corruption and opportunistic government changes without converting every legislative vote into a test of party loyalty.

The objective should be stable government alongside meaningful legislative scrutiny, internal party democracy and representative accountability.

26. Integrated Understanding

The Anti-Defection Law is an important constitutional response to political instability and opportunistic changes of allegiance. The Tenth Schedule identifies grounds of disqualification, provides limited exceptions and assigns adjudicatory responsibility to the presiding officer of the relevant House.

Judicial interpretation has established that voluntary abandonment of party membership can be inferred from conduct, that the presiding officer's decisions are subject to judicial review and that unreasonable delay can undermine the law's purpose.

At the same time, broad party control over legislative voting, political adjudication and delays can weaken deliberative democracy. Reform should therefore preserve government stability while protecting legitimate legislative debate, representative autonomy and impartial adjudication.
