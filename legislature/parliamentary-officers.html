1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Parliamentary officers are constitutional or institutional functionaries who facilitate the orderly, impartial and effective functioning of the Houses of Parliament. Their responsibilities include presiding over sittings, regulating debates, interpreting procedural rules, maintaining discipline and protecting the rights of members.

The presiding officers occupy a particularly important position because parliamentary democracy requires both majority decision-making and fair opportunities for deliberation, opposition scrutiny and minority participation.

The Constitution provides for the Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha under Articles 93–97 and the Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha under Articles 89–92. Other officers and parliamentary authorities are governed by constitutional provisions, parliamentary rules and statutes.

The distinction between the two Houses is significant. The Lok Sabha Speaker and Deputy Speaker are members of the House they preside over. The Rajya Sabha Chairman is the Vice-President and is not a member of the House, whereas the Deputy Chairman is a member.

3. Speaker of the Lok Sabha

Article 93 requires the Lok Sabha to choose a Speaker and a Deputy Speaker as soon as may be. The Speaker is the principal presiding authority of the House and plays a central role in maintaining parliamentary order and ensuring the proper conduct of business.

Election

  • The Speaker is elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from among themselves.
  • The election is conducted in accordance with the Rules of Procedure and the applicable House arrangements.
  • The Speaker ordinarily continues in office until immediately before the first meeting of the Lok Sabha after its dissolution, subject to the constitutional provisions governing the office.

Tenure and vacation of office

Article 94 provides that the Speaker vacates office if they cease to be a member of the Lok Sabha, may resign by writing addressed to the Deputy Speaker, or may be removed by a resolution of the House.

A resolution for removal requires a majority of all the then members of the Lok Sabha and at least fourteen days' notice. The Speaker does not automatically vacate office upon dissolution of the Lok Sabha; the Speaker continues until immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha.

Major powers and functions

  • Presides over sittings of the Lok Sabha and regulates proceedings.
  • Maintains order and decorum and may take disciplinary action under the House rules.
  • Interprets and applies the Rules of Procedure and Conduct of Business.
  • Decides questions of admissibility of motions, resolutions and amendments.
  • Determines whether a member may speak and regulates the duration and sequence of debate.
  • Decides whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 110, with the Speaker's decision endorsed by the constitutional certification procedure.
  • Presides over joint sittings of Parliament under Article 108.
  • Exercises functions relating to parliamentary committees, including their constitution and procedural supervision under the applicable rules.
  • Decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule in relation to Lok Sabha members, subject to judicial review.
  • Exercises a casting vote in the event of equality of votes, but does not ordinarily vote in the first instance.

The Speaker's authority is procedural and institutional. It must be exercised consistently with the Constitution, parliamentary rules and principles of fairness.

4. Deputy Speaker of the Lok Sabha

The Deputy Speaker is elected by the Lok Sabha from among its members under Article 93. The office ensures continuity in presiding arrangements and supports the functioning of the House.

  • The Deputy Speaker presides when the Speaker is absent or when the office of Speaker is vacant, subject to Article 95.
  • While presiding, the Deputy Speaker exercises the powers of the Speaker.
  • The Deputy Speaker vacates office if they cease to be a member, resign or are removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the House, after fourteen days' notice.
  • The Deputy Speaker may preside over a sitting even when the Speaker is present if the Speaker is not presiding, in accordance with the applicable arrangements.

Article 96 provides that the Speaker or Deputy Speaker cannot preside while a resolution for their removal is under consideration. The officer concerned may participate in the proceedings and vote in the first instance, but cannot exercise a casting vote in such proceedings.

The Constitution does not prescribe that the Deputy Speaker must belong to an opposition party. However, the office has traditionally been associated with the expectation of broader political accommodation.

5. Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

Article 89 provides that the Vice-President of India is the ex officio Chairman of the Council of States. The Chairman presides over the Rajya Sabha and regulates its proceedings.

The Chairman is not a member of the Rajya Sabha. The office is linked to the constitutional office of Vice-President, who is elected by members of both Houses of Parliament through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote and secret ballot.

Powers and functions

  • Presides over sittings of the Rajya Sabha.
  • Maintains order and ensures compliance with the Rules of Procedure.
  • Regulates debate and decides procedural questions.
  • Determines admissibility of motions and other parliamentary business under the rules.
  • Exercises a casting vote in case of equality of votes, but does not vote in the first instance.
  • Exercises powers relating to the constitution and functioning of Rajya Sabha committees.
  • Decides questions of disqualification under the Tenth Schedule in relation to Rajya Sabha members, subject to judicial review.

The Chairman does not certify Money Bills. Under Article 110, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha performs the certification function.

6. Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha

Article 89 requires the Rajya Sabha to choose one of its members as Deputy Chairman as soon as may be.

  • The Deputy Chairman presides when the Chairman's office is vacant or when the Chairman is absent.
  • When presiding, the Deputy Chairman exercises the powers of the Chairman.
  • The Deputy Chairman vacates office if they cease to be a member, resign or are removed by a resolution of the Rajya Sabha passed by a majority of all the then members, after fourteen days' notice.

Under Article 91, Parliament may make provision for the performance of the Chairman's duties during a vacancy or absence. Article 92 restricts the Chairman and Deputy Chairman from presiding during consideration of a resolution for their removal.

8. Removal and Resignation: Constitutional Procedures

The Constitution provides distinct procedures for the presiding officers of the two Houses.

Speaker and Deputy Speaker

  • Article 94 governs vacation of office, resignation and removal.
  • Removal requires a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Lok Sabha.
  • At least fourteen days' notice must be given.
  • The Speaker's continuation after dissolution is protected until immediately before the first meeting of the new Lok Sabha.

Chairman and Deputy Chairman

  • Article 90 governs vacation, resignation and removal of the Deputy Chairman.
  • The Deputy Chairman may be removed by a resolution passed by a majority of all the then members of the Rajya Sabha, after fourteen days' notice.
  • The Chairman is the Vice-President and is removed under Article 67(b), not by the procedure applicable to the Deputy Chairman.
  • A resolution to remove the Vice-President must originate in the Rajya Sabha, be passed by a majority of all the then members of that House, and be agreed to by the Lok Sabha. At least fourteen days' notice is required.

9. Casting Vote and Impartiality

Article 100 provides that questions in either House are generally decided by a majority of members present and voting, excluding the presiding officer's vote in the first instance. In the event of equality of votes, the presiding officer has a casting vote.

The casting vote is intended to resolve a deadlock, not to provide the presiding officer with an ordinary additional vote. It is exercised within the framework of parliamentary procedure.

Impartiality is essential because the presiding officer must protect the rights of all members, including the opposition, while ensuring that the majority can conduct business. The legitimacy of the office depends on fair application of rules and reasoned procedural decisions.

10. Speaker's Decision on Money Bills

Article 110 defines a Money Bill. Article 110(3) provides that if a question arises whether a Bill is a Money Bill, the decision of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha is final. Article 110(4) requires the Speaker's certificate to be endorsed on a Money Bill when it is transmitted to the Rajya Sabha and when it is presented to the President for assent.

This certification has significant consequences because the Rajya Sabha's role in relation to a Money Bill is limited to recommendations within fourteen days.

Although the Constitution describes the Speaker's decision as final, the scope of judicial review of constitutional and procedural questions remains an important issue. The Supreme Court has examined the relationship between certification, constitutional limits and judicial review in cases involving the Money Bill procedure.

The office therefore requires careful and constitutionally faithful exercise of this power, particularly where classification affects the legislative role of the second House.

11. Presiding Officers and the Anti-Defection Law

The Tenth Schedule assigns the power to decide questions of disqualification on grounds of defection to the Speaker or Chairman, as the case may be.

The presiding officer's decision is subject to judicial review. Important principles include:

  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule while recognising judicial review of the presiding officer's decision on specified grounds.
  • Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020): the Supreme Court emphasised the need for timely decisions and suggested that Parliament consider an independent mechanism for deciding defection disputes.
  • Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra (2023): the Supreme Court addressed the Speaker's role and the constitutional framework governing disqualification proceedings and political party matters.

The central institutional concern is whether a presiding officer who is ordinarily associated with a political party can act as a neutral adjudicator in disputes that may determine the political survival of that party or government.

12. Parliamentary Secretariat

Article 98 provides for separate secretarial staff for each House of Parliament. Parliament may regulate recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to the secretarial staff through legislation.

The secretariats provide institutional and administrative support for parliamentary functioning. Their responsibilities include:

  • Providing procedural and legislative assistance to members and presiding officers.
  • Supporting the conduct of sittings, debates and parliamentary questions.
  • Maintaining records, reports and official parliamentary publications.
  • Providing secretarial support to committees.
  • Facilitating research, information services and member support.
  • Managing administrative, technical and procedural arrangements for the House.

The separate secretariats contribute to institutional autonomy by supporting each House's work through its own administrative machinery.

13. Secretary-General and Parliamentary Administration

The Secretary-General is the senior permanent official of each House's Secretariat. The office provides continuity, procedural expertise and institutional memory across changes in the political composition of Parliament.

The Secretary-General and Secretariat support the presiding officer in procedural matters, coordinate the conduct of business, maintain records and facilitate committee work.

Parliamentary administration must remain professionally impartial. Its role is to facilitate the functioning of elected representatives and constitutional authorities rather than to determine political outcomes.

14. Leader of the House and Leader of the Opposition

The Leader of the House is a parliamentary functionary who coordinates government business in the House. The position is recognised under the relevant rules and parliamentary practice.

The Leader of the Opposition is the leader of the largest opposition party recognised as such under the applicable statutory and parliamentary framework. The office has an important role in democratic scrutiny and in certain statutory appointment processes.

The Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977 provides the statutory framework for recognition and entitlements. The recognised status of the Leader of the Opposition should be distinguished from the political leader of an opposition party in ordinary public discussion.

Both positions facilitate the coordination of parliamentary business, but neither replaces the constitutional authority of the presiding officer over House proceedings.

15. Whips and Party Coordination

A whip is a party functionary responsible for coordinating the attendance and voting behaviour of party members in the legislature.

  • Whips communicate party positions on legislative business.
  • They help secure attendance and participation during important votes.
  • They coordinate the party's legislative strategy and communication with members.
  • Directions issued by political parties may have consequences under the Tenth Schedule when a member voluntarily gives up party membership or votes or abstains contrary to a party direction without prior permission and without condonation within the prescribed period.

Whips are not presiding officers and do not exercise the constitutional powers of the Speaker or Chairman. Their role arises from party organisation and parliamentary practice.

The use of party whips raises a continuing question: how can party discipline and government stability be reconciled with independent legislative deliberation and constituency representation?

16. Presiding Officers and Parliamentary Committees

Presiding officers have important responsibilities in the parliamentary committee system. These may include nominating members, constituting committees, referring Bills or matters for examination and ensuring that committee procedures operate under the applicable rules.

The Speaker and Chairman exercise these functions in relation to their respective Houses and the committees associated with them. Some committees are joint committees with members drawn from both Houses.

Committees enable detailed scrutiny that may not be possible during floor debates. The impartiality and procedural fairness of presiding officers are therefore important to effective legislative oversight.

17. Challenges to the Independence of Presiding Officers

  • Political party affiliations may create perceptions of bias in procedural decisions.
  • Delays in deciding disqualification petitions can affect government formation and legislative outcomes.
  • Disputes over Money Bill certification can affect the constitutional balance between the two Houses.
  • Frequent disruptions and declining deliberative time increase pressure on presiding officers.
  • Inconsistent application of disciplinary rules can undermine confidence in parliamentary neutrality.
  • Vacancies in the office of Deputy Speaker can weaken institutional balance and continuity.
  • Limited public reasoning in certain procedural decisions can reduce transparency and trust.

18. Reforms and Institutional Safeguards

  • Develop stronger conventions and institutional safeguards to support the impartiality of presiding officers.
  • Ensure timely election of Deputy Speakers and Deputy Chairmen.
  • Consider an independent adjudicatory mechanism for defection disputes, consistent with the constitutional framework.
  • Provide reasoned and transparent decisions on important procedural and disqualification questions.
  • Strengthen parliamentary secretariats and non-partisan research support.
  • Improve the consistency and transparency of disciplinary procedures.
  • Protect the rights of opposition members and smaller parties to participate in parliamentary business.
  • Promote training and continuing procedural development for members and parliamentary staff.

Institutional reform should protect the authority of the presiding officer while ensuring that procedural discretion is exercised fairly, transparently and consistently with constitutional values.

20. Important Judicial Decisions

  • Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992): established that decisions under the Tenth Schedule are subject to judicial review on recognised grounds.
  • Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016): examined the constitutional position of a presiding officer and the limits on the exercise of powers when a removal notice is pending.
  • Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020): highlighted the need for timely decisions in defection cases and discussed possible institutional alternatives.
  • Subhash Desai v. Principal Secretary, Governor of Maharashtra (2023): clarified aspects of the constitutional role of the Speaker in political and disqualification disputes.
  • Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024): clarified that legislative privilege does not immunise bribery connected with a vote or speech from criminal prosecution.

21. Integrated Understanding

Presiding officers are essential to the constitutional functioning of Parliament. They translate procedural rules into an orderly process of representation, debate, decision-making and accountability.

The Speaker and Chairman have different constitutional origins and powers, but both must protect the institutional dignity of their Houses. Their legitimacy depends on impartiality, reasoned decisions and respect for the rights of members.

The wider parliamentary administrative framework—including Deputy Presiding Officers, Secretariats, Secretaries-General, Leaders of the House and Opposition, and party whips—supports the functioning of the legislature through distinct constitutional, statutory and conventional roles.
