1. Constitutional Position and Significance

State Legislatures are the representative law-making institutions at the State level. They enact laws on subjects within the legislative competence of States, authorise State expenditure, deliberate on public policy and hold the State executive politically accountable.

Part VI of the Constitution, particularly Articles 168–212, provides the principal framework governing State Legislatures. The constitutional design permits both unicameral and bicameral legislatures.

  • Unicameral legislature: consists of the Legislative Assembly alone.
  • Bicameral legislature: consists of the Legislative Assembly and the Legislative Council.

Unlike Parliament, where bicameralism is constitutionally prescribed, the Constitution allows a State to have either one or two Houses, subject to the procedure under Article 169 for creating or abolishing a Legislative Council.

State Legislatures are important to Indian federalism because they translate constitutional distribution of powers into representative law-making, State-level financial control and democratic accountability.

3. Composition of State Legislatures: Article 168

Article 168 provides that every State shall have a Legislature consisting of the Governor and either:

  • One House, known as the Legislative Assembly; or
  • Two Houses, known as the Legislative Assembly and Legislative Council.

The Governor is a constituent part of the State Legislature for constitutional purposes, but is not a member of either House. The Governor participates in the legislative process through summoning, prorogation, dissolution of the Assembly, addresses, messages, assent and reservation of Bills, as provided by the Constitution.

The Legislative Assembly is the directly elected popular House. Where a Legislative Council exists, it is a continuing second chamber with a mixed composition of elected and nominated members.

4. Legislative Assembly: Composition and Representation

Article 170 governs the composition of State Legislative Assemblies.

  • The Assembly ordinarily consists of not more than 500 and not less than 60 members.
  • Members are chosen by direct election from territorial constituencies within the State.
  • Constituencies are organised on the basis of population, subject to constitutional provisions governing delimitation and readjustment.
  • Parliament may provide exceptions to the ordinary numerical limits for specified States under the Constitution.

Article 170 also provides the constitutional framework for readjustment of constituencies following census-related processes. The Constitution contains specific provisions affecting the timing of readjustment and the relationship between population representation and delimitation.

Article 332 provides for reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in State Legislative Assemblies, in accordance with the constitutional framework.

The earlier provision permitting nomination of an Anglo-Indian member to a State Assembly under Article 333 ceased to operate following the 104th Constitutional Amendment, which extended SC/ST legislative reservation while discontinuing that special nomination arrangement.

5. Legislative Council: Composition and Character

The Legislative Council is the upper House of a bicameral State Legislature. It is a continuing chamber and is not subject to dissolution.

Article 171 governs its composition. The total membership of a Legislative Council:

  • Shall not exceed one-third of the total membership of the State Legislative Assembly.
  • Shall not ordinarily be less than 40 members.

The Constitution provides for a mixed composition designed to represent local bodies, graduates, teachers, members of the Legislative Assembly and persons with special knowledge or practical experience.

6. Creation and Abolition of Legislative Councils: Article 169

Article 169 enables Parliament to create or abolish a Legislative Council in a State. It reflects the flexible nature of State-level bicameralism.

Procedure

  1. The Legislative Assembly of the concerned State passes a resolution seeking creation or abolition of its Legislative Council.
  2. The resolution must be supported by a majority of the total membership of the Assembly.
  3. It must also be supported by a majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  4. Parliament may then enact a law to create or abolish the Council.

The resolution of the State Assembly is a constitutional prerequisite, but it does not itself create or abolish the Council. Parliament must pass the necessary law.

A law under Article 169 may contain provisions for the composition, representation and related matters of the Legislative Council. Such a law is not deemed to be a constitutional amendment for the purposes of Article 368.

Consequently, it can be passed by Parliament through the ordinary legislative procedure rather than the special majority procedure under Article 368.

Constitutional significance

  • Allows States to choose whether a second chamber is institutionally desirable.
  • Preserves Parliament's role in changing the constitutional structure of a State Legislature.
  • Combines State-level political consent with national legislative action.
  • Demonstrates that the existence of State Legislative Councils is not an unalterable constitutional requirement.

7. States with Bicameral Legislatures

As of November 2026, the States with Legislative Councils are:

  • Andhra Pradesh
  • Telangana
  • Karnataka
  • Maharashtra
  • Bihar
  • Uttar Pradesh

The remaining States have unicameral legislatures. The number and composition of Legislative Councils may change through the procedure prescribed under Article 169.

8. Duration of the Legislative Assembly and Council

Legislative Assembly: Article 172

  • The normal term is five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved.
  • It may be dissolved earlier by the Governor within the constitutional framework of the parliamentary system.
  • While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, Parliament may extend its term by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time.
  • Such an extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Emergency has ceased to operate.

Legislative Council: Article 172

  • It is a permanent House and is not subject to dissolution.
  • As nearly as possible, one-third of its members retire every second year.
  • The term of an individual member is ordinarily six years, subject to the constitutional and statutory framework.

The continuing character of the Council ensures institutional continuity, while the Assembly remains directly accountable to the electorate through periodic elections.

9. Qualifications for Membership

Article 173 prescribes the constitutional qualifications for membership of a State Legislature.

  • The person must be a citizen of India.
  • The person must make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation before an authorised person.
  • For a Legislative Assembly, the person must be at least 25 years old.
  • For a Legislative Council, the person must be at least 30 years old.
  • The person must possess other qualifications prescribed by or under a law made by Parliament.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 and related election laws provide further requirements governing eligibility and candidature.

10. Disqualifications and Vacation of Seats

Article 191 specifies grounds on which a person may be disqualified from being chosen as, or for being, a member of a State Legislature.

  • Holding an office of profit under the Union or a State Government, unless exempted by law.
  • Being of unsound mind and so declared by a competent court.
  • Being an undischarged insolvent.
  • Not being a citizen of India, or voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign State, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
  • Being disqualified by or under a law made by Parliament.
  • Being disqualified under the Tenth Schedule on grounds of defection.

Decision-making authority

Under Article 192, a question arises whether a member has become subject to a disqualification under Article 191(1). The Governor decides the question after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission and is required to act according to that opinion.

For disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, the decision is made by the Speaker or Chairman of the relevant House, subject to judicial review under the constitutional framework.

Article 190 deals with vacation of seats, including circumstances involving dual membership, resignation and other constitutionally prescribed situations.

11. Sessions, Summoning and Prorogation

Article 174 empowers the Governor to summon each House or both Houses of the State Legislature, prorogue the House or Houses and dissolve the Legislative Assembly.

  • The Governor acts within the constitutional framework governing the parliamentary executive.
  • There cannot be a gap of more than six months between the last sitting in one session and the first sitting in the next session.
  • Prorogation terminates a session.
  • Dissolution ends the life of the Legislative Assembly.
  • The Legislative Council is not dissolved when the Assembly is dissolved.

Article 175 allows the Governor to address and send messages to the House or Houses. Article 176 provides for the Governor's special address at the commencement of the first session after each general election to the Assembly and at the first session of each year.

The Governor's address outlines the policies and programmes of the State Government. The discussion on the address provides an important opportunity for legislative scrutiny of executive priorities.

12. Presiding Officers of State Legislatures

Speaker and Deputy Speaker of the Assembly: Article 178

Every Legislative Assembly elects a Speaker and a Deputy Speaker from among its members.

The Speaker performs important constitutional and procedural functions:

  • Presides over sittings of the Legislative Assembly.
  • Maintains order and ensures observance of procedural rules.
  • Interprets and applies the Rules of Procedure.
  • Decides questions relating to admissibility of motions and other parliamentary business.
  • Certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill under the applicable constitutional procedure.
  • Exercises functions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.
  • Constitutes or nominates members to committees as provided by the rules.

Article 179 provides for vacation, resignation and removal from office. The Speaker may be removed by a resolution of the Assembly passed by a majority of all the then members of the Assembly, after at least fourteen days' notice.

Under Article 181, the Speaker or Deputy Speaker cannot preside while a resolution for their own removal is under consideration, although the Constitution permits participation and voting in the circumstances specified by that Article.

Chairman and Deputy Chairman of the Council: Article 182

Where a Legislative Council exists, it elects a Chairman and Deputy Chairman from among its members. Their procedural role in the Council broadly corresponds to that of the Speaker and Deputy Speaker in the Assembly.

Articles 183 and 184 regulate vacation, resignation, removal and the performance of the Chairman's duties when the office is vacant or the Chairman is absent.

13. Oath, Voting and Quorum

  • Article 188: requires members to make and subscribe the prescribed oath or affirmation before taking their seats.
  • Article 189: regulates voting, vacancies and quorum.
  • Article 190: deals with vacation of seats.
  • Article 191: specifies disqualifications for membership.
  • Article 192: provides for decision on specified disqualification questions by the Governor after obtaining the Election Commission's opinion.
  • Article 193: provides for penalties for sitting and voting before taking the required oath or when not qualified or disqualified.

Unless otherwise provided by the Constitution, questions are decided by a majority of members present and voting. The presiding officer ordinarily has a casting vote in the event of equality of votes.

The quorum is ten members or one-tenth of the total number of members of the House, whichever is greater, unless the State Legislature provides otherwise by law.

14. Legislative Powers and Competence

State Legislatures exercise law-making powers within the constitutional distribution of legislative subjects between the Union and States.

State List

Under Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule, State Legislatures ordinarily have exclusive legislative competence over matters in the State List, subject to constitutional exceptions.

Concurrent List

Both Parliament and State Legislatures may legislate on subjects in the Concurrent List. In the event of repugnancy, Article 254 generally gives precedence to Parliamentary law, subject to the exception for a State law reserved for and receiving Presidential assent under Article 254(2). Parliament may subsequently override such a State law.

Residuary powers

Under Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List, Parliament has residuary legislative competence over matters not enumerated in the State List or Concurrent List, including the power to impose taxes not mentioned in those Lists.

Territorial extent

Under Article 245, the Legislature of a State may make laws for the whole or any part of the State, subject to the Constitution.

15. Circumstances in Which Parliament Can Legislate on State List Matters

The Constitution permits Parliament to legislate on State List subjects in specified circumstances. These provisions qualify the ordinary distribution of legislative powers.

Article Circumstance

These exceptions enable national coordination and constitutional responses to exceptional situations while preserving the ordinary division of legislative competence.

16. Ordinary Legislative Procedure

Articles 196–200 govern important aspects of the legislative process in State Legislatures.

Introduction and passage

  • An ordinary Bill may originate in either House of a bicameral State Legislature, subject to constitutional restrictions.
  • It must be passed by the House or Houses in accordance with the applicable rules and procedure.
  • In a bicameral State, the Legislative Council may delay or recommend changes to an ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly.
  • The Assembly has the ultimate legislative advantage in resolving disagreement over ordinary Bills under Article 197.

Role of the Legislative Council

When an ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly is sent to the Council, the Council may:

  • Pass the Bill without amendments.
  • Suggest amendments, which the Assembly may accept or reject.
  • Reject the Bill.
  • Fail to pass the Bill within the prescribed period.

If the Council rejects the Bill, proposes amendments unacceptable to the Assembly or fails to pass it within three months, the Assembly may pass it again. If the Council then rejects it, proposes amendments unacceptable to the Assembly or fails to pass it within one month, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form approved by the Assembly.

There is no provision for a joint sitting of the two Houses of a State Legislature to resolve a deadlock over an ordinary Bill.

17. Money Bills and Financial Powers

Articles 198 and 199 govern Money Bills in a bicameral State Legislature. The Legislative Assembly has a dominant role in financial legislation.

Definition of Money Bill

Article 199 specifies when a Bill is deemed to be a Money Bill. It must contain only provisions dealing with one or more of the matters specified in that Article, such as taxation, borrowing, custody and withdrawal of money from the Consolidated Fund, appropriation and related matters.

Procedure in a bicameral State

  • A Money Bill cannot be introduced in the Legislative Council.
  • After passage by the Assembly, it is transmitted to the Council for recommendations.
  • The Council must return it with recommendations within fourteen days.
  • The Assembly may accept or reject any or all recommendations.
  • If the Council does not return the Bill within fourteen days, it is deemed to have been passed by both Houses in the form passed by the Assembly.

The Speaker of the Legislative Assembly certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill under Article 199(3), and the certificate is endorsed when the Bill is transmitted to the Council and presented for assent.

The Council cannot veto or indefinitely delay a Money Bill.

18. State Budget and Control over Public Expenditure

Articles 202–207 establish the framework for State financial procedure.

  • Article 202: provides for the Annual Financial Statement, commonly called the State Budget.
  • Article 203: governs voting on Demands for Grants by the Legislative Assembly.
  • Article 204: provides for Appropriation Bills authorising withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Article 205: provides for supplementary, additional and excess grants.
  • Article 206: provides for votes on account, votes of credit and exceptional grants.
  • Article 207: contains special provisions relating to Financial Bills.

Demands for Grants are submitted to the Legislative Assembly. The Legislative Council may discuss the budget but does not vote on Demands for Grants.

The Assembly may exercise financial control through discussion, voting, cut motions and scrutiny of expenditure. The Appropriation Act provides the legal authority for withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund of the State.

19. Governor's Assent and Reservation of Bills

Article 200 governs the Governor's action when a Bill passed by the State Legislature is presented for assent.

The Governor may:

  • Give assent to the Bill.
  • Withhold assent.
  • Reserve the Bill for consideration of the President.
  • In the case of a Bill other than a Money Bill, return it to the House or Houses with a message requesting reconsideration of the Bill or specified provisions.

If the Bill is passed again by the House or Houses, with or without amendments, and presented again, the constitutional text requires the Governor not to withhold assent. The proviso also states that the Governor shall not assent to, but shall reserve for the consideration of the President, a Bill which in the Governor's opinion would, if it became law, so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is designed to fill by the Constitution.

Article 201 governs the President's consideration of a Bill reserved for assent. The President may assent or withhold assent. In the case of a non-Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature for reconsideration. If the Bill is passed again within six months and presented to the President, the President is not constitutionally bound to assent.

The exercise of gubernatorial powers must be assessed in light of the constitutional text, the nature of the function and binding judicial decisions concerning constitutional offices and federalism.

20. Privileges of State Legislatures

Article 194 provides for the powers, privileges and immunities of State Legislatures, their members and committees.

Important aspects include:

  • Freedom of speech in the Legislature, subject to the Constitution and the rules and standing orders regulating procedure.
  • Protection from court proceedings in respect of anything said or any vote given in the Legislature or its committees, within the constitutional scope of the provision.
  • Other powers, privileges and immunities determined by the constitutional framework and applicable law.
  • Protection of legislative proceedings and institutional functioning.

Article 194 must be read with constitutional supremacy, fundamental rights and the rule of law. Privilege is intended to protect legislative functioning, not to create an unrestricted personal immunity.

In Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024), the Supreme Court held that legislative privilege does not protect a legislator from criminal prosecution for bribery connected with a vote or speech. The judgment also overruled the contrary approach in the earlier P.V. Narasimha Rao case on this issue.

21. Anti-Defection and State Legislatures

The Tenth Schedule applies to members of State Legislative Assemblies and Legislative Councils. It provides for disqualification on grounds of defection.

Important grounds include:

  • Voluntarily giving up membership of the political party on whose ticket the member was elected.
  • Voting or abstaining from voting contrary to a party direction or whip, without prior permission and without condonation within the prescribed period.
  • Independent members joining a political party after election.
  • Nominated members joining a political party after the constitutionally permitted period.

The Speaker or Chairman decides disqualification questions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.

The law seeks to promote political stability and prevent opportunistic defections, but raises concerns about party control, legislative independence and the impartiality of the adjudicating authority.

22. Legislative Committees at the State Level

State Legislatures use committees to conduct detailed examination of expenditure, legislation, administrative performance and other matters. Their structure and functions are governed by the rules and practices of the respective Houses.

Important committee categories include:

  • Public Accounts Committee: examines public expenditure and relevant audit reports of the Comptroller and Auditor General.
  • Estimates Committee: examines estimates and suggests improvements in economy and efficiency.
  • Public Undertakings Committee: examines selected public undertakings and their performance.
  • Committee on Subordinate Legislation: examines rules, regulations and other delegated legislation.
  • Committee on Government Assurances: reviews assurances and promises made by ministers.
  • Privileges and Ethics Committees: examine matters falling within their respective mandates.

Committees strengthen legislative scrutiny by enabling members to examine complex issues in greater detail than is usually possible during full House proceedings.

23. Legislative Council: Role and Limitations

The Legislative Council is intended to provide a revising chamber that can examine legislation from a broader and sometimes more specialised perspective.

Potential contributions

  • Provides a second forum for legislative deliberation.
  • Includes representation through local authorities, graduates and teachers.
  • Allows participation of persons nominated for special knowledge or practical experience.
  • Provides continuity because it is a permanent House.
  • May identify drafting weaknesses and unintended consequences in Bills.

Constitutional limitations

  • It cannot permanently block an ordinary Bill passed by the Assembly.
  • It cannot originate a Money Bill.
  • It cannot vote on Demands for Grants.
  • It cannot remove the State Council of Ministers through a no-confidence motion.
  • Its composition is partly indirect and partly nominated rather than based entirely on direct popular election.

The Council is therefore a revising and deliberative chamber, not an equal counterpart to the directly elected Assembly in all legislative and financial matters.

25. Legislative Accountability of the State Executive

Article 164(2) provides that the State Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly. This is the foundation of parliamentary executive accountability at the State level.

Legislative instruments of accountability include:

  • Question Hour and supplementary questions.
  • Zero Hour and other discussion procedures.
  • Confidence and no-confidence motions.
  • Debates on the Governor's address.
  • Budget discussions, cut motions and voting on Demands for Grants.
  • Committee scrutiny and examination of CAG reports.
  • Legislative oversight of delegated legislation and government assurances.

Although both Houses may deliberate on government policies, the Legislative Assembly has the decisive role in testing the political confidence of the State Council of Ministers.

26. Relationship with Parliament and the Union

State Legislatures function within a federal constitutional structure. Their legislative authority is protected by the distribution of powers, but it is subject to specified constitutional limitations and Union legislative powers.

Important connections include:

  • Article 246: distributes legislative competence through the Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
  • Article 249: enables Parliament to legislate on State List matters following a Rajya Sabha resolution in the national interest.
  • Article 252: permits Parliament to legislate for consenting States on specified State List matters.
  • Article 254: governs repugnancy between Union and State laws on Concurrent List subjects.
  • Article 356: permits constitutional machinery provisions to operate in a State under specified circumstances.
  • Article 200: provides for the Governor's action on State Bills, including reservation for the President.

These provisions demonstrate that State Legislatures are autonomous within their constitutional field, but do not operate independently of the wider constitutional order.

27. Challenges Facing State Legislatures

  • Limited sitting days: insufficient time restricts legislative debate, questions and scrutiny.
  • Executive dominance: the government may control the legislative agenda through its majority and scheduling of business.
  • Disruptions: repeated interruptions reduce the quality of deliberation and oversight.
  • Rushed law-making: Bills may be passed without adequate committee examination or public consultation.
  • Weak committee capacity: inadequate research, staff and technical support constrain scrutiny.
  • Party control: strong whip systems and anti-defection rules may limit independent deliberation.
  • Financial dependence: limited fiscal autonomy can constrain the ability of States to translate legislative priorities into public programmes.
  • Governor–State Government disputes: disagreement over assent, reservation, summoning and constitutional functions may create institutional friction.
  • Uneven legislative research: members may lack timely access to reliable data and specialist advice.
  • Public participation gaps: consultation and disclosure may be insufficient in the formulation and scrutiny of legislation.

28. Reforms to Strengthen State Legislatures

  • Provide a predictable legislative calendar and adequate sitting days.
  • Refer important Bills to committees for detailed scrutiny wherever appropriate.
  • Strengthen legislative research services, libraries and technical support.
  • Improve the quality and accessibility of budgetary and departmental information.
  • Institutionalise meaningful public consultation on major Bills.
  • Ensure timely follow-up on committee recommendations and audit findings.
  • Promote impartiality and procedural fairness in the functioning of presiding officers.
  • Improve opportunities for opposition members to raise public issues and scrutinise the executive.
  • Encourage constructive debate and reduce disruptions.
  • Assess the need for Legislative Councils on institutional merit rather than short-term political considerations.
  • Strengthen legislative capacity to examine delegated legislation and the performance of public institutions.

31. Integrated Understanding

State Legislatures are central to representative government and federal democracy. The Legislative Assembly provides direct electoral representation and holds the State executive politically accountable. The Legislative Council, where established, offers continuity and a second forum for legislative examination.

The Constitution gives State Legislatures law-making authority within their allocated fields, control over State finances and institutional mechanisms for executive scrutiny. Their functioning is shaped by the relationship between the Governor, the Council of Ministers, presiding officers, political parties, committees and the Union constitutional framework.

The quality of State-level democracy depends not merely on the existence of legislative institutions but on their ability to deliberate, scrutinise, legislate and represent citizens effectively. Strengthening their institutional capacity is therefore essential to cooperative federalism, accountable governance and constitutional democracy.
