1. Meaning and Constitutional Foundation

Parliamentary accountability is the constitutional and political obligation of the executive to explain, justify and defend its decisions and actions before the legislature. It enables elected representatives to scrutinise public policy, question administrative performance, examine expenditure and, where constitutionally appropriate, withdraw political support from the government.

In India's parliamentary system, accountability is not confined to elections. It operates continuously through parliamentary questions, debates, motions, financial scrutiny, committees, audit reports and legislative procedures.

The central constitutional foundation is Article 75(3), which provides that the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People. At the State level, Article 164(2) establishes collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Legislative Assembly.

Parliamentary accountability serves several purposes:

  • Ensures that the executive remains answerable to elected representatives.
  • Provides information about government decisions and their implementation.
  • Enables scrutiny of public expenditure and administrative performance.
  • Protects democratic debate and opposition participation.
  • Helps identify policy failures, irregularities and institutional weaknesses.
  • Strengthens transparency, responsiveness and public trust.

2. Collective Responsibility: The Core Principle

Article 75(3) makes the Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha. This principle means that the ministry functions as a politically accountable unit and must retain the confidence of the directly elected House.

Key features

  • Common responsibility: ministers are collectively accountable for the broad policies and decisions of the government.
  • Common fate: if the government loses the confidence of the Lok Sabha, the Council of Ministers is expected to resign as a whole.
  • Cabinet solidarity: ministers are ordinarily expected to publicly support Cabinet decisions or leave office if they cannot do so.
  • Confidentiality: Cabinet deliberations are generally protected to enable candid collective decision-making.
  • Legislative answerability: ministers must answer questions and defend government policies in Parliament.

Collective responsibility does not eliminate the individual accountability of ministers for their departments. It combines collective political responsibility with ministerial responsibility for departmental administration.

3. Individual Ministerial Responsibility

Individual ministerial responsibility refers to the political accountability of a minister for the functioning of the ministry or department under their charge, as well as their conduct in public office.

In India's constitutional framework, Article 75(2) provides that ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President. In practice, this operates within the parliamentary system and the advice of the Prime Minister. The Prime Minister exercises a central role in determining the composition and continuation of the Council of Ministers.

Individual responsibility may arise in circumstances involving:

  • Serious administrative failure or departmental negligence.
  • Misconduct or loss of public confidence.
  • Failure to implement government policy effectively.
  • Misleading statements or failure to provide accurate information to Parliament.
  • Loss of confidence of the Prime Minister or political leadership.

Unlike collective responsibility, the resignation of an individual minister does not necessarily require the resignation of the entire Council of Ministers.

4. Parliamentary Questions

Parliamentary questions are among the most direct instruments through which members obtain information from the executive. They enable scrutiny of policy, administration, expenditure and implementation.

Type Nature of answer Accountability value
Starred question Oral answer Allows supplementary questions and immediate clarification.
Unstarred question Written answer Creates a detailed written record of government information.
Short-notice question Oral answer on an urgent matter, subject to admission Enables the House to seek information on an urgent public issue.

Question Hour is a structured mechanism for executive answerability. Members may seek information about decisions, expenditure, programme outcomes, administrative delays and public grievances.

Its effectiveness depends on relevant answers, reliable data, sufficient time for supplementary questions and meaningful follow-up by members.

5. Zero Hour and Other Discussion Mechanisms

Zero Hour allows members to raise matters of urgent public importance through parliamentary practice. It is not expressly mentioned in the Constitution and is not originally a formal device prescribed by the Rules of Procedure.

Other discussion mechanisms also contribute to accountability:

  • Short Duration Discussion: enables discussion of a matter of urgent public importance without a formal motion being put to vote.
  • Calling Attention: enables a member to draw a minister's attention to an urgent matter and seek a statement.
  • Half-an-Hour Discussion: permits further clarification on a matter arising from an answer to a parliamentary question.
  • Special Mention and related procedures: provide opportunities to raise matters of public importance under the rules of the relevant House.

These procedures help Parliament respond to emerging concerns, although their impact depends on the quality of ministerial responses and subsequent action.

6. Motions as Instruments of Political Accountability

Motions enable the House to express its opinion, demand explanations or formally test the government's political support.

No-confidence motion

A no-confidence motion in the Lok Sabha tests whether the Council of Ministers continues to enjoy the confidence of the House. Its constitutional basis lies in the principle of collective responsibility under Article 75(3).

  • It is a mechanism against the Council of Ministers as a whole.
  • It is moved in the Lok Sabha, not the Rajya Sabha.
  • Admission, notice and discussion are regulated by the Lok Sabha Rules of Procedure.
  • If passed, the government is expected to resign.

Confidence motion

A confidence motion is moved by the government to establish that it commands the support of the Lok Sabha. It may be used when the government's majority is uncertain or when political circumstances require a formal test.

Censure motion

A censure motion expresses disapproval of a specific government policy, action or failure. It differs from a no-confidence motion because it ordinarily identifies the grounds for criticism and does not automatically constitute a formal withdrawal of confidence in the entire Council of Ministers.

Adjournment motion

An adjournment motion in the Lok Sabha seeks to interrupt normal business to discuss a definite matter of urgent public importance. Its admissibility is subject to strict procedural conditions.

Other motions and resolutions

Substantive motions, statutory resolutions, private members' resolutions and other procedural devices allow Parliament to express views, scrutinise government conduct and require political explanations.

7. Debates and Legislative Deliberation

Parliamentary debates enable members to examine government policies, challenge executive reasoning, propose alternatives and represent public concerns.

Important occasions for accountability include:

  • Discussion on the President's Address under Article 87.
  • Debates on government Bills and proposed legislation.
  • Discussion on the Union Budget and Demands for Grants.
  • Debates on public emergencies, national issues and major policy decisions.
  • Discussion on reports of parliamentary committees and other statutory or constitutional authorities when presented to Parliament.

The quality of debate matters as much as the number of proceedings. Meaningful scrutiny requires access to information, adequate time, reasoned responses and the opportunity for opposition members to question the government.

8. Financial Accountability and Parliamentary Control

Parliament exercises control over public finance through constitutional procedures governing taxation, expenditure, appropriation and audit. The executive cannot treat public funds as its own resources; expenditure must be authorised and accounted for within the constitutional framework.

The Lok Sabha has the primary role in voting on Demands for Grants. The Rajya Sabha may discuss the budget but does not vote on Demands for Grants. The constitutional division of financial powers reflects the principle that the directly elected House controls the government's expenditure authorisation.

9. Cut Motions and Budgetary Scrutiny

Cut motions enable members of the Lok Sabha to express disapproval of demands for grants and scrutinise government expenditure.

Type Purpose Proposed reduction
Policy cut Expresses disapproval of the policy underlying a demand. Reduces the demand to Re. 1.
Economy cut Suggests economy in expenditure. Reduces the demand by a specified amount.
Token cut Ventilates a specific grievance within the government's responsibility. Reduces the demand by Rs. 100.

Cut motions provide an opportunity to scrutinise individual demands and connect expenditure decisions with policy performance. Their practical use is affected by party discipline, the government's majority and the limited time available for discussion.

10. Parliamentary Committees and Continuous Oversight

Parliamentary committees provide specialised scrutiny that cannot always be undertaken effectively in the full House. They examine public expenditure, departmental performance, legislative proposals, delegated legislation and specific issues referred to them.

Principal financial committees

  • Public Accounts Committee: examines appropriation accounts, finance accounts and relevant CAG audit reports to assess whether public money was legally and properly spent.
  • Estimates Committee: examines estimates and suggests improvements in economy, efficiency and administrative organisation.
  • Committee on Public Undertakings: examines selected public undertakings, their accounts, performance and relevant audit findings.

Department-Related Standing Committees

DRSCs examine Demands for Grants, referred Bills, annual reports and policy matters relating to assigned ministries and departments. They provide an institutional mechanism for detailed scrutiny across government sectors.

Other oversight committees

  • Committee on Government Assurances monitors promises and undertakings given by ministers.
  • Committee on Subordinate Legislation examines the exercise of delegated legislative powers.
  • Committee of Privileges examines questions involving breach of parliamentary privilege.
  • Committee on Petitions considers petitions and grievances referred under parliamentary procedure.
  • Committee on Ethics examines matters relating to members' ethical conduct.

Committee recommendations are generally not binding in the manner of court orders. Their influence depends on parliamentary scrutiny, public reasoning and the government's response.

11. CAG Audit and Legislative Accountability

The Comptroller and Auditor General of India is a constitutional authority established under Article 148. The CAG audits public accounts and submits reports in accordance with constitutional and statutory provisions.

Under Article 151:

  • Reports relating to the accounts of the Union are submitted to the President, who causes them to be laid before each House of Parliament.
  • Reports relating to the accounts of a State are submitted to the Governor, who causes them to be laid before the State Legislature.

Audit supports parliamentary accountability by providing independent examination of public expenditure, compliance, financial management and performance.

The relationship between the CAG and PAC is particularly important. The CAG's audit findings provide evidence for the PAC's examination, while the committee enables the legislature to question departments and seek corrective action.

12. Control over Delegated Legislation

Parliament often delegates rule-making powers to the executive because modern governance involves technical and detailed matters that cannot all be prescribed in primary legislation.

Delegated legislation includes rules, regulations, orders, notifications and other instruments made under authority granted by an Act of Parliament.

Parliamentary control is important because delegated legislation can substantially affect rights, duties, procedures and administrative powers.

Mechanisms of control include:

  • Laying requirements under the parent Act, where prescribed.
  • Examination by the Committee on Subordinate Legislation.
  • Parliamentary questions and debates concerning the exercise of delegated powers.
  • Amendment or repeal of the parent legislation by Parliament.
  • Judicial review where delegated legislation exceeds statutory authority or violates constitutional requirements.

Effective oversight requires that delegated powers remain within the limits and purposes of the enabling legislation.

13. Parliamentary Privileges and Accountability

Article 105 recognises the powers, privileges and immunities of Parliament and its members, while Article 194 contains the corresponding provisions for State Legislatures.

Privileges protect the independence and effective functioning of legislative institutions. They include freedom of speech in Parliament, subject to constitutional provisions and House rules, and protections relating to parliamentary proceedings.

Privileges are not a general exemption from all law or a substitute for public accountability. Their exercise must be understood in the context of constitutional supremacy, institutional independence and the rule of law.

The Supreme Court's decision in Sita Soren v. Union of India (2024) clarified that legislative privilege does not confer immunity from criminal prosecution for bribery connected with a legislator's vote or speech. The Court overruled the contrary approach in the earlier P.V. Narasimha Rao decision on this issue.

14. Opposition and Parliamentary Accountability

The opposition is essential to the functioning of parliamentary democracy. It provides an alternative political voice, questions executive decisions and brings public concerns into legislative deliberation.

Its accountability role includes:

  • Questioning ministers and seeking information.
  • Scrutinising Bills, budgets and policy decisions.
  • Moving motions and participating in debates.
  • Highlighting administrative failures and possible irregularities.
  • Participating in parliamentary committees.
  • Presenting alternative policy approaches and representing dissenting views.

The office of the Leader of the Opposition is recognised under the Salary and Allowances of Leaders of Opposition in Parliament Act, 1977. The Act defines the Leader of the Opposition by reference to the leader of the party in opposition to the government with the greatest numerical strength in the relevant House, recognised as such by the Speaker or Chairman, as applicable.

The opposition's effectiveness depends on adequate parliamentary time, access to information, fair procedural treatment and the ability to participate meaningfully in legislative scrutiny.

15. Parliamentary Accountability at the State Level

The principles of parliamentary accountability also apply to State governments. Under Article 164(2), the Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the Legislative Assembly of the State.

State Legislatures exercise oversight through:

  • Questions and debates addressed to State ministers.
  • Confidence and no-confidence procedures in the Legislative Assembly.
  • Budgetary scrutiny and voting on Demands for Grants.
  • Public Accounts and other legislative committees.
  • Examination of CAG reports relating to State accounts.
  • Oversight of State departments, public undertakings and delegated legislation.

Where a State has a Legislative Council, its role in legislative scrutiny and deliberation operates within the constitutional distribution of powers between the two Houses. The Legislative Assembly remains central to the political accountability of the State Council of Ministers.

16. Constitutional Accountability and Judicial Review

Parliamentary accountability operates alongside judicial review and other constitutional mechanisms. The legislature scrutinises the executive politically and financially, while courts examine legality and constitutional compliance in cases properly brought before them.

Important distinctions include:

  • Parliamentary scrutiny does not replace judicial determination of legal rights.
  • Judicial review does not ordinarily substitute courts for Parliament in political accountability.
  • Constitutional authorities such as the CAG provide independent institutional checks.
  • Statutory bodies and regulators may contribute to accountability within their legal mandates.
  • Public access to information and civil society scrutiny supplement formal constitutional institutions.

Article 122 protects parliamentary proceedings from being questioned in court merely on the ground of procedural irregularity. It does not create an unrestricted immunity for unconstitutional or substantively unlawful action. The constitutional relationship between legislative privilege, procedural autonomy and judicial review must be assessed in light of the relevant constitutional provisions and judicial decisions.

17. Anti-Defection and the Quality of Accountability

The Tenth Schedule regulates disqualification on grounds of defection. It seeks to discourage political defections and promote stability, but its operation has implications for legislative deliberation and executive accountability.

Potential tensions include:

  • Party discipline may restrict the ability of legislators to express independent views.
  • Whips may reduce the scope for issue-based voting and deliberation.
  • Disqualification proceedings may create disputes over the neutrality and timing of decisions by the presiding officer.
  • Political majorities may become less responsive to individual legislative scrutiny.

In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the Tenth Schedule while recognising judicial review of decisions made by the presiding officer under it, subject to the limits explained by the Court.

In Keisham Meghachandra Singh v. Speaker, Manipur Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court highlighted the need for timely decisions in disqualification proceedings and suggested that Parliament consider an independent mechanism.

18. Limitations in the Parliamentary Accountability Framework

  • Disruptions: repeated interruptions reduce time for questions, debates and legislative scrutiny.
  • Rushed legislation: limited debate and inadequate committee examination may weaken the quality of law-making.
  • Party dominance: strong party control and majority discipline may constrain independent legislative scrutiny.
  • Limited sitting days: insufficient parliamentary time restricts the opportunity to examine government policy and expenditure.
  • Weak follow-up: recommendations and assurances may not always result in timely corrective action.
  • Information gaps: members may lack timely access to reliable data and independent research support.
  • Executive dominance: control over legislative scheduling and government business can limit the space available for opposition scrutiny.
  • Committee capacity: inadequate specialist support and time can affect the depth of examination.
  • Declining deliberation: adversarial political practices may reduce reasoned debate and cross-party engagement.

19. Measures to Strengthen Parliamentary Accountability

  • Predictable parliamentary calendar: establish adequate and regular sitting days to enable sustained scrutiny.
  • Stronger committee system: refer important Bills and policy proposals for detailed examination wherever appropriate.
  • Protected accountability time: ensure meaningful opportunities for Question Hour, opposition business and discussion of urgent public matters.
  • Better legislative scrutiny: provide adequate time for debate, consultation and examination of amendments.
  • Research support: strengthen non-partisan legislative research and technical assistance for members and committees.
  • Action-taken monitoring: improve tracking of committee recommendations, CAG findings and ministerial assurances.
  • Transparent information: make budgetary, performance and implementation data accessible in usable formats.
  • Presiding officer impartiality: strengthen conventions and institutional safeguards that protect fair parliamentary procedure.
  • Constructive opposition: preserve meaningful space for dissent and alternative policy proposals.
  • Responsible participation: encourage members to balance protest with the constitutional obligation to deliberate and transact business.

22. Distinctions for Conceptual Clarity

Concepts Distinction
Collective vs individual responsibilityCollective responsibility concerns the Council of Ministers as a whole; individual responsibility concerns a particular minister's political accountability.
No-confidence vs censure motionNo-confidence tests support for the government; censure expresses disapproval of specified conduct or policy.
Question Hour vs Zero HourQuestion Hour is a formal question procedure; Zero Hour developed through parliamentary practice.
PAC vs CAGCAG conducts audit and submits reports; PAC examines relevant reports and holds departments accountable through parliamentary scrutiny.
Committee report vs government actionA committee report makes findings and recommendations; implementation requires appropriate action by the government or House.
Political accountability vs judicial accountabilityPolitical accountability is exercised through legislative confidence and scrutiny; judicial accountability concerns legal and constitutional compliance through courts.
Legislative control vs executive administrationParliament authorises, scrutinises and holds the executive accountable; day-to-day administration remains primarily an executive responsibility.

23. Integrated Understanding

Parliamentary accountability is a continuous process rather than a single constitutional event. Collective responsibility establishes the political relationship between the executive and the Lok Sabha. Questions, debates and motions enable members to obtain information and challenge government decisions. Budgetary procedures and audit protect legislative control over public money, while committees provide detailed and sustained scrutiny.

These mechanisms operate alongside constitutional institutions, judicial review, public access to information and democratic participation. Their effectiveness depends on adequate parliamentary time, reliable information, fair procedures, meaningful opposition participation and follow-up on recommendations.

The central governance challenge is to ensure that executive efficiency does not become executive dominance. A functioning parliamentary democracy requires an executive capable of governing and a legislature capable of scrutinising it effectively.
