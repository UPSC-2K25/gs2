1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

The parliamentary executive is a system in which the political executive derives its authority from the legislature and remains collectively accountable to its elected lower House. In India, the Union executive operates within a parliamentary framework under which the President is the constitutional head of the Union, while the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers exercise political and executive leadership.

The system combines a formal head of State with a politically responsible government. It seeks to ensure that executive power is exercised through constitutional institutions and remains answerable to the representatives of the people.

The parliamentary executive is not merely an arrangement for selecting ministers. It connects executive authority with legislative confidence, collective responsibility, ministerial accountability and constitutional limitations.

2. Constitutional Framework

Part V, Chapter I of the Constitution deals with the Union Executive. The principal provisions governing the parliamentary executive include:

  • Article 52: There shall be a President of India.
  • Article 53: The executive power of the Union is vested in the President and is exercised in accordance with the Constitution.
  • Article 74: A Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head shall aid and advise the President.
  • Article 75: Deals with appointment, tenure, responsibility, qualifications and other conditions relating to Union ministers.
  • Article 77: Deals with the conduct of business of the Government of India.
  • Article 78: Prescribes duties of the Prime Minister in relation to furnishing information to the President.

These provisions must be read together. Article 53 identifies the constitutional repository of Union executive power, while Articles 74 and 75 establish the responsible ministerial government through which the executive is politically directed.

3. Parliamentary and Presidential Systems

The parliamentary system differs from a presidential system in the relationship between the executive and legislature.

Basis Parliamentary system Presidential system
Executive leadership Prime Minister heads the political executive; the head of State is generally distinct. President is ordinarily both head of State and head of government.
Relationship with legislature Executive is drawn from and remains accountable to the legislature. Executive and legislature have separately derived mandates.
Tenure Government continues while it enjoys the confidence of the lower House, subject to constitutional rules. Executive ordinarily has a fixed term.
Collective responsibility A central feature of the system. Not ordinarily the basis of executive survival.
Executive-legislative relationship Fusion of political executive and legislature, with institutional checks. Greater formal separation of executive and legislative branches.
Removal of government Loss of confidence may require resignation or another constitutionally appropriate course. Ordinarily through constitutionally prescribed removal procedures, not a routine confidence vote.

The distinction is one of institutional design, not an absolute division between systems. Parliamentary systems also contain checks and balances, while presidential systems require legislative oversight and constitutional accountability.

4. Why India Adopted the Parliamentary System

The Constituent Assembly adopted a parliamentary form of government influenced by India's constitutional experience under colonial legislatures and responsible government institutions. The choice was shaped by the need to establish democratic accountability in a diverse and newly independent country.

  • Responsible government: The executive would remain answerable to elected representatives.
  • Collective leadership: Executive decisions would be taken through a Council of Ministers rather than concentrated in one directly elected executive office.
  • Flexibility: A government losing legislative confidence could be replaced without waiting for the expiry of a fixed executive term.
  • Representation: The ministerial system could accommodate political and regional diversity through legislative representation and coalition arrangements.
  • Constitutional continuity: Existing institutional experience provided a practical foundation for the new system.

The choice was not without risks. Parliamentary government can produce executive dominance where a government has a large legislative majority, instability where coalitions are fragile, or weakened scrutiny where legislative deliberation is inadequate.

5. The President: Constitutional Head of the Union

The President is the constitutional head of the Union and the formal executive authority under the Constitution. The President's position must be understood in conjunction with the aid-and-advice framework under Article 74.

  • The President appoints the Prime Minister under Article 75.
  • Other Union ministers are appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.
  • Government business is expressed formally in the President's name in accordance with Article 77.
  • The President is kept informed by the Prime Minister under Article 78.
  • The President performs constitutional functions relating to Parliament, legislation, appointments and other executive matters.

The President is not an alternative political executive competing with the elected government. The office provides constitutional continuity, formal authority and a mechanism for ensuring that constitutional procedures are observed.

Article 74(2) prevents courts from inquiring into whether, and if so what, advice was tendered by ministers to the President. This protection concerning ministerial advice does not remove the possibility of judicial review of the legality or constitutionality of government action itself.

6. The Prime Minister: Head of the Political Executive

The Prime Minister is the head of the Council of Ministers and the central coordinating authority of the Union political executive. The Prime Minister provides political direction, leads the government in Parliament and coordinates the work of ministries.

Important constitutional and political functions include:

  • Advising the President on the appointment of other ministers.
  • Allocating and reshuffling ministerial portfolios through the constitutional process.
  • Presiding over the Council of Ministers and Cabinet.
  • Coordinating government policy and resolving inter-ministerial differences.
  • Communicating decisions of the Council of Ministers to the President.
  • Furnishing information to the President under Article 78.
  • Leading the government in Parliament and defending its policies.

The Prime Minister's authority is substantial but not unlimited. It operates within the Constitution, parliamentary confidence, collective ministerial responsibility, political party structures, federal arrangements and judicially enforceable legal limits.

7. Council of Ministers: Composition and Structure

Article 74 provides for a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head. Article 75 governs its appointment and responsibility. The Council is the constitutional body through which the Union's political executive functions.

The Council generally includes the following categories:

  • Cabinet Ministers: Senior ministers who ordinarily head major ministries and participate in Cabinet decision-making.
  • Ministers of State with independent charge: Ministers entrusted with independent responsibility for specified departments or ministries, without being attached to a Cabinet Minister for that charge.
  • Ministers of State: Ministers who assist Cabinet Ministers in the discharge of ministerial responsibilities.

These categories describe the political and administrative organisation of the ministry. The Constitution does not create separate constitutional classes of ministers with different degrees of membership in the Council of Ministers.

Article 75(1A), inserted by the 91st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2003, provides that the total number of ministers, including the Prime Minister, in the Council of Ministers shall not exceed 15 per cent of the total number of members of the Lok Sabha.

A minister who is not a member of either House of Parliament for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister under Article 75(5).

8. Collective and Individual Ministerial Responsibility

Collective responsibility — Article 75(3)

The Council of Ministers is collectively responsible to the House of the People. This is the central principle connecting executive authority with parliamentary confidence.

  • The ministry must retain the confidence of the Lok Sabha.
  • Ministers are expected to publicly support collective government decisions.
  • A serious defeat on a matter of confidence may require the ministry to resign or follow the constitutionally appropriate process.
  • The Council functions as a politically united executive for the purpose of parliamentary accountability.

Individual responsibility — Article 75(2)

Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President. In the parliamentary system, this provision operates within the constitutional relationship between the President, the Prime Minister and the Council of Ministers. It is not ordinarily understood as giving the President an independent political power to dismiss ministers at will.

Individual ministerial responsibility also operates as a political convention: a minister may be expected to resign over serious departmental failure or loss of the Prime Minister's confidence.

Collective responsibility and individual responsibility are related but distinct. Collective responsibility concerns the ministry's survival and unity; individual responsibility concerns the position and accountability of a particular minister.

9. Cabinet and Council of Ministers

The Council of Ministers is the larger constitutional body. The Cabinet is its smaller, senior decision-making core. The Cabinet provides political direction and takes major policy decisions.

Basis Council of Ministers Cabinet
Composition Includes all categories of Union ministers. Comprises senior ministers selected for Cabinet participation.
Constitutional reference Expressly recognised under Article 74. Expressly referred to in provisions including Articles 352 and 75(3A), and in constitutional practice.
Role Provides the wider ministerial structure of government. Acts as the principal forum for major policy and executive decisions.
Meetings Does not ordinarily meet as a whole for every major decision. Meets regularly for collective consideration of major matters.
Decision-making Ministers discharge assigned responsibilities within the collective framework. Provides central political coordination and decision-making.

Article 352 uses the expression Cabinet in the context of a National Emergency and requires the decision of the Union Cabinet to be communicated in writing to the President. Article 75(3A), inserted by the 91st Amendment, also refers to the Cabinet for the purpose of the Council's size limit.

10. Appointment, Tenure and Membership of Ministers

  • Prime Minister: Appointed by the President under Article 75(1).
  • Other ministers: Appointed by the President on the advice of the Prime Minister.
  • Oath: The President administers the oaths of office and secrecy under Article 75(4), in accordance with the Third Schedule.
  • Tenure: Ministers hold office during the pleasure of the President, subject to the conventions and constitutional structure of parliamentary government.
  • Parliamentary membership: Under Article 75(5), a minister who is not a member of either House for six consecutive months ceases to be a minister.
  • Disqualification: A minister is subject to applicable constitutional disqualifications and other legal requirements.

The Prime Minister must ordinarily be a person who can command the confidence of the Lok Sabha. Where no party or coalition has a clear majority, the President's choice of Prime Minister is guided by the constitutional objective of identifying a person likely to demonstrate majority support in the House.

A Prime Minister or minister may initially be appointed without being a member of Parliament, but must secure membership of either House within the six-month period prescribed by Article 75(5).

11. Executive-Legislative Relationship

The parliamentary system creates a close institutional relationship between the executive and Parliament. Ministers are generally members of Parliament, participate in legislative proceedings and are accountable to the House.

Major mechanisms of legislative control include:

  • Questions and supplementary questions: Enable members to seek information and explanations from ministers.
  • Debates and discussions: Provide opportunities to scrutinise government policy and administration.
  • Motions: Including confidence and no-confidence motions, which directly relate to the ministry's political survival.
  • Financial control: Parliamentary approval is required for the withdrawal and authorisation of public expenditure in accordance with constitutional procedures.
  • Committees: Examine expenditure, legislation, departmental functioning and government assurances.
  • Collective responsibility: Makes the ministry answerable to the Lok Sabha as a whole.

The executive also plays a major role in legislative business by initiating government Bills, proposing financial legislation and determining policy priorities. This creates a practical risk of executive dominance, particularly where the government commands a strong legislative majority.

12. Article 78: Duties of the Prime Minister

Article 78 specifies the duties of the Prime Minister in relation to the President. The Prime Minister must:

  1. Communicate to the President all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of the affairs of the Union and proposals for legislation.
  2. Furnish such information relating to the administration of Union affairs and proposals for legislation as the President may call for.
  3. If the President so requires, submit for consideration of the Council of Ministers any matter on which a minister has taken a decision but which has not been considered by the Council.

Article 78 supports constitutional communication and collective ministerial functioning. It enables the President to remain informed about the administration of the Union while preserving the parliamentary principle of government through the Council of Ministers.

13. Article 77: Conduct of Government Business

Article 77 provides the formal framework for the conduct of the business of the Government of India.

  • All executive action of the Government of India is expressed to be taken in the name of the President.
  • Orders and instruments made and executed in the President's name are authenticated in the manner specified by rules.
  • The validity of an authenticated order or instrument cannot be questioned on the ground that it was not made or executed by the President personally.
  • The President makes rules for the more convenient transaction of government business and allocation of business among ministers.

The Transaction of Business Rules and Allocation of Business Rules organise the work of ministries and departments. They support coordination, responsibility and administrative continuity.

Formal expression of executive action in the President's name should not be confused with personal exercise of political executive power by the President.

14. Parliamentary Executive at the State Level

A broadly similar parliamentary structure operates in the States. The Governor is the constitutional head, while the Chief Minister leads the State Council of Ministers.

The State framework has distinctive features, particularly concerning the Governor's constitutional position, the role of the Chief Minister and the relationship between State and Union institutions. These require separate examination and cannot be treated as identical to the Union framework.

15. Judicial Interpretation

Ram Jawaya Kapur v. State of Punjab (1955)

The Supreme Court explained the nature of executive power in India's parliamentary system and recognised that executive action is not confined only to matters covered by existing legislation, provided it remains within constitutional and legal limits.

U.N.R. Rao v. Indira Gandhi (1971)

The Supreme Court held that the President cannot function without a Council of Ministers. The dissolution of the Lok Sabha does not eliminate the constitutional requirement for a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the President.

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

The Supreme Court affirmed the constitutional position of the President and Governor as formal heads who ordinarily act on the aid and advice of their respective Councils of Ministers, subject to constitutionally recognised exceptions.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The Court emphasised that the question of whether a ministry enjoys majority support should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the legislature, rather than determined through subjective assessments outside it.

Nabam Rebia v. Deputy Speaker (2016)

The Supreme Court examined the constitutional limits on gubernatorial discretion and reinforced the principle that the Governor's office cannot be used to destabilise the parliamentary functioning of an elected government.

16. Strengths of the Parliamentary Executive

  • Democratic accountability: The government remains answerable to the elected lower House.
  • Collective decision-making: Policy is developed through a ministerial structure rather than a single executive office.
  • Flexibility: A government may be replaced when it loses legislative confidence, subject to constitutional processes.
  • Legislative-executive coordination: The executive can ordinarily secure legislative support for its programme while remaining subject to scrutiny.
  • Political representation: Coalition arrangements and ministerial composition can accommodate diverse political interests.
  • Continuous accountability: Questions, debates, committees and financial procedures provide recurring opportunities for scrutiny.

17. Challenges and Criticisms

  • Executive dominance: A large legislative majority and party discipline may weaken independent legislative scrutiny.
  • Party control: Strong whip practices can constrain deliberation and independent voting by legislators.
  • Coalition instability: Fragmented legislative support can create uncertainty in government formation and continuity.
  • Cabinet centralisation: Important decisions may become concentrated in the Prime Minister's Office or a small group of senior ministers.
  • Weak legislative scrutiny: Limited sitting time, disruptions and the passage of Bills without adequate examination can reduce accountability.
  • Departmental accountability: Political responsibility may not always translate into effective administrative correction or clear attribution of responsibility.
  • Ordinance dependence: Excessive use of ordinance-making powers may bypass ordinary legislative deliberation.
  • Ministerial capacity: Large portfolios, frequent reshuffles and limited subject expertise can affect policy coordination.

18. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Strengthen parliamentary committees and ensure adequate time for legislative scrutiny.
  • Improve the quality of legislative debates and reduce avoidable disruptions.
  • Ensure that the Council of Ministers functions through meaningful collective deliberation.
  • Promote transparent and consistent procedures for government decision-making.
  • Improve coordination between ministries and clarify departmental accountability.
  • Strengthen the capacity of ministers and legislative institutions through research and professional support.
  • Use ordinance-making powers consistently with their constitutional purpose and the requirement of legislative accountability.
  • Maintain constitutional conventions governing the President, Prime Minister and Council of Ministers.
  • Protect the ability of Parliament to scrutinise executive action, including through effective opposition participation.

The objective is not to weaken the executive's ability to govern, but to ensure that executive effectiveness is accompanied by democratic accountability, legislative scrutiny and constitutional restraint.

20. Important Distinctions

  • President and Prime Minister: The President is the constitutional head; the Prime Minister leads the political executive.
  • Council of Ministers and Cabinet: The Council is the larger constitutional body; the Cabinet is its smaller, senior decision-making core.
  • Collective and individual responsibility: Collective responsibility concerns the ministry's accountability to the Lok Sabha; individual responsibility concerns a particular minister's position and conduct.
  • Formal and political executive: Formal executive authority is vested in the President; political direction is exercised by the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers within the Constitution.
  • Article 74(2) and judicial review: Courts cannot inquire into the advice tendered to the President, but this does not create blanket immunity for executive action from judicial review.
  • Six-month rule and ministerial tenure: Article 75(5) permits a non-member to serve as a minister for up to six consecutive months without membership of either House; it does not create a separate six-month tenure for ministers who are already members.

21. Integrated Understanding

India's parliamentary executive rests on the combination of constitutional form and political responsibility. The President provides the formal constitutional headship of the Union, while the Prime Minister and Council of Ministers exercise political leadership and remain accountable to the Lok Sabha.

Articles 74 and 75 establish the core relationship; Articles 77 and 78 organise government business and communication with the President. Parliamentary questions, debates, committees and financial control provide mechanisms for scrutiny.

The effectiveness of the system therefore depends not only on constitutional text but also on conventions, responsible party conduct, legislative capacity, transparency and respect for institutional boundaries.
