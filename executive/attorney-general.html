1. Constitutional Position

The Attorney General of India is the highest law officer of the Union Government and its principal constitutional legal adviser. The office is established under Article 76 of the Constitution.

The Attorney General advises the Government of India on legal matters, performs legal duties assigned by the President and represents the Union in legal proceedings where required.

The office is constitutional rather than statutory. It is distinct from the Ministry of Law and Justice, the Solicitor General of India and other law officers.

3. Appointment

Article 76(1) provides that the President shall appoint a person who is qualified to be appointed a Judge of the Supreme Court as the Attorney General of India.

The appointment is made by the President. In the parliamentary system, the appointment operates within the constitutional framework of the Union executive.

The Constitution does not prescribe a separate public election or parliamentary confirmation process for this office.

4. Qualifications

The Attorney General must possess the qualifications required for appointment as a Judge of the Supreme Court under Article 124(3).

A person must satisfy one of the following routes:

  • Be a citizen of India and have been a Judge of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession for at least five years; or
  • Be a citizen of India and have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such Courts in succession for at least ten years; or
  • Be a citizen of India and, in the opinion of the President, be a distinguished jurist.

These are qualifications for eligibility. The Attorney General is not thereby appointed as a judge and does not acquire judicial office.

5. Term of Office and Resignation

Article 76 does not prescribe a fixed tenure for the Attorney General. Under Article 76(4), the Attorney General holds office during the pleasure of the President.

  • The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed five-year term.
  • The Attorney General may resign by submitting a resignation through the appropriate constitutional process.
  • The President may end the appointment in accordance with the pleasure provision.
  • The Attorney General is entitled to remuneration determined by the President.

In practice, the tenure is closely associated with the government's term and the confidence of the Union executive, although the Constitution itself does not specify a fixed relationship between the two.

6. Duties of the Attorney General

Article 76(2) provides that it shall be the duty of the Attorney General to give advice to the Government of India upon legal matters and perform other duties of a legal character assigned by the President.

Principal functions include:

  • Advising the Union Government on constitutional and legal questions.
  • Providing opinions on proposed legislation, executive decisions and government policy where legal advice is required.
  • Representing the Union Government before the Supreme Court and other courts when engaged to do so.
  • Performing legal duties assigned by the President.
  • Discharging functions conferred by the Constitution or other applicable law.

7. Right of Audience in Courts

Article 76(3) gives the Attorney General the right of audience in all courts in the territory of India in the performance of official duties.

This constitutional right facilitates the Attorney General's role in representing and advising the Union Government in judicial proceedings.

The right of audience does not mean that the Attorney General automatically represents the Union in every case. Representation depends on the matter, instructions and the applicable legal arrangements.

8. Rights in Parliament

Article 88 grants the Attorney General the right to speak in and otherwise take part in the proceedings of either House of Parliament, any joint sitting of the Houses and any parliamentary committee of which the Attorney General may be named a member.

However, the Attorney General does not have a right to vote in Parliament merely by virtue of the office.

The right facilitates legal and constitutional assistance to Parliament without making the Attorney General an elected or nominated member of either House.

9. Privileges and Immunities

Under Article 105(4), the provisions relating to parliamentary privileges and immunities apply to persons who have a right to speak in, or otherwise take part in, the proceedings of a House or a committee by virtue of the Constitution.

Accordingly, the Attorney General enjoys the relevant parliamentary privileges while participating in parliamentary proceedings within the scope of Article 88.

These privileges are linked to parliamentary participation; they do not make the Attorney General a member of Parliament.

10. Independence and Professional Responsibilities

The Attorney General occupies a distinctive position between the Union Government and the legal system. The office must provide sound legal advice while maintaining professional integrity.

  • Legal advice should be based on constitutional and statutory principles.
  • The Attorney General must assist the courts through accurate and responsible legal submissions.
  • Conflicts of interest should be managed in accordance with professional obligations and applicable rules.
  • Confidential government legal advice should be handled appropriately.
  • The Attorney General's public role should preserve confidence in the administration of justice.

The Constitution does not establish a fixed tenure or an independent appointment commission for the office. The balance between executive confidence and professional independence is therefore an important institutional concern.

11. Limitations on the Office

  • The Attorney General is not a member of the Union Council of Ministers merely by holding the office.
  • The Attorney General does not have voting rights in Parliament by virtue of Article 88.
  • The Attorney General is not a judge and does not exercise judicial power.
  • The Attorney General's tenure is not constitutionally fixed.
  • The office is not a constitutional tribunal or an independent prosecutorial authority with general jurisdiction over all criminal cases.
  • The Attorney General's functions are primarily legal and advisory, subject to the Constitution and applicable law.

14. Role in Constitutional Litigation

The Attorney General may represent the Union Government in important constitutional proceedings, including disputes concerning the interpretation of the Constitution, legislative competence, federal relations and the validity of Union legislation.

The office may also assist the Supreme Court in matters where the Union's legal position or constitutional obligations are relevant.

Article 143 enables the President to refer questions of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion. The Attorney General may assist in proceedings arising from such a reference, as appropriate.

15. Relationship with the Ministry of Law and Justice

The Ministry of Law and Justice is a Union Government ministry responsible for a broad range of legal and legislative functions.

The Attorney General is not the administrative head of the Ministry and does not replace its institutional responsibilities.

The Department of Legal Affairs and other legal institutions support the Government of India in legal advice, litigation and related matters. The Attorney General performs the distinct constitutional role under Article 76.

16. Issues and Challenges

  • Executive dependence: Appointment and tenure during presidential pleasure may raise questions about the perceived independence of the office.
  • Conflict of interest: The Attorney General's advisory role and participation in litigation require careful management of professional conflicts.
  • Public confidence: The office must maintain credibility as a legal adviser and representative of the Union.
  • Workload: Complex constitutional litigation and a wide range of government legal matters create substantial demands.
  • Institutional distinction: The roles of the Attorney General, Solicitor General and government departments must remain clear.
  • Confidentiality and transparency: Government legal advice requires appropriate confidentiality, while public legal positions should remain accountable to law and constitutional principles.

17. Reforms and the Way Forward

  • Strengthen transparent and merit-based selection practices.
  • Maintain clear professional standards for conflicts of interest and government representation.
  • Ensure that legal advice is grounded in constitutional principles rather than short-term political considerations.
  • Improve coordination among the Attorney General, Solicitor General, Ministry of Law and Justice and other government legal offices.
  • Promote timely and consistent legal advice to reduce avoidable litigation.
  • Preserve the credibility of the office through professional independence and responsible advocacy.

18. Important Distinctions for Revision

  • Constitutional and statutory office: The Attorney General is a constitutional office under Article 76, whereas the Solicitor General is not established by the Constitution.
  • Attorney General and Advocate General: Article 76 relates to the Union; Article 165 relates to the State.
  • Right to speak and right to vote: Article 88 grants parliamentary participation rights but not voting rights to the Attorney General.
  • Legal adviser and judge: The Attorney General advises and represents the Union; the office does not exercise judicial power.
  • Qualification and appointment: Eligibility for appointment as a Supreme Court Judge is required, but the Attorney General is appointed as a law officer, not as a judge.
  • Article 76 and Article 88: Article 76 establishes the office and its duties; Article 88 provides rights to participate in parliamentary proceedings.

19. Integrated Understanding

The Attorney General of India is a constitutional link between the Union executive and the legal system. The office supports lawful government by providing constitutional and legal advice, representing the Union in judicial proceedings and assisting Parliament when required.

Its effectiveness depends on professional competence, credibility, ethical conduct and the ability to reconcile the government's legal interests with constitutional principles and the rule of law.
