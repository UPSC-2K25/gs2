1. Constitutional Position

The President of India is the constitutional head of the Union and occupies the highest constitutional office in the Republic. Article 52 provides for the office of the President, while Article 53 vests the executive power of the Union in the President.

India follows a parliamentary system. Consequently, the President ordinarily exercises constitutional powers on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Prime Minister, subject to the limited constitutional space for reconsideration and constitutionally recognised discretion.

The President is therefore neither a purely ceremonial figure without constitutional responsibility nor an independently governing executive comparable to a presidential system. The office represents constitutional continuity, formal executive authority and the obligation to act within the Constitution.

3. Election of the President

Article 54 provides for an indirect election by an electoral college. The President is elected by elected representatives of the Union Parliament and the elected Legislative Assemblies of the States.

The electoral college comprises:

  • Elected members of the Lok Sabha.
  • Elected members of the Rajya Sabha.
  • Elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the States.
  • Elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of the Union Territories of Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu and Kashmir, as provided by the constitutional framework and applicable law.

Nominated members of Parliament, nominated members of State Legislative Assemblies and members of State Legislative Councils do not participate in the presidential election.

The inclusion of elected members of the Legislative Assemblies of specified Union Territories reflects the constitutional arrangement introduced through the relevant amendments.

4. Value of Votes and Proportional Representation

Article 55 seeks to ensure uniformity in the representation of States and parity between the Union and the States in the presidential election. The election uses proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote and voting is by secret ballot.

Value of an MLA's vote

The value of the vote of each elected MLA is calculated by dividing the population of the State by the total number of elected members of its Legislative Assembly and then dividing the quotient by 1,000. The constitutional method includes the prescribed rounding rule.

The Constitution currently retains the 1971 Census population reference for this calculation until the relevant figures of the first census taken after 2026 are published.

Value of an MP's vote

The total value of votes of all elected MLAs is divided by the total number of elected members of both Houses of Parliament. The quotient determines the value of each elected MP's vote, subject to the constitutional method of calculation.

Voting method

  • Electors rank candidates in order of preference.
  • A candidate must obtain the prescribed quota of valid votes.
  • First-preference votes are counted initially.
  • If no candidate reaches the quota, the candidate with the lowest votes is eliminated and votes are transferred according to subsequent preferences.
  • The process continues until a candidate secures the required quota.

This system is intended to produce a broadly representative constitutional choice rather than a simple plurality result.

5. Qualifications for Election

Article 58 prescribes the following qualifications:

  • The candidate must be a citizen of India.
  • The candidate must have completed 35 years of age.
  • The candidate must be qualified for election as a member of the Lok Sabha.
  • The candidate must not hold an office of profit under the Union Government, a State Government or a local or other authority subject to the constitutional exceptions.

A person is not considered to hold an office of profit merely because the person is the President, Vice-President, Governor or a minister of the Union or a State.

The Presidential and Vice-Presidential Elections Act, 1952 and the rules made under it regulate the election procedure.

6. Conditions of the President's Office

Article 59 lays down the conditions of office:

  • The President cannot be a member of either House of Parliament or a House of a State Legislature. If such a member is elected President, the seat is deemed vacant from the date the President enters office.
  • The President cannot hold any other office of profit.
  • The President is entitled to official residence and emoluments, allowances and privileges determined by law.
  • The emoluments and allowances of the President cannot be diminished during the term of office.

7. Term, Resignation and Re-election

  • Term: Five years from the date of entering office under Article 56.
  • Resignation: The President may resign by writing addressed to the Vice-President.
  • Removal: The President may be removed through impeachment for violation of the Constitution.
  • Continuation: The President continues in office until the successor enters office, even after the five-year term expires.
  • Re-election: Article 57 permits a person who holds or has held the office to be eligible for re-election. The Constitution does not prescribe a maximum number of terms.

Article 62 requires an election to fill a vacancy caused by expiry of the term to be completed before the term expires. A casual vacancy must be filled as soon as possible and within six months. A person elected to fill a casual vacancy serves a full five-year term from the date of entering office.

8. Oath or Affirmation

Under Article 60, the President takes an oath or affirmation before entering office. The oath is administered by the Chief Justice of India or, in the Chief Justice's absence, the senior-most available judge of the Supreme Court.

The President undertakes to:

  • Faithfully execute the office of President.
  • Preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law.
  • Devote the President's service to the well-being of the people of India.

The oath reflects the constitutional nature of the office: the President is bound by the Constitution and does not possess an independent mandate to govern outside it.

9. Impeachment of the President

Article 61 provides the procedure for impeachment of the President for violation of the Constitution.

  1. The charge may be initiated in either House of Parliament.
  2. The proposal must be contained in a resolution moved after at least 14 days' written notice.
  3. The notice must be signed by at least one-fourth of the total membership of the House initiating the charge.
  4. The initiating House must pass the resolution by a majority of not less than two-thirds of its total membership.
  5. The other House investigates the charge or causes it to be investigated.
  6. The President has the right to appear and to be represented during the investigation.
  7. If the investigating House also passes the resolution by a majority of not less than two-thirds of its total membership, the President stands removed from office from the date of passage.

The Constitution does not define the expression "violation of the Constitution" for the purpose of Article 61. The procedure is deliberately demanding and requires substantial parliamentary agreement.

State legislatures do not participate in the impeachment process. Nominated members of either House of Parliament may participate in impeachment proceedings, unlike in the presidential electoral college.

10. Vacancy and Acting President

Article 65 provides that the Vice-President acts as President when a vacancy occurs due to death, resignation, removal or otherwise, and also discharges the President's functions during absence, illness or another inability.

When the Vice-President acts as President or discharges the President's functions, the Vice-President does not simultaneously perform the duties of the Chairperson of the Rajya Sabha.

The President's election must be held within six months of a casual vacancy under Article 62. The person elected to fill the vacancy receives a full five-year term.

Article 70 permits Parliament to make provision for the discharge of the President's functions in contingencies not otherwise provided for in the constitutional chapter.

11. Executive Powers

Article 53 vests Union executive power in the President. The exercise of this power is regulated by the Constitution and the parliamentary system.

  • Appoints the Prime Minister and, on the Prime Minister's advice, other ministers.
  • Appoints Governors of States.
  • Appoints the Attorney-General of India.
  • Appoints the Comptroller and Auditor General, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Appoints judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts in accordance with the constitutional framework.
  • Appoints members of the Union Public Service Commission and other constitutional authorities as prescribed.
  • Conducts Union executive business formally in the President's name under Article 77.
  • Receives information from the Prime Minister under Article 78.

These functions are generally performed on ministerial advice. The President's formal role in appointments should not be confused with an independent power to select office-holders contrary to the constitutional process.

12. Legislative Powers

The President is an integral part of Parliament under Article 79, which provides that Parliament consists of the President and the two Houses.

Summoning, prorogation and dissolution

  • Summons each House of Parliament under Article 85.
  • May prorogue either House.
  • May dissolve the Lok Sabha.
  • The gap between the last sitting of one session and the first sitting of the next session cannot exceed six months.

Address and messages

  • Addresses both Houses assembled together at the commencement of the first session after each general election to the Lok Sabha and at the commencement of the first session each year under Article 87.
  • May address either House or both Houses assembled together.
  • May send messages to either House under Article 86.

Nomination

The President nominates 12 members to the Rajya Sabha under Article 80 from persons having special knowledge or practical experience in literature, science, art and social service.

The earlier provision permitting nomination of two Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha ceased to operate following the constitutional amendment that discontinued that arrangement.

Assent to Bills — Article 111

When a Bill is presented for assent, the President may:

  • Give assent to the Bill.
  • Withhold assent.
  • Return a Bill, other than a Money Bill, for reconsideration by Parliament.

If Parliament passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendments, and presents it for assent, the President cannot withhold assent.

The Constitution does not prescribe a specific time limit for the President's decision on Union Bills. The Supreme Court's 2025 advisory opinion in the Presidential Reference concerning assent to Bills clarified that courts cannot impose fixed timelines or create a deemed-assent mechanism for the President, while constitutional inaction remains subject to the applicable constitutional principles.

The President cannot return a Constitution Amendment Bill passed under Article 368 for reconsideration. The President is constitutionally required to give assent to such a Bill under Article 368(2).

13. Veto Powers

The President's veto power concerns the decision on Bills passed by Parliament and presented for assent.

Absolute veto

The President withholds assent to a Bill. The Bill does not become law. This is commonly described as an absolute veto.

Suspensive veto

The President returns an ordinary Bill for reconsideration. If Parliament passes it again, with or without amendments, the President must give assent.

Pocket veto

The Constitution does not prescribe a specific time limit for the President to decide on an ordinary Bill. Withholding a decision for an extended period is commonly described as a pocket veto, although the term is not used in the Constitution.

Limits on veto powers

  • A Money Bill cannot be returned for reconsideration under Article 111.
  • A Constitution Amendment Bill passed under Article 368 must receive assent.
  • The President's decision must be understood within the aid-and-advice framework of Article 74.

14. Ordinance-Making Power — Article 123

Article 123 empowers the President to promulgate ordinances when both Houses of Parliament are not simultaneously in session and circumstances make immediate action necessary.

Important features include:

  • An ordinance may be promulgated when both Houses are not simultaneously in session.
  • The President must be satisfied that circumstances require immediate action.
  • An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of Parliament while it remains in operation.
  • It is subject to the same constitutional limitations as parliamentary legislation.
  • It must be laid before both Houses when they reassemble.
  • It ceases to operate six weeks after Parliament reassembles, unless approved through legislation or otherwise ceases earlier.
  • Where the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six-week period is calculated from the later date.
  • The President may withdraw an ordinance at any time.

The President's satisfaction under Article 123 operates within the constitutional aid-and-advice framework. Ordinances are temporary legislative instruments and cannot be used to permanently bypass Parliament.

In D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), the Supreme Court criticised repeated re-promulgation of ordinances as a constitutional subversion. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017), the Court reaffirmed that ordinance-making is subject to constitutional limitations and that re-promulgation cannot become a parallel law-making process.

15. Financial Powers

  • Causes the Annual Financial Statement to be laid before Parliament under Article 112.
  • Recommends the introduction of certain financial Bills under Articles 117 and related provisions.
  • Authorises advances from the Contingency Fund of India under the applicable law and constitutional framework.
  • Causes reports of the Finance Commission and other constitutional authorities to be laid before Parliament as required.
  • Exercises the formal constitutional functions associated with the Union's financial procedure.

The President's financial functions operate within parliamentary control over public finance. The executive cannot withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund of India except in accordance with constitutional authorisation.

16. Judicial Powers and Clemency — Article 72

Article 72 empowers the President to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, and to suspend, remit or commute sentences in specified cases.

The power applies to:

  • All cases where punishment or sentence is by a court martial.
  • All offences relating to matters within the executive power of the Union.
  • All cases where a sentence of death has been passed.

The clemency power is distinct from judicial appeal. It enables constitutional reconsideration of punishment in light of relevant circumstances, including justice, mercy and public interest.

Forms of clemency

  • Pardon: Removes the punishment and its penal consequences to the extent legally recognised.
  • Commutation: Substitutes a lighter form of punishment for a more severe one.
  • Remission: Reduces the period of the sentence without changing its character.
  • Respite: Awards a lesser sentence due to special circumstances.
  • Reprieve: Temporarily postpones the execution of a sentence, particularly to allow time for further consideration.

The President ordinarily exercises Article 72 on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. The exercise of clemency is not wholly immune from judicial review where constitutional limits, arbitrariness or legally relevant procedural defects are involved.

17. Diplomatic and Military Powers

Diplomatic powers

  • Represents India in formal diplomatic relations.
  • Appoints Indian ambassadors and high commissioners in accordance with the constitutional process.
  • Receives foreign diplomatic representatives.
  • International treaties and agreements are formally concluded in the name of the President, subject to constitutional and legal requirements.

Military powers

  • The President is the Supreme Commander of the Defence Forces of India.
  • Appointments to senior defence positions are made within the constitutional and statutory framework.
  • War and peace functions are exercised in accordance with constitutional procedures and parliamentary control.

These powers are formal constitutional powers. Their exercise is not an independent personal foreign or defence policy mandate.

18. Emergency Powers

The President has an important formal role in the proclamation of emergencies under Part XVIII of the Constitution.

National Emergency — Article 352

  • May be proclaimed on grounds of war, external aggression or armed rebellion.
  • The President must receive the written decision of the Union Cabinet.
  • The proclamation requires parliamentary approval within one month by the prescribed special majority.
  • It continues for six months at a time if approved through the constitutional process.

President's Rule — Article 356

  • May be proclaimed where the President, on the basis of a Governor's report or otherwise, is satisfied that the government of a State cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution.
  • The proclamation must be approved by Parliament within two months.
  • It is subject to constitutional conditions and judicial review.

Financial Emergency — Article 360

  • May be proclaimed where the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.
  • It must be approved by Parliament within two months.
  • It enables directions concerning financial propriety and other constitutional consequences.

The President's emergency powers are not personal political discretion. They operate within constitutional conditions, ministerial advice, parliamentary approval and judicial review.

19. Discretionary Space of the President

The President ordinarily acts on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers. Article 74(1), as amended, permits the President to require the Council to reconsider its advice, either generally or otherwise. The President must act in accordance with the advice tendered after reconsideration.

The Constitution does not establish a general discretionary executive power for the President. However, certain situations require the President to exercise constitutional judgment within the parliamentary framework.

  • Appointment of Prime Minister: Where no party or coalition has a clear majority, the President must identify a person likely to command the confidence of the Lok Sabha.
  • Floor test: The President may require a government whose majority is genuinely in doubt to demonstrate confidence through the constitutionally appropriate process.
  • Reconsideration of advice: The President may return ministerial advice once for reconsideration under Article 74(1).
  • Return of ordinary Bills: The President may return a non-Money Bill for reconsideration under Article 111.
  • Information: The President may seek information from the Prime Minister under Article 78.

These functions should be exercised with constitutional restraint. The President is expected to facilitate the formation and continuity of a government enjoying legislative confidence, rather than become an alternative political centre.

20. Aid and Advice — Article 74

Article 74 requires a Council of Ministers with the Prime Minister at its head to aid and advise the President. The President ordinarily exercises functions in accordance with this advice.

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 made the aid-and-advice requirement explicit. The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 added the power of the President to require reconsideration of advice. The President must act in accordance with the advice tendered after reconsideration.

Article 74(2) provides that the question whether any advice was tendered by ministers to the President, and if so what advice, shall not be inquired into by any court.

The provision protects the confidentiality of ministerial advice. It does not prevent judicial review of the constitutionality of the resulting executive action.

22. Important Judicial Decisions

Shamsher Singh v. State of Punjab (1974)

Established that the President and Governor are constitutional heads who ordinarily exercise their functions on the aid and advice of their respective Councils of Ministers, subject to limited constitutional exceptions.

Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981)

The Supreme Court held that the President's power under Article 72 is exercised on the advice of the Central Government and is subject to constitutional limitations.

Kehar Singh v. Union of India (1989)

The Court recognised the broad nature of the President's clemency power and explained that the President may examine the relevant merits of a case while considering a mercy petition.

Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006)

The Court held that clemency decisions are subject to limited judicial review where they are affected by mala fides, arbitrariness or irrelevant considerations.

Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017)

The Court examined ordinance-making under Article 123 and emphasised that ordinances are temporary constitutional instruments, not a substitute for regular parliamentary legislation.

In re: Assent, Withholding or Reservation of Bills by the Governor and the President of India (2025)

In its advisory opinion, the Supreme Court addressed constitutional questions concerning assent to Bills, including the limits of judicially imposed timelines and the absence of a deemed-assent mechanism for the President under Article 111.

23. Constitutional Importance of the Office

  • Constitutional continuity: The office continues independently of changes in the elected government.
  • Formal executive authority: Union executive action is expressed in the President's name.
  • Legislative integration: The President is a constituent part of Parliament under Article 79.
  • Constitutional safeguard: The President's limited powers of reconsideration and constitutional judgment can help ensure observance of constitutional processes.
  • National representation: The office symbolises the unity and constitutional identity of the Republic.
  • Federal participation: State representation in the electoral college connects the Union's constitutional headship with the federal structure.

24. Challenges and Issues

  • Role ambiguity: The boundary between constitutional judgment and political discretion may become contentious during government formation.
  • Delay in assent: Prolonged inaction on Bills can raise questions about legislative functioning and constitutional accountability.
  • Ordinance misuse: Frequent or repeated ordinances may undermine parliamentary deliberation.
  • Clemency delays: Prolonged consideration of mercy petitions raises concerns about fairness and human dignity.
  • Political controversy: The office may face pressure to act in ways that favour the incumbent government or political interests.
  • Limited public understanding: Formal constitutional powers are sometimes confused with independent political authority.

25. Way Forward

  • Maintain the non-partisan constitutional character of the office.
  • Respect the aid-and-advice framework while using the reconsideration power where constitutionally justified.
  • Follow transparent and constitutionally consistent practices during government formation.
  • Ensure timely and reasoned constitutional processing of Bills within the applicable framework.
  • Use ordinance-making powers only where immediate action is constitutionally necessary.
  • Strengthen institutional procedures for timely consideration of clemency petitions.
  • Preserve the distinction between formal executive authority and political responsibility.

26. Important Distinctions for Revision

  • Election and impeachment: Election is governed by Articles 54 and 55; impeachment is governed by Article 61.
  • Electoral college and impeachment electorate: Nominated members do not vote in the presidential election, but nominated members of Parliament may participate in impeachment proceedings.
  • Absolute and suspensive veto: Absolute veto withholds assent; suspensive veto returns an ordinary Bill for reconsideration.
  • Ordinance and Act: An ordinance has the force of law temporarily but is subject to parliamentary approval and constitutional time limits.
  • Pardon and remission: Pardon removes the punishment and its penal consequences to the extent legally recognised; remission reduces the period of punishment without changing its character.
  • President and Prime Minister: The President is the constitutional head; the Prime Minister is the head of the political executive.
  • Article 72 and Article 161: Article 72 governs the President's clemency powers; Article 161 governs the Governor's clemency powers within the State's constitutional sphere.

27. Integrated Understanding

The President occupies a position of constitutional authority within India's parliamentary system. The office combines formal executive power, legislative functions, clemency, emergency responsibilities and constitutional continuity.

The central principle is that these powers are exercised within the constitutional framework of responsible government. The President ordinarily acts on ministerial advice, may require reconsideration once, and must thereafter act in accordance with the advice tendered.

The effectiveness of the office depends on constitutional restraint, non-partisanship, respect for parliamentary responsibility and faithful observance of constitutional procedures.
