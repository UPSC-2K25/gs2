1. Constitutional Position

The Governor is the constitutional head of a State and forms an essential part of the State executive. Part VI of the Constitution, particularly Articles 153–167, provides the principal framework governing the office.

The Governor ordinarily exercises constitutional functions on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister, except where the Constitution permits the exercise of discretion.

The office connects the Union and State constitutional structures, but the Governor is not an agent of the Union Government in the ordinary administrative sense.

3. Appointment of Governor

Under Article 155, the Governor is appointed by the President by warrant under the President's hand and seal.

  • The Governor is not directly elected by the people.
  • The Governor is not elected by the State Legislature.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe a formal consultation with the Chief Minister as a mandatory condition of appointment.
  • The appointment is made by the President within the Union constitutional framework.

The Constituent Assembly adopted appointment rather than direct election, partly to avoid creating a competing popular mandate within the State executive.

4. Qualifications

Article 157 prescribes two qualifications:

  • The person must be a citizen of India.
  • The person must have completed 35 years of age.

The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum educational qualification, legislative experience or prior administrative service for appointment as Governor.

5. Conditions of Office

Article 158 provides that:

  • The Governor shall not be a member of either House of Parliament or a House of the State Legislature. If such a member is appointed Governor, the seat is deemed vacated on entering office.
  • The Governor shall not hold any other office of profit.
  • The Governor is entitled to official residence and emoluments, allowances and privileges determined by law.
  • The emoluments and allowances cannot be diminished during the term of office.

Where one person is appointed Governor of two or more States, Article 158(3A) provides for allocation of emoluments and allowances among the States in the proportion determined by the President.

6. Oath or Affirmation

Under Article 159, the Governor takes an oath or affirmation before entering office.

The Governor undertakes to:

  • Faithfully execute the office.
  • Preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law.
  • Devote oneself to the service and well-being of the people of the State.

The oath is administered by the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court or, in the Chief Justice's absence, the senior-most available judge of that Court.

7. Term, Resignation and Removal

Article 156 provides that the Governor holds office during the pleasure of the President. Subject to this provision, the Governor normally holds office for five years from the date of entering office.

  • The Governor may resign by writing addressed to the President.
  • The Governor may continue in office until a successor enters office, even after completion of five years.
  • The Constitution does not prescribe an impeachment procedure for removal of a Governor.
  • The President may remove the Governor under the pleasure provision.

In B.P. Singhal v. Union of India (2010), the Supreme Court held that the pleasure doctrine does not permit arbitrary or capricious removal. The decision must not be based on irrelevant or improper considerations.

8. Executive Powers

Article 154 vests the executive power of the State in the Governor. This power is exercised directly or through subordinate officers in accordance with the Constitution.

  • Appointments and constitutional functions relating to the Chief Minister and other ministers under Article 164.
  • Appointment of the Advocate-General under Article 165.
  • Appointment of the State Election Commissioner under Article 243K.
  • Appointment of the Chairperson and members of the State Public Service Commission under Article 316.
  • Appointment of persons to certain constitutional and statutory offices where the Constitution or law provides.
  • Conduct of State Government business under Article 166.

These powers must be understood alongside the parliamentary principle that the elected Council of Ministers ordinarily exercises the real executive authority.

9. Legislative Powers

The Governor is a component of the State Legislature under Article 168. The office performs several legislative functions.

  • Summons the State Legislature under Article 174.
  • Prorogues the House or Houses.
  • May dissolve the Legislative Assembly.
  • Addresses the Legislature under Articles 175 and 176.
  • May send messages to the House or Houses under Article 175.
  • Causes the presentation of the annual financial statement under Article 202.
  • Acts on Bills under Article 200.
  • May promulgate ordinances under Article 213 when the Legislature is not in session and constitutional conditions are satisfied.

10. Assent to State Bills: Article 200

When a Bill passed by the State Legislature is presented to the Governor, Article 200 provides the constitutional framework for action.

The Governor may:

  • Give assent to the Bill.
  • Withhold assent.
  • Reserve the Bill for consideration of the President.
  • Return a Bill, if it is not a Money Bill, for reconsideration with a message requesting the House or Houses to reconsider specified provisions or the Bill as a whole.

If the Legislature passes the returned Bill again, with or without amendment, and presents it to the Governor, Article 200 requires that the Governor shall not withhold assent. The constitutional text nevertheless preserves the option of reserving the Bill for the President where applicable.

Article 200 contains a specific requirement to reserve a Bill for the President if, in the Governor's opinion, the Bill would so derogate from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is designed to fill under the Constitution.

11. Reservation of Bills: Article 201

When a Bill is reserved for the consideration of the President, Article 201 governs the next stage.

  • The President may declare assent or withhold assent.
  • Where the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return it to the State Legislature for reconsideration.
  • The Legislature must reconsider the Bill within six months from receipt of the message.
  • The Bill is then presented again to the President for consideration.

Reservation is an important feature of India's federal structure. It allows constitutional scrutiny of certain State legislation within the Union constitutional framework.

12. Ordinance-Making Power: Article 213

Article 213 empowers the Governor to promulgate ordinances when the State Legislature is not in session, subject to constitutional conditions.

  • The Governor must be satisfied that circumstances exist which render immediate action necessary.
  • An ordinance has the same force and effect as an Act of the State Legislature while it remains in operation.
  • It must be laid before the Legislative Assembly and, where applicable, both Houses.
  • It ceases to operate six weeks after reassembly of the Legislature, unless earlier withdrawn or disapproved.
  • Where the Houses reassemble on different dates, the six-week period is calculated from the later date.

In specified situations, the Governor cannot promulgate an ordinance without instructions from the President, including where a Bill containing the same provisions would require prior presidential sanction or reservation for the President's consideration under the constitutional framework.

In D.C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar (1987), the Supreme Court condemned routine re-promulgation of ordinances as a subversion of the legislative process. In Krishna Kumar Singh v. State of Bihar (2017), the Court reaffirmed that ordinance-making is a limited constitutional power and is subject to judicial review.

13. Financial Powers

  • Causes the annual financial statement of the State to be laid before the Legislature under Article 202.
  • Recommends demands for grants under Article 203.
  • Recommends certain financial Bills under Article 207.
  • Causes supplementary, additional or excess grants to be presented under Article 205.
  • Constitutes the State Finance Commission under Article 243I and, for municipalities, the relevant framework under Article 243Y.

These functions operate within the State's parliamentary financial procedure. The Governor does not independently formulate or approve the State budget outside the constitutional process.

14. Judicial and Clemency-Related Powers

Article 161 empowers the Governor to grant pardons, reprieves, respites or remissions of punishment, or to suspend, remit or commute sentences, in cases relating to matters within the executive power of the State.

The Governor's power is not identical to the President's power under Article 72. The President's power extends to specified categories including court-martial cases and all cases involving a sentence of death. The Governor does not possess the constitutional power to pardon a death sentence, although the Governor may suspend, remit or commute it within the constitutional framework.

In Maru Ram v. Union of India (1981) and Epuru Sudhakar v. Government of Andhra Pradesh (2006), the Supreme Court explained that clemency powers are constitutional powers subject to judicial review on limited grounds, including arbitrariness, mala fides and irrelevant considerations.

15. Discretionary Powers

Article 163 provides for a Council of Ministers to aid and advise the Governor, except in so far as the Constitution requires the Governor to exercise functions in discretion.

Recognised situations involving discretion include:

  • Appointment of a Chief Minister when no party or coalition has a clear majority.
  • Seeking a floor test where the existence of majority support is genuinely in doubt.
  • Reservation of certain Bills for the President under Article 200.
  • Special responsibilities expressly assigned by the Constitution in specified States or circumstances.
  • Reporting to the President under Article 356 where constitutional conditions are believed to exist.

Discretion is not a general licence to act independently of the elected government. It must be traceable to the Constitution and exercised within constitutional limits.

16. Appointment of Chief Minister and Government Formation

Under Article 164, the Chief Minister is appointed by the Governor, and other ministers are appointed on the advice of the Chief Minister.

Where a party or coalition has a clear majority in the Legislative Assembly, the Governor ordinarily appoints its chosen leader as Chief Minister.

In a hung Assembly, the Governor must assess who is most likely to command the confidence of the House. The assessment should be guided by objective constitutional considerations rather than personal preference.

The floor of the Legislative Assembly is ordinarily the appropriate forum to establish majority support.

17. Floor Test and Constitutional Morality

A floor test is a legislative procedure to determine whether the Council of Ministers enjoys the confidence of the Legislative Assembly.

The Governor may require a floor test when objective material gives rise to a genuine doubt about the government's majority. The power cannot be used arbitrarily to destabilise an elected government.

In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court emphasised the importance of floor tests in determining legislative majority and placed presidential action under Article 356 within judicial review.

In Shivraj Singh Chouhan v. Speaker, Madhya Pradesh Legislative Assembly (2020), the Supreme Court recognised the Governor's power to direct a floor test where objective circumstances justified it, while emphasising that such power must be exercised cautiously.

18. Governor and President's Rule: Article 356

Article 356 allows the President to issue a proclamation where the constitutional machinery of a State has failed in the manner contemplated by the Constitution.

The Governor may submit a report to the President concerning the situation in the State. However, the President may also act otherwise than on a Governor's report.

A Governor's report should be based on relevant material and should not be used as a political instrument.

In S.R. Bommai (1994), the Supreme Court held that the proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review and that legislative majority should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House.

19. Relationship with the Chief Minister

Article 167 specifies the duties of the Chief Minister in relation to the Governor.

The Chief Minister must:

  • Communicate to the Governor all decisions of the Council of Ministers relating to the administration of State affairs and proposals for legislation.
  • Furnish information relating to the administration of State affairs and proposals for legislation as the Governor may call for.
  • Submit for consideration of the Council of Ministers a matter on which a minister has taken a decision but which has not been considered by the Council, if the Governor so requires.

These duties facilitate constitutional communication and informed executive functioning. They do not create a parallel executive authority in the Governor.

20. Immunities and Constitutional Protection: Article 361

Article 361 provides constitutional protection to the President and Governors in respect of the exercise and performance of their official powers and duties.

  • The Governor is not answerable to any court for the exercise and performance of the powers and duties of office.
  • No criminal proceedings can be instituted or continued against the Governor during the term of office.
  • No process for arrest or imprisonment can be issued during the term of office.
  • Civil proceedings concerning personal acts may be instituted subject to the constitutional requirement of prior notice.

Article 361 does not make the Governor's actions immune from judicial scrutiny in all circumstances. The constitutional validity of government action can be examined through appropriate proceedings.

23. Issues and Challenges

  • Political appointments: Perceptions of political affiliation can affect confidence in the neutrality of the office.
  • Centre-State tensions: Conflicts may arise when the Governor and elected State Government are politically opposed.
  • Assent to Bills: Delays or uncertainty in the exercise of Article 200 powers can disrupt the legislative process.
  • Government formation: Discretion in hung Assemblies can become controversial without clear, consistent standards.
  • Article 356 reports: Reports may be criticised if they appear partisan or insufficiently evidence-based.
  • Ordinance-making: Repeated or politically motivated use can undermine legislative deliberation.
  • Constitutional ambiguity: The scope of discretion in some situations remains a source of institutional disagreement.

24. Reform Recommendations

  • Sarkaria Commission: Recommended that the Governor should be an eminent person, preferably from outside the State, and not deeply involved in local politics.
  • Punchhi Commission: Examined the need to clarify the Governor's role, discretion and relationship with the elected State Government.
  • Appointment consultation: Consider wider consultation with the Chief Minister and other relevant constitutional stakeholders.
  • Security of tenure: Establish clear conventions and safeguards against arbitrary removal.
  • Time-bound constitutional action: Develop transparent and constitutionally sound procedures for dealing with Bills.
  • Objective floor tests: Use the Legislative Assembly as the appropriate forum to determine majority support.
  • Political neutrality: Maintain impartiality and constitutional restraint in all official functions.
  • Institutional conventions: Strengthen conventions governing government formation, legislative sessions and reservation of Bills.

25. Important Distinctions for Revision

  • Articles 153 and 155: Article 153 provides for Governors; Article 155 governs appointment.
  • Articles 156 and 157: Article 156 deals with tenure; Article 157 specifies qualifications.
  • Articles 161 and 72: Governor and President have distinct clemency powers and constitutional jurisdictions.
  • Articles 163 and 164: Article 163 deals with aid and advice; Article 164 governs appointment and responsibility of State ministers.
  • Articles 200 and 201: Article 200 concerns the Governor's action on State Bills; Article 201 concerns Bills reserved for the President.
  • Articles 213 and 123: Article 213 concerns State ordinances; Article 123 concerns Union ordinances.
  • Governor and President: Both are constitutional heads, but their functions operate within different levels of India's federal parliamentary system.

26. Integrated Understanding

The Governor is designed to be a constitutional head who facilitates stable and lawful State administration within India's parliamentary federal system. The office performs important executive, legislative, financial and clemency functions, but ordinarily acts on ministerial advice.

The constitutional challenge is to preserve the Governor's ability to discharge specific constitutional responsibilities while preventing the office from becoming a source of political interference in elected State governments.
