1. Meaning and Concept of Asymmetric Federalism

Asymmetric federalism refers to a federal arrangement in which all constituent units do not enjoy identical constitutional powers, institutional arrangements or administrative responsibilities. Certain States or regions may receive differentiated treatment because of their historical circumstances, geographical characteristics, cultural identity, tribal composition, political agreements or developmental requirements.

Unlike a strictly symmetrical federation, where constituent units broadly enjoy the same constitutional status and powers, an asymmetric federation recognises that uniform arrangements may not adequately address the diversity of all regions.

India's federal structure contains several forms of asymmetry. These include special provisions for particular States, autonomous district and regional councils, differentiated arrangements for Union Territories, and protections for tribal and indigenous communities.

Core characteristics

  • Differentiated constitutional or statutory arrangements.
  • Recognition of historical and cultural diversity.
  • Protection of tribal and indigenous institutions.
  • Regional autonomy within the constitutional framework.
  • Accommodation of distinct political and developmental needs.
  • Preservation of national unity through differentiated governance.

2. Why Does India Need Asymmetric Federalism?

India is characterised by substantial diversity in language, religion, ethnicity, customary practices, geography and levels of development. A uniform institutional model may not always provide effective governance in every region.

Historical considerations

Several regions entered the Indian Union under distinctive historical circumstances. Political integration, constitutional negotiations and administrative transitions sometimes required differentiated safeguards.

Tribal and cultural diversity

Tribal communities may have distinctive customary institutions, land relations, social practices and systems of community governance. Special constitutional arrangements can protect these institutions while maintaining constitutional accountability.

Geographical and developmental conditions

Remote, mountainous, border and strategically sensitive regions may face administrative and developmental challenges that differ from those of other parts of the country.

Political accommodation

Differentiated arrangements can provide a constitutional framework for addressing regional aspirations, reducing alienation and strengthening democratic participation.

3. Constitutional Foundations of Asymmetry

The Constitution does not establish one single provision titled "asymmetric federalism". Instead, asymmetry emerges from multiple provisions across different Parts and Schedules.

Important constitutional provisions

  • Article 244 — Administration of Scheduled Areas and Tribal Areas.
  • Article 244A — Formation of an autonomous State comprising certain tribal areas in Assam and creation of a local Legislature or Council of Ministers or both.
  • Article 275(1) — Grants-in-aid, including special grants for promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • Article 371 — Special provisions for Maharashtra and Gujarat.
  • Articles 371A–371J — Special provisions for specified States.
  • Fifth Schedule — Administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Sixth Schedule — Administration of tribal areas in specified North Eastern States.
  • Article 239AA — Special constitutional framework for the National Capital Territory of Delhi.
  • Article 239AB — Provision for failure of constitutional machinery in the National Capital Territory.
  • Article 240 — Power of the President to make regulations for certain Union Territories.

4. Fifth Schedule: Administration of Scheduled Areas

The Fifth Schedule deals with the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than the tribal areas covered by the Sixth Schedule.

It provides a differentiated administrative framework to protect tribal interests, land, resources and customary practices.

Declaration of Scheduled Areas

  • The President may declare an area to be a Scheduled Area.
  • The President may increase or decrease the area, alter its boundaries or rescind its designation, as constitutionally provided.
  • The Governor has a significant role in the administration of Scheduled Areas.

Tribes Advisory Council

A Tribes Advisory Council is to be established in each State having Scheduled Areas and may also be established in a State having Scheduled Tribes but no Scheduled Areas if the President so directs.

  • It advises on matters concerning the welfare and advancement of Scheduled Tribes in the State.
  • Its membership is predominantly drawn from representatives of Scheduled Tribes in the State Legislature.
  • The Governor may make regulations concerning the Council's composition and procedure, subject to the constitutional framework.

Governor's regulatory powers

The Governor may make regulations for the peace and good government of a Scheduled Area. Such regulations may address matters including:

  • Transfer of land by or among members of Scheduled Tribes.
  • Regulation of allotment of land to members of Scheduled Tribes.
  • Regulation of money-lending to members of Scheduled Tribes.

Regulations affecting specified matters require the assent of the President. The Governor is also required to consult the Tribes Advisory Council where such a Council exists, as provided by the Schedule.

Role of the Governor and President

  • The Governor reports annually, or whenever required, to the President regarding the administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • The Union has an important supervisory and coordinating role in the administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • The President may issue directions to the State concerning the administration of Scheduled Areas.

5. Sixth Schedule: Autonomous District and Regional Councils

The Sixth Schedule provides a distinctive constitutional arrangement for tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. It seeks to protect tribal autonomy, customary practices, land and community institutions through autonomous councils.

Constitutional basis

  • Article 244(2).
  • Sixth Schedule to the Constitution.

Autonomous District Councils

The Governor may constitute autonomous districts and autonomous regions in accordance with the Sixth Schedule. Each autonomous district has a District Council.

A District Council ordinarily consists of up to 30 members, of whom not more than four may be nominated by the Governor and the remainder are elected on the basis of adult suffrage. The Schedule provides for the composition and tenure of these Councils, subject to its specific provisions.

Legislative powers

District and Regional Councils may make laws on specified subjects, including:

  • Land other than reserved forests.
  • Management of forests other than reserved forests.
  • Use of canals and watercourses for agriculture.
  • Regulation of shifting cultivation.
  • Village and town administration.
  • Appointment and succession of traditional chiefs.
  • Inheritance of property.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Social customs.

Such laws require the assent of the Governor as provided in the Schedule.

Judicial powers

The Councils may establish village councils or courts for specified disputes and cases involving parties belonging to Scheduled Tribes within the autonomous areas. The Schedule provides for jurisdiction and appellate arrangements.

Financial and administrative powers

  • Assessment and collection of specified land revenue.
  • Imposition of certain taxes, tolls and fees.
  • Management of markets and fairs.
  • Regulation of money-lending and trading by non-tribals, subject to the Schedule.
  • Management of primary education and other assigned functions.

Limitations and accountability

Autonomous Councils operate within the Constitution and the Sixth Schedule. Their powers are not equivalent to those of a State Legislature. The Governor retains constitutionally assigned responsibilities, and the relevant State Legislature and Parliament retain powers under the constitutional framework.

7. Special Provisions under Articles 371–371J

Part XXI contains special provisions for certain States. These provisions reflect historical agreements, regional aspirations, administrative needs and developmental disparities.

Article 371: Maharashtra and Gujarat

The President may provide for special responsibilities of the Governors of Maharashtra and Gujarat regarding the establishment of separate development boards for specified regions, equitable allocation of funds and adequate facilities for technical education and vocational training.

Article 371A: Nagaland

Article 371A provides that no Act of Parliament concerning specified matters shall apply to Nagaland unless the Nagaland Legislative Assembly so decides by resolution.

The protected matters include:

  • Religious or social practices of the Nagas.
  • Naga customary law and procedure.
  • Administration of civil and criminal justice involving decisions according to Naga customary law.
  • Ownership and transfer of land and its resources.

Article 371A also contains special administrative provisions relating to law and order and the Governor's role, subject to its constitutional conditions.

Article 371B: Assam

Article 371B provides for a committee of the Assam Legislative Assembly consisting of members elected from specified tribal areas and such other members as may be included under the constitutional arrangement.

Article 371C: Manipur

Article 371C provides for a committee of the Manipur Legislative Assembly consisting of members elected from the Hill Areas. The Governor has special responsibilities regarding the administration of the Hill Areas and submits reports to the President.

Article 371D: Andhra Pradesh and Telangana

Article 371D provides for equitable opportunities and facilities in public employment and education for people belonging to different parts of the State, through arrangements made by Presidential Order.

The provision was introduced in the context of regional imbalances and concerns over opportunities in employment and education. Its operation must be read with the relevant Presidential Orders and subsequent constitutional and legal developments.

Article 371E: Central University in Andhra Pradesh

Article 371E empowers Parliament by law to provide for the establishment of a university in Andhra Pradesh. It is a special provision but does not create the same type of regional autonomy as several other provisions in this group.

Article 371F: Sikkim

Article 371F contains special provisions relating to the admission of Sikkim into the Union and the protection of its distinctive political and institutional arrangements.

  • It provides for the number of Assembly seats and representation arrangements.
  • It enables Parliament to provide for the protection of the rights and interests of different sections of Sikkim's population.
  • It contains transitional and continuity provisions concerning existing laws and institutions.

Article 371G: Mizoram

Article 371G provides protections similar in broad character to Article 371A. Parliamentary laws concerning specified matters do not apply to Mizoram unless the Mizoram Legislative Assembly so decides by resolution.

The protected matters include:

  • Religious or social practices of the Mizos.
  • Mizo customary law and procedure.
  • Administration of civil and criminal justice involving customary law.
  • Ownership and transfer of land.

Article 371G also provides for a minimum strength of 40 members in the Mizoram Legislative Assembly.

Article 371H: Arunachal Pradesh

Article 371H gives the Governor special responsibility with respect to law and order in Arunachal Pradesh, subject to the constitutional provisions governing the exercise of this responsibility.

It also provides for a Legislative Assembly consisting of not less than 30 members.

Article 371I: Goa

Article 371I provides that the Legislative Assembly of Goa shall consist of not less than 30 members.

Article 371J: Karnataka

Article 371J provides special arrangements for the Hyderabad-Karnataka region, now officially referred to as the Kalyana Karnataka region.

  • Establishment of a development board for the region.
  • Equitable allocation of funds for developmental expenditure.
  • Reservation of seats in educational and vocational institutions.
  • Reservation in public employment for specified local cadres and posts, in accordance with constitutional and legal provisions.

8. Asymmetry in Union Territories

Union Territories are administered under a framework different from that applicable to States. They illustrate another dimension of differentiated federal arrangements.

Article 239

Every Union Territory is administered by the President acting through an administrator appointed by the President, unless Parliament provides otherwise.

Article 239AA: National Capital Territory of Delhi

Delhi has a special constitutional arrangement involving an elected Legislative Assembly and Council of Ministers, subject to specified exclusions and constitutional limitations.

  • The Legislative Assembly may legislate on matters in the State List and Concurrent List, except the excluded subjects specified in Article 239AA.
  • Parliament retains legislative power over Delhi.
  • The Council of Ministers aids and advises the Lieutenant Governor in matters within its constitutional domain.
  • Public order, police and land remain excluded from the Legislative Assembly's legislative competence under Article 239AA.

The constitutional relationship between the elected government and the Lieutenant Governor has been clarified through judicial interpretation, including the Supreme Court's Constitution Bench decisions concerning the division of powers and the role of constitutional authorities.

Puducherry

Article 239A empowers Parliament to create a Legislature and Council of Ministers for certain Union Territories, including Puducherry. The institutional framework is provided by parliamentary law.

The Union Territory of Jammu and Kashmir has a separate statutory framework under the Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019, and the Constitution as applicable.

9. Asymmetric Federalism and Tribal Self-Governance

Constitutional asymmetry is closely connected with the protection of tribal self-governance and community participation.

Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996

The PESA Act extends Part IX of the Constitution to Scheduled Areas subject to specified modifications and exceptions. It recognises the importance of Gram Sabhas and traditional community institutions in tribal self-governance.

  • Recognition of customary law, social and religious practices, and traditional management of community resources.
  • A central role for the Gram Sabha in safeguarding traditions, cultural identity and community resources.
  • Consultation before land acquisition and resettlement in the manner provided by law.
  • Powers concerning minor forest produce, village markets and local plans, subject to the statutory framework.

PESA is a statutory extension of local self-government to Scheduled Areas. It should not be confused with the autonomous district council structure of the Sixth Schedule.

Forest Rights Act, 2006

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006 recognises individual and community forest rights and provides for the role of Gram Sabhas in the statutory process.

These laws complement the constitutional framework by connecting recognition of community rights with democratic participation and local decision-making.

10. Asymmetry and the Principle of National Unity

Asymmetric federalism is not necessarily a challenge to national integration. It can strengthen national unity by recognising legitimate regional differences within a common constitutional framework.

Differentiated arrangements may help address regional grievances, protect minority cultures and provide space for democratic self-governance.

However, differentiated arrangements must remain consistent with constitutional values, equality, fundamental rights and democratic accountability.

Constitutional balance

  • Article 14 permits reasonable classification, subject to constitutional requirements.
  • Cultural and educational rights protect diversity.
  • Federalism accommodates regional autonomy within the Union.
  • Fundamental rights and judicial review provide constitutional safeguards.
  • Democratic institutions ensure that autonomy remains accountable to the people.

11. Judicial Perspective

Judicial interpretation has shaped the relationship between regional autonomy, constitutional powers and national integration.

R.C. Poudyal v. Union of India (1994)

The Supreme Court considered the special constitutional arrangements concerning Sikkim, including distinctive representation arrangements. The decision is relevant to the understanding that constitutional equality does not invariably require identical institutional arrangements in every context.

State of Nagaland v. R. R. P. R. S. Rao (2002)

The Court examined issues concerning the application of constitutional and statutory provisions in Nagaland's distinctive constitutional framework. It is relevant to understanding the interaction between special provisions and general legal arrangements.

Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018)

The Constitution Bench examined the constitutional relationship between the elected government of Delhi and the Lieutenant Governor. It emphasised representative government, constitutional morality, collaborative governance and the need to respect the constitutional division of responsibilities.

Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2023)

The Supreme Court considered the allocation of control over services in Delhi, interpreting Article 239AA and the constitutional distribution of responsibilities. Parliament subsequently enacted legislation concerning services in the National Capital Territory.

Significance of judicial review

  • Maintains constitutional boundaries of autonomy.
  • Protects democratic accountability.
  • Interprets the relationship between special and general provisions.
  • Prevents arbitrary exercise of constitutional power.
  • Balances regional interests with national constitutional principles.

12. Challenges of Asymmetric Federalism

  • Uneven institutional capacity: Autonomous institutions may lack adequate financial, administrative and technical resources.
  • Ambiguity in powers: Overlap between State governments, Governors and autonomous councils can create disputes.
  • Weak implementation: Constitutional autonomy may remain limited if powers are not effectively devolved.
  • Political centralisation: Excessive central intervention may weaken trust and regional participation.
  • Inter-community tensions: Autonomy arrangements may not always resolve competing claims among communities within a region.
  • Representation concerns: Smaller groups within autonomous regions may feel excluded from decision-making.
  • Developmental disparities: Special provisions do not automatically ensure improved social and economic outcomes.
  • Changing political expectations: Historical settlements may require periodic review as demographic, economic and political conditions change.
  • Accountability gaps: Differentiated institutions require transparent finances, public participation and effective oversight.

13. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Meaningful devolution: Transfer functions, functionaries and financial resources to autonomous institutions in accordance with constitutional and statutory provisions.
  2. Institutional clarity: Define the respective roles of Governors, State governments and autonomous councils to reduce administrative disputes.
  3. Strengthening local democracy: Ensure effective participation of Gram Sabhas, traditional institutions and elected autonomous councils.
  4. Financial empowerment: Improve predictable funding, transparent budgeting and accountability mechanisms.
  5. Protection of minority interests: Ensure that autonomy does not result in domination by a single community or group.
  6. Respect for customary institutions: Integrate customary practices with constitutional rights, gender equality and rule of law.
  7. Cooperative intergovernmental relations: Use regular consultations and institutional forums to resolve disagreements.
  8. Outcome-based evaluation: Assess whether special arrangements improve representation, development, public service delivery and social inclusion.
  9. Constitutional fidelity: Preserve the balance between regional autonomy, national unity, fundamental rights and democratic accountability.

14. Important Conceptual Distinctions

  • Symmetric vs asymmetric federalism: Symmetric federalism broadly provides similar constitutional arrangements to constituent units; asymmetric federalism permits differentiated arrangements.
  • Fifth Schedule vs Sixth Schedule: The Fifth Schedule provides a special administrative framework for Scheduled Areas; the Sixth Schedule creates autonomous district and regional councils in specified North Eastern States.
  • Special provisions vs separate sovereignty: Special constitutional arrangements operate within the sovereignty and constitutional framework of India.
  • Autonomous councils vs State Legislatures: Autonomous councils exercise powers assigned by the Sixth Schedule and do not possess the full legislative competence of State Legislatures.
  • PESA vs Sixth Schedule: PESA extends local self-government to Fifth Schedule areas; the Sixth Schedule establishes autonomous district and regional councils.
  • Article 371 provisions: These provisions are not identical. Each addresses distinct historical, geographical, administrative or developmental circumstances.

16. Integrated Revision Framework

Understand asymmetric federalism through four connected dimensions:

  1. Territorial and tribal administration: Fifth Schedule, Sixth Schedule and Article 244.
  2. State-specific arrangements: Articles 371–371J.
  3. Union Territory differentiation: Articles 239, 239A, 239AA and related provisions.
  4. Community self-governance: PESA, Gram Sabhas and the Forest Rights Act.

The central constitutional question is how India can accommodate regional diversity without weakening equality, democratic accountability, national integration and constitutional supremacy.
