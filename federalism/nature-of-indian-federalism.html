1. Meaning and Concept of Federalism

Federalism is a constitutional arrangement in which governmental powers are divided between a central authority and constituent regional governments. Each level derives its authority from the Constitution and exercises powers within its assigned sphere.

Unlike a merely administrative decentralisation, federalism constitutionally recognises more than one level of government. Regional governments are not simply subordinate agencies of the Union in matters assigned to them by the Constitution.

Core characteristics

  • Dual or multi-level government: Government operates at more than one constitutionally recognised level.
  • Constitutional distribution of powers: Legislative, executive and financial responsibilities are allocated by the Constitution.
  • Constitutional supremacy: Both levels operate within constitutional limits.
  • Written and relatively rigid Constitution: A written constitutional framework defines institutional powers; amendments may require special procedures.
  • Independent judiciary: Courts interpret the Constitution and adjudicate disputes concerning the distribution of powers.
  • Financial arrangements: Revenue powers, expenditure responsibilities and transfers support the functioning of different levels.

These features are analytical indicators rather than a rigid checklist. Federal systems differ in their degree of centralisation, institutional design and political practice.

2. Constitutional Design of Indian Federalism

The Constitution establishes a Union of States while distributing governmental responsibilities between the Union and the States. It combines federal institutions with significant centralising features, reflecting India's historical, territorial, social and security circumstances.

Important constitutional provisions

  • Article 1: India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States.
  • Article 2: Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new States.
  • Article 3: Parliament may form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States, subject to the prescribed procedure.
  • Article 4: Laws made under Articles 2 and 3 may amend the First and Fourth Schedules and are not treated as constitutional amendments under Article 368.
  • Articles 245–255: Legislative relations between the Union and States.
  • Articles 256–263: Administrative relations and intergovernmental coordination.
  • Articles 268–293: Financial relations.
  • Article 131: Original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in specified federal disputes.
  • Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule: Distribution of legislative subjects.
  • Article 368: Constitutional amendment, including State ratification for specified federal provisions.

3. Why India Adopted a Federal Structure

India's federal arrangement was shaped by the need to accommodate territorial diversity while maintaining political unity and effective national government.

  • Geographical scale: A large territory with substantial regional differences requires government closer to local conditions.
  • Linguistic and cultural diversity: Different languages, traditions and regional identities require constitutional space for political representation and self-government.
  • Historical experience: Colonial centralisation and the integration of princely States influenced the design of the post-independence Union.
  • National unity: Partition, integration challenges and security concerns encouraged a strong Union.
  • Democratic representation: State governments provide institutional channels for regional political preferences.
  • Developmental requirements: Different regions face different economic and social conditions, requiring differentiated policy responses.

Indian federalism therefore seeks to reconcile two constitutional objectives: the unity and integrity of India and meaningful regional self-government.

4. Federal Features of the Indian Constitution

4.1 Two levels of government

The Constitution recognises the Union and the States as constitutionally established levels of government. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments further strengthened local self-government by giving constitutional recognition to Panchayats and Municipalities.

4.2 Distribution of legislative powers

Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects across the Union List, State List and Concurrent List. This division is a central feature of the federal arrangement.

4.3 Written Constitution and constitutional supremacy

The Constitution defines the powers of public institutions and limits their exercise. Neither the Union nor a State can claim authority beyond constitutional competence.

4.4 Partly rigid amendment procedure

Article 368 prescribes a special procedure for constitutional amendments. Amendments affecting specified federal provisions require ratification by at least half of the State legislatures before being presented for presidential assent.

4.5 Independent judiciary

The Supreme Court and High Courts protect constitutional limits through judicial review. The Supreme Court's original jurisdiction under Article 131 provides a mechanism for adjudicating specified disputes between the Union and States or among States.

4.6 Bicameral Union legislature

Rajya Sabha represents the States and certain Union Territories at the Union level. It provides a federal dimension to parliamentary law-making, although representation is not equal among all States.

4.7 State governments with constitutionally assigned powers

State legislatures and executives exercise authority in their constitutionally assigned domains. State autonomy is subject to constitutional limitations and the specific provisions governing Union-State relations.

5. Unitary or Centralising Features

Indian federalism is not based on a strict separation in which the Union and States operate in complete isolation. The Constitution gives the Union substantial powers to preserve coordination, national integrity and uniformity where considered necessary.

  • Strong Union: The Union List contains important subjects of national significance, and Parliament has residuary legislative power under Article 248 read with Entry 97 of the Union List.
  • Single Constitution: The Constitution principally governs both the Union and the States, rather than providing separate State constitutions as a general rule.
  • Single citizenship: India provides for single citizenship rather than separate citizenship of the Union and individual States.
  • Parliamentary power over State boundaries: Article 3 enables Parliament to alter State areas, boundaries and names through the constitutional procedure.
  • Emergency provisions: Articles 352, 356 and 360 provide for exceptional constitutional arrangements that can significantly affect the normal federal distribution of authority.
  • Governor: The Governor is appointed by the President and occupies a constitutionally significant position in State administration.
  • Integrated judiciary: The judicial system has a unified institutional structure rather than entirely separate Union and State judicial systems.
  • All India Services: Article 312 permits the creation of services common to the Union and States, supporting administrative integration.
  • Parliamentary legislation on State subjects in specified circumstances: Articles 249, 250, 252 and 253 provide constitutionally defined routes for Parliament to legislate on matters otherwise within the State sphere.

These features do not by themselves make India unitary. They must be understood within the Constitution's overall distribution of powers and federal structure.

7. Nature of Indian Federalism: Constitutional and Judicial Understanding

7.1 Constituent Assembly perspective

The Constitution-makers chose the expression "Union of States" in Article 1. The formulation reflected the indestructible character of the Union and the absence of a constitutional right of secession. At the same time, the Constitution created States with constitutionally assigned powers.

7.2 State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)

The Supreme Court rejected the argument that Indian States possess sovereignty independent of the Constitution. The judgment emphasised the distinctive and strong Union structure of the Indian Constitution. It remains important for understanding the constitutional position of States within the Union.

7.3 Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament's power to amend the Constitution does not extend to destroying its basic structure. Federalism is recognised as one of the features associated with the Constitution's basic structure.

7.4 S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

The Supreme Court gave significant constitutional protection to federalism and limited arbitrary use of Article 356. The judgment established that a proclamation under Article 356 is subject to judicial review and that the legislative majority of a government should ordinarily be tested on the floor of the House.

7.5 Government of NCT of Delhi v. Union of India (2018 and 2023)

The Supreme Court examined the constitutional relationship between the elected government of the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union. The decisions are relevant to representative government, constitutional interpretation and the special governance arrangement for the national capital. Delhi's constitutional status must be distinguished from that of a full-fledged State.

7.6 Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional character of recommendations made by the GST Council. It explained the importance of cooperative federalism and recognised that Council recommendations have persuasive value rather than automatically binding legislative force on Parliament and State legislatures.

8. Types and Evolving Models of Federalism

8.1 Cooperative federalism

Cooperative federalism emphasises consultation, coordination and joint action between the Union and States. It recognises that many public policy problems require shared responsibility.

Examples: GST Council, Inter-State Council, NITI Aayog's Governing Council, centrally sponsored schemes and coordinated disaster management.

8.2 Competitive federalism

Competitive federalism encourages States to improve governance, investment conditions, public services and development outcomes through comparison and performance-based incentives.

Potential benefits: Innovation, administrative efficiency, investment and better service delivery.

Risks: Unequal starting capacities, a race to the bottom in regulation, excessive emphasis on rankings and widening regional disparities.

8.3 Collaborative federalism

Collaborative federalism involves sustained engagement among different levels of government and public institutions. It may include shared planning, technical assistance, joint implementation and common standards.

8.4 Fiscal federalism

Fiscal federalism concerns the allocation of taxation powers, expenditure responsibilities, revenue sharing, grants and borrowing authority among levels of government. It is essential because the level responsible for delivering a service may not control the revenue needed to finance it.

8.5 Asymmetric federalism

Asymmetric federalism recognises that different States or regions may require different constitutional, administrative or fiscal arrangements. Such differentiation can accommodate historical, geographical, cultural and tribal circumstances.

Examples include special provisions under Articles 371 and 371A–371J, and the constitutional framework for Sixth Schedule areas. The former special constitutional status of Jammu and Kashmir under Article 370 is a distinct historical and legal development and should not be treated as the current general arrangement.

9. Federalism and the Three-Tier Structure

Indian federalism has evolved beyond the Union-State relationship. The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments gave constitutional recognition to rural and urban local governments.

  • Union: Handles national responsibilities and subjects assigned to it by the Constitution.
  • States: Exercise constitutionally assigned legislative, executive and financial responsibilities.
  • Local bodies: Panchayats and Municipalities perform functions assigned under State law within the framework of Parts IX and IX-A.

Local government is not a third tier with the same constitutional position or autonomy as the Union and States. Its effective authority depends substantially on State legislation, devolution of functions, funds and functionaries, and institutional capacity.

10. Challenges in the Working of Indian Federalism

10.1 Centralisation of political and administrative power

Excessive concentration of decision-making at the Union level can weaken State autonomy and reduce the space for region-specific policy choices.

10.2 Governor's office and State autonomy

Disputes concerning gubernatorial discretion, assent to Bills, government formation and the timing of constitutional decisions can create friction between elected State governments and the Union.

10.3 Fiscal imbalance

States carry major responsibilities in sectors such as health, education, agriculture and policing, while important taxation powers are concentrated at the Union level. Transfers and grants are therefore central to the functioning of fiscal federalism.

10.4 Centrally sponsored schemes

National programmes can promote common development objectives, but rigid design, matching contribution requirements or limited flexibility may constrain State-specific implementation.

10.5 Inter-State disputes

Water sharing, boundary disputes, migration, environmental concerns and resource allocation can generate conflicts requiring effective intergovernmental institutions and dispute resolution.

10.6 Uneven development

Differences in revenue capacity, infrastructure, human development and administrative capability can produce unequal outcomes among States. Competitive federalism must therefore be balanced with equalisation and solidarity.

10.7 Regional identity and national integration

Language, culture, ethnicity and regional aspirations require democratic accommodation. Failure to address legitimate concerns can weaken trust in federal institutions.

10.8 Weak local devolution

Incomplete transfer of functions, funds and functionaries limits the ability of Panchayats and Municipalities to perform their constitutional role.

10.9 Institutional coordination deficits

Intergovernmental forums may be weakened by irregular meetings, inadequate follow-up, limited information sharing or insufficient dispute-resolution capacity.

11. Strengthening Indian Federalism: Reform Priorities

  • Institutionalise consultation: Strengthen the regular functioning of the Inter-State Council and other intergovernmental forums.
  • Respect constitutional conventions: Ensure that the Governor's office functions impartially and within constitutional limits.
  • Improve fiscal predictability: Promote transparent, timely and rule-based transfers and meaningful consultation on fiscal decisions.
  • Enhance State flexibility: Allow appropriate adaptation of national programmes to local needs while retaining agreed national objectives.
  • Strengthen dispute resolution: Improve institutional processes for resolving inter-State water, boundary and resource disputes.
  • Promote evidence-based cooperation: Share data, best practices and administrative capacity across governments.
  • Deepen local democracy: Transfer appropriate functions, funds and functionaries to local bodies and strengthen their accountability.
  • Balance competition with equalisation: Encourage innovation while supporting States with weaker fiscal and administrative capacity.
  • Build constitutional trust: Use dialogue and constitutional morality to resolve political disagreements within institutional processes.

14. Integrated Revision Framework

Study the nature of Indian federalism through five connected questions:

  1. Why federalism? Understand India's diversity, scale, democratic requirements and national unity concerns.
  2. What is distributed? Examine legislative, administrative and financial powers.
  3. Who interprets the boundaries? Understand the role of the Supreme Court and constitutional remedies.
  4. How do governments cooperate? Study intergovernmental councils, fiscal transfers and shared programmes.
  5. How can the system improve? Examine State autonomy, local devolution, institutional trust and cooperative mechanisms.

The central analytical theme is that Indian federalism is a dynamic constitutional balance between national coordination and regional autonomy. Its effectiveness depends not only on the formal distribution of powers but also on institutions, political conventions, fiscal capacity and democratic cooperation.
