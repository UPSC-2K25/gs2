1. Meaning and Significance of Urban Local Government

Urban local government refers to institutions responsible for governing cities and towns within a defined municipal area. These institutions provide local civic services, regulate urban activities, facilitate development planning and represent the interests of urban residents.

Urban governance is broader than municipal administration. It includes elected Municipalities, State departments, urban development authorities, public utilities, metropolitan agencies, private service providers and citizen organisations.

Why urban local government matters

  • Urbanisation increases demand for housing, transport, water and sanitation.
  • Local institutions are essential for everyday civic service delivery.
  • Urban planning requires coordination across land use, infrastructure and environment.
  • Municipal representation enables citizen participation in local decisions.
  • Effective urban governance supports economic productivity and inclusive growth.
  • Local accountability helps address neighbourhood-level service deficiencies.

2. Evolution of Urban Local Government in India

Colonial origins

  • The Madras Municipal Corporation, established in 1688, is generally recognised as India's earliest municipal corporation.
  • The municipal institutions of the colonial period were initially associated with administration, public health, taxation and civic regulation.
  • Lord Ripon's Resolution of 1882 promoted local self-government and greater participation of non-official members.
  • The Government of India Acts of 1919 and 1935 expanded provincial responsibility for local government.

Post-independence development

After Independence, urban local bodies continued under State laws. Their powers, finances and institutional structures varied across States. Rapid urbanisation and the expansion of urban infrastructure increased the need for stronger local institutions.

National Commission on Urbanisation, 1988

The National Commission on Urbanisation examined urban development and governance challenges and highlighted the importance of strengthening urban institutions, planning and financial capacity.

74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The Constitution (Seventy-fourth Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule. It came into force on 1 June 1993 and provided constitutional recognition to Municipalities.

3. Constitutional Framework: Part IXA

Part IXA of the Constitution, comprising Articles 243P to 243ZG, provides the constitutional framework for urban local government.

Article 243P: Definitions

Defines terms used in Part IXA, including metropolitan area, municipality, municipal area, population and related concepts.

Article 243Q: Constitution of Municipalities

Provides for three categories of urban local bodies:

  • Nagar Panchayat: For a transitional area moving from rural to urban status.
  • Municipal Council: For a smaller urban area.
  • Municipal Corporation: For a larger urban area.

The Governor specifies the relevant area after considering factors such as population, density of population, revenue generated for local administration, percentage of non-agricultural employment and economic importance.

An industrial township may be treated as an exception where municipal services are provided or proposed to be provided by an industrial establishment.

Article 243R: Composition

Members are generally elected directly from territorial constituencies known as wards. State law may provide for representation of persons with special knowledge or experience in municipal administration, Members of Parliament and State Legislatures representing the relevant areas, and other specified categories.

Article 243S: Ward Committees

Ward Committees are to be constituted in Municipalities with a population of three lakh or more. State law determines their composition and territorial jurisdiction.

They provide a mechanism for neighbourhood-level participation and monitoring of municipal services.

Article 243T: Reservation

  • Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population.
  • Not less than one-third of directly elected seats are reserved for women.
  • Not less than one-third of offices of Chairpersons are reserved for women in the manner provided by State law.
  • State Legislatures may provide reservation for backward classes.

Several States provide 50 per cent reservation for women under their respective municipal laws.

Article 243U: Duration

Every Municipality continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved under law. Elections must be completed before expiry of the term.

Where a Municipality is dissolved before the expiry of its term, elections must ordinarily be completed within six months, subject to the constitutional exception where the remainder of the term is less than six months.

Article 243V: Disqualifications

State law may specify disqualifications for membership, subject to constitutional requirements. A person must have attained the age of 21 years to contest a municipal election.

Article 243W: Powers and responsibilities

State Legislatures may endow Municipalities with powers and authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government. These may include preparation of plans for economic development and social justice and performance of functions relating to the Twelfth Schedule.

Article 243X: Municipal taxation and funds

State Legislatures may authorise Municipalities to levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees. They may assign revenues, provide grants-in-aid and establish municipal funds.

Article 243Y: Finance Commission

The State Finance Commission constituted under Article 243I also reviews the financial position of Municipalities and recommends principles concerning distribution of revenues, assigned taxes and grants-in-aid.

Article 243Z: Audit of accounts

State Legislatures may make provisions concerning maintenance and audit of municipal accounts.

Article 243ZA: Municipal elections

The State Election Commission supervises, directs and controls the preparation of electoral rolls and conduct of municipal elections.

Article 243ZB: Application to Union Territories

Part IXA applies to Union Territories subject to constitutional provisions and modifications specified by the President.

Article 243ZC: Exceptions

Part IXA does not automatically apply to the areas and institutions specified in the Constitution, including certain areas covered by Article 244 and the Sixth Schedule.

Article 243ZD: District Planning Committee

Provides for consolidation of plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities and preparation of a draft development plan for the district as a whole.

Article 243ZE: Metropolitan Planning Committee

Provides for a Metropolitan Planning Committee to prepare a draft development plan for a metropolitan area as a whole.

Article 243ZF: Continuance of existing laws

Existing municipal laws continued during the constitutionally specified transitional period unless amended or repealed earlier.

Article 243ZG: Bar to interference by courts

Delimitation and allotment of seats cannot ordinarily be questioned in court. Municipal elections may be challenged through election petitions in the manner provided by State law.

4. Twelfth Schedule: 18 Municipal Functions

The Twelfth Schedule lists 18 subjects that may be entrusted to Municipalities under Article 243W.

  1. Urban planning, including town planning.
  2. Regulation of land use and construction of buildings.
  3. Planning for economic and social development.
  4. Roads and bridges.
  5. Water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes.
  6. Public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid waste management.
  7. Fire services.
  8. Urban forestry, protection of the environment and promotion of ecological aspects.
  9. Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections, including persons with disabilities.
  10. Slum improvement and upgradation.
  11. Urban poverty alleviation.
  12. Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens and playgrounds.
  13. Promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects.
  14. Burials, burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds and electric crematoriums.
  15. Cattle pounds and prevention of cruelty to animals.
  16. Vital statistics, including registration of births and deaths.
  17. Public amenities, including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences.
  18. Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries.

These subjects are not automatically transferred to Municipalities merely because they appear in the Schedule. The actual distribution of responsibilities depends on State legislation and administrative arrangements.

5. Types of Urban Local Bodies

Nagar Panchayat

A Nagar Panchayat governs a transitional area that is moving from rural to urban characteristics. It is intended to address the changing service and planning requirements of settlements undergoing urbanisation.

Municipal Council

A Municipal Council governs a smaller urban area. Its responsibilities may include local roads, sanitation, water supply, public conveniences, building regulation and local development functions assigned by State law.

Municipal Corporation

A Municipal Corporation generally governs a larger urban area with more complex infrastructure and service requirements. It may have specialised departments and a larger administrative structure.

Comparison

Institution Area Typical governance context
Nagar Panchayat Transitional area Settlement moving from rural to urban status.
Municipal Council Smaller urban area Town-level civic administration.
Municipal Corporation Larger urban area Complex urban services and infrastructure.

The criteria for classifying urban areas and establishing particular municipal categories are specified by State law within the constitutional framework.

6. Municipal Political and Administrative Structure

Municipal Council or Corporation

The elected council represents residents through ward representatives and deliberates on municipal matters. Its powers and relationship with the executive vary across State municipal laws.

Mayor or Chairperson

The Mayor or Chairperson is the political head of the municipal institution. The mode of election, tenure and executive powers differ among States.

In some States, the Mayor has a primarily deliberative and representative role, while substantial executive authority rests with the municipal commissioner. Other States provide different arrangements.

Municipal Commissioner

The Municipal Commissioner is generally the chief executive or administrative officer of a Municipal Corporation. The Commissioner is commonly appointed through the State administrative system and implements municipal decisions and statutory responsibilities.

Standing committees

Municipal laws may provide for standing committees dealing with finance, public works, health, education and other municipal functions. These committees support specialised scrutiny and deliberation.

Ward-level institutions

Ward Committees and other local committees can connect residents with municipal decision-making and improve monitoring of neighbourhood services.

7. Ward Committees and Citizen Participation

Ward Committees are constitutionally envisaged under Article 243S for Municipalities with a population of three lakh or more.

Potential functions

  • Identifying neighbourhood service deficiencies.
  • Facilitating resident participation in local decisions.
  • Monitoring sanitation, street lighting and local amenities.
  • Communicating ward-level priorities to the Municipality.
  • Supporting public disclosure and grievance redressal.

Challenges

  • Irregular meetings and weak institutional support.
  • Limited awareness among residents.
  • Inadequate authority over municipal services.
  • Weak representation of marginalised communities.
  • Insufficient access to municipal budgets and service information.

Citizen participation should extend beyond consultation. Residents need access to information, meaningful opportunities to influence priorities and clear mechanisms for tracking municipal responses.

8. Urban Planning and Land-Use Governance

Urban planning coordinates land use, housing, transport, infrastructure, economic activity and environmental protection. It is essential for managing urban growth and preventing unplanned expansion.

Major planning instruments

  • Master plans and development plans.
  • Land-use zoning and development control regulations.
  • Building regulations and occupancy requirements.
  • Transport and mobility plans.
  • Housing and slum improvement plans.
  • Water, sewerage and drainage plans.
  • Environmental and disaster-risk plans.

Role of Municipalities

Municipalities may be assigned responsibilities relating to urban planning, land-use regulation, building permissions and local infrastructure. However, State-level urban development authorities may retain important planning powers.

Planning challenges

  • Fragmented authority across multiple agencies.
  • Outdated master plans and weak enforcement.
  • Expansion of informal settlements.
  • Mismatch between land use and transport infrastructure.
  • Insufficient attention to climate and disaster risks.
  • Limited public participation in planning decisions.

9. District and Metropolitan Planning

District Planning Committee: Article 243ZD

The District Planning Committee consolidates plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities in the district and prepares a draft development plan for the district as a whole.

Not less than four-fifths of its total members are to be elected by and from among the elected members of district-level Panchayats and Municipalities in proportion to the ratio between rural and urban populations in the district.

Metropolitan Planning Committee: Article 243ZE

A Metropolitan Planning Committee is to be constituted for every metropolitan area to prepare a draft development plan for the metropolitan area as a whole.

Not less than two-thirds of its members are to be elected by and from among elected members of Municipalities and Chairpersons of Panchayats in the metropolitan area, in proportion to the ratio between municipal and Panchayat populations.

What is a metropolitan area?

The Constitution defines a metropolitan area as an area having a population of ten lakh or more, comprised in one or more districts and consisting of two or more Municipalities, Panchayats or other contiguous areas specified by the Governor through public notification.

Importance of metropolitan planning

  • Coordinates land use across municipal boundaries.
  • Integrates transport, housing and infrastructure.
  • Addresses shared water and environmental resources.
  • Supports regional economic development.
  • Reduces duplication and fragmented investments.

Implementation gap

Metropolitan Planning Committees have often been constrained by limited authority, weak institutional capacity, fragmented agency structures and inadequate integration with State-level planning bodies.

10. Municipal Finance

Municipal finance determines whether urban local bodies can perform their assigned functions. A Municipality without adequate and predictable revenue cannot maintain infrastructure or deliver reliable services.

Own-source revenues

  • Property tax.
  • Fees and licences.
  • User charges for municipal services.
  • Advertisement-related charges where authorised.
  • Other taxes and duties permitted under State law.

Assigned revenues

State governments may assign specified revenues or shares of taxes to Municipalities under State legislation.

Grants-in-aid

Municipalities receive transfers and grants from State and Union government programmes. The conditions attached to grants may influence how funds can be used.

State Finance Commission

Under Articles 243I and 243Y, the State Finance Commission reviews the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities. It recommends principles for revenue distribution, assignment of taxes and grants-in-aid.

Union Finance Commission

Article 280 provides for the Union Finance Commission to recommend measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Municipalities, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.

Municipal bonds

Municipal bonds are debt instruments through which eligible urban local bodies can raise capital from investors for infrastructure and other permissible purposes.

Their viability depends on credible accounts, predictable revenues, project appraisal, debt management and transparent financial disclosure.

Financial constraints

  • Low property tax coverage and weak valuation systems.
  • Inadequate collection of user charges.
  • Dependence on transfers and tied grants.
  • Delayed release of funds.
  • Weak financial reporting and accounting systems.
  • Limited capacity to prepare bankable infrastructure projects.
  • Mismatch between expenditure responsibilities and available revenue.

11. Urban Service Delivery

Water supply

Urban water governance involves source protection, treatment, distribution, metering, maintenance and equitable access. Institutional responsibility may be divided between Municipalities, water boards and State agencies.

Sanitation and sewerage

Effective urban sanitation requires sewerage networks, wastewater treatment, safe disposal and faecal sludge management where sewer networks are absent.

Solid waste management

  • Segregation at source.
  • Door-to-door collection.
  • Material recovery and recycling.
  • Scientific processing and disposal.
  • Protection of sanitation workers.
  • Public participation and behaviour change.

Urban roads and transport

Municipalities may manage local roads, parking, footpaths and related infrastructure. Larger transport systems often require coordination with State transport agencies and metropolitan institutions.

Public health

Urban local institutions support public health through sanitation, environmental health, disease prevention, registration of births and deaths and coordination with health departments.

Urban green spaces

Parks, urban forests, lakes and other public spaces contribute to ecological resilience, public health and quality of life.

12. Urban Poverty and Slum Governance

Urban poverty is multidimensional. It includes inadequate income, insecure housing, poor access to water and sanitation, informal employment, vulnerability to eviction and limited access to public services.

Role of Municipalities

  • Identification of local service gaps.
  • Slum improvement and upgradation.
  • Coordination of housing and livelihood programmes.
  • Provision of basic civic amenities.
  • Support for inclusion in urban planning.
  • Facilitation of community participation.

Challenges

  • Insecure land tenure and informal housing.
  • Inadequate access to water, sanitation and drainage.
  • Limited participation of informal settlements in planning.
  • Displacement and livelihood disruption.
  • Fragmented responsibility among municipal and development agencies.

Inclusive urban governance requires recognising informal settlements in planning, improving essential services and protecting the dignity and livelihoods of vulnerable residents.

13. Urban Governance and Climate Resilience

Urban areas face increasing risks from heatwaves, floods, water scarcity, air pollution and extreme weather events. Local institutions play an important role in reducing exposure and strengthening resilience.

Major urban risks

  • Urban flooding caused by inadequate drainage and loss of natural water bodies.
  • Urban heat island effects and limited green cover.
  • Water stress and depletion of local water resources.
  • Air pollution from transport, construction and other sources.
  • Waste management failures and environmental degradation.
  • Disaster vulnerability in informal and low-lying settlements.

Local governance responses

  • Integrate climate risks into land-use and infrastructure planning.
  • Protect lakes, wetlands, drainage channels and urban green spaces.
  • Improve stormwater drainage and flood management.
  • Promote energy-efficient buildings and public transport.
  • Strengthen local disaster preparedness and emergency response.
  • Include vulnerable communities in resilience planning.

14. Major Urban Development Programmes

Smart Cities Mission

The Smart Cities Mission was designed to promote selected urban areas through improved infrastructure, technology-enabled services and area-based development.

Its implementation experience raises important governance questions concerning municipal accountability, Special Purpose Vehicles, citizen participation and integration with ordinary municipal systems.

Atal Mission for Rejuvenation and Urban Transformation

AMRUT focused on improving basic urban infrastructure, particularly water supply, sewerage and related services. AMRUT 2.0 expanded the emphasis on water security and sustainable urban water management.

Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban

The urban component of Swachh Bharat Mission promotes sanitation, elimination of open defecation, solid waste management and improved cleanliness.

Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana-Urban

PMAY-Urban was designed to address urban housing needs through different implementation components and support mechanisms. Municipal bodies and State agencies have roles in local implementation under applicable guidelines.

National Urban Livelihoods Mission

The urban livelihoods framework seeks to support skill development, self-employment and livelihoods among the urban poor, including vulnerable groups and street vendors.

Governance lessons

  • Scheme implementation should strengthen, not bypass, elected Municipalities.
  • Urban projects need long-term operation and maintenance plans.
  • Local priorities should inform project selection.
  • Performance information and expenditure should be transparent.
  • Coordination across schemes and agencies is essential.

15. Fragmented Urban Governance

A major institutional challenge is the division of urban responsibilities among Municipalities and multiple specialised agencies.

Common institutional actors

  • Municipal Corporations and Municipal Councils.
  • Urban development authorities.
  • Water supply and sewerage boards.
  • Transport and metropolitan transport agencies.
  • Housing boards and development corporations.
  • State urban development departments.
  • Special Purpose Vehicles created for specific programmes.

Consequences

  • Overlapping responsibilities and unclear accountability.
  • Weak coordination of infrastructure investments.
  • Limited control of elected Municipalities over essential services.
  • Difficulty integrating land use and transport planning.
  • Multiple channels of public expenditure without unified oversight.

Institutional reform

Urban governance requires clear assignment of responsibilities, coordination mechanisms and public accountability. Elected Municipalities should have a meaningful role in planning and oversight of services delivered within their jurisdiction.

16. Committees and Reform Perspectives

National Commission on Urbanisation, 1988

Examined the role of urbanisation in India's development and highlighted the need for integrated urban planning and stronger institutional arrangements.

Second Administrative Reforms Commission

Its work on local governance and public administration provides recommendations relevant to decentralisation, accountability and service delivery.

Finance Commission recommendations

Union Finance Commissions have recommended grants and reforms to strengthen the financial capacity of urban local bodies. State Finance Commissions provide the constitutional mechanism for reviewing municipal finances within each State.

Key reform themes

  • Strengthen elected urban local government.
  • Improve municipal financial autonomy.
  • Clarify the division of responsibilities among agencies.
  • Make metropolitan planning institutions effective.
  • Build professional municipal capacity.
  • Improve citizen participation and transparency.

17. Challenges in Urban Local Government

  1. Incomplete devolution: The transfer of Twelfth Schedule functions remains uneven across States.
  2. Financial weakness: Limited own-source revenue and dependence on transfers constrain municipal autonomy.
  3. Administrative dependence: Municipalities may lack control over key personnel and technical departments.
  4. Fragmented governance: Multiple agencies create overlapping authority and accountability gaps.
  5. Weak planning: Master plans and metropolitan coordination mechanisms may not respond effectively to rapid urban growth.
  6. Inadequate participation: Ward Committees and participatory forums may remain inactive or under-resourced.
  7. Capacity constraints: Municipalities often need more trained staff in planning, finance, engineering and data management.
  8. Social exclusion: Informal settlements, migrants and vulnerable communities may have limited influence on urban decisions.
  9. Environmental stress: Urban growth can intensify flooding, heat, pollution and water scarcity.
  10. Weak accountability: Complex institutional arrangements make it difficult for citizens to identify who is responsible for service failures.

18. Way Forward

  1. Implement the 3Fs: Transfer functions, funds and functionaries in a coordinated manner.
  2. Strengthen municipal revenue: Modernise property tax assessment, improve collection and rationalise user charges while protecting vulnerable households.
  3. Improve fiscal transfers: Ensure timely State Finance Commissions and predictable intergovernmental transfers.
  4. Clarify agency responsibilities: Align urban development authorities and service agencies with accountable municipal and metropolitan planning arrangements.
  5. Strengthen elected leadership: Clarify the powers and accountability of Mayors, Chairpersons, councils and municipal executives.
  6. Build professional capacity: Recruit and train municipal personnel in planning, finance, engineering, environmental management and digital governance.
  7. Make planning inclusive: Involve residents, informal settlements, businesses and vulnerable groups in planning.
  8. Empower Ward Committees: Provide clear responsibilities, resources and access to municipal information.
  9. Strengthen metropolitan institutions: Make Metropolitan Planning Committees effective platforms for regional coordination.
  10. Improve municipal transparency: Publish budgets, contracts, service standards, performance data and audit findings.
  11. Promote climate-resilient cities: Integrate water security, green infrastructure, flood management and heat mitigation into local plans.
  12. Focus on service outcomes: Assess performance through reliability, accessibility, quality and citizen satisfaction.

21. Integrated Revision Framework

Urban local government can be understood through six connected dimensions:

  1. Constitutional foundation: Part IXA and the Twelfth Schedule.
  2. Political institutions: Municipal Councils, Corporations, Mayors and Ward Committees.
  3. Planning: Land-use regulation, District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees.
  4. Finance: Own-source revenue, transfers, State Finance Commissions and municipal borrowing.
  5. Service delivery: Water, sanitation, roads, public health, housing and waste management.
  6. Accountability: Citizen participation, transparency, audit and performance assessment.

The central governance challenge is to make urban local bodies genuine institutions of self-government rather than merely local implementing agencies. This requires meaningful authority, adequate resources, professional capacity and accountability to urban residents.
