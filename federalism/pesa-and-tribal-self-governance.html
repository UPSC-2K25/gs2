1. Introduction: Tribal Self-Governance in India

Tribal self-governance refers to the participation and decision-making authority of Scheduled Tribe communities in managing their social, cultural, economic and community affairs. It recognises the importance of customary institutions, community resources, traditional practices and local participation in development.

India's constitutional framework recognises the distinct circumstances of Scheduled Areas and tribal communities. The Fifth Schedule, the Sixth Schedule, the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 (PESA), and the Forest Rights Act, 2006 provide important but distinct protections.

PESA seeks to adapt the Panchayati Raj framework to the social and institutional conditions of Fifth Schedule areas. It places the Gram Sabha at the centre of local self-governance.

2. Constitutional Framework

Article 244

  • Article 244(1) provides for the administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram under the Fifth Schedule.
  • Article 244(2) provides for the administration of tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram under the Sixth Schedule.

Fifth Schedule

The Fifth Schedule deals with the administration and control of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes. It provides for the role of the Governor, the Tribes Advisory Council and special legislative arrangements.

Article 243M

Article 243M generally excludes certain areas and States from the ordinary application of Part IX of the Constitution. However, Article 243M(4)(b) empowers Parliament to extend Part IX to Scheduled Areas and tribal areas, subject to exceptions and modifications.

Parliament exercised this power through PESA, 1996, extending the Panchayat framework to Fifth Schedule areas with specified modifications.

Article 338A

Article 338A establishes the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes. It provides an institutional mechanism for monitoring safeguards, investigating complaints and advising on measures for the protection and development of Scheduled Tribes.

3. Historical Background and Evolution of PESA

The ordinary Panchayati Raj framework could not simply be applied to Scheduled Areas without accounting for tribal customs, community institutions, traditional resource management and the distinct constitutional safeguards applicable to these areas.

Important developments

  • Fifth Schedule: Established a special constitutional framework for the administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • 73rd Constitutional Amendment: Constitutionalised Panchayati Raj institutions through Part IX, but excluded Scheduled Areas from its ordinary application.
  • Bhuri Singh Committee / Bhuria Committee: The Committee on the extension of Panchayati Raj to Scheduled Areas, chaired by Dilip Singh Bhuria, examined how democratic decentralisation could be adapted to tribal conditions.
  • PESA, 1996: Parliament enacted the Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996.
  • Forest Rights Act, 2006: Recognised individual and community forest rights and assigned an important role to the Gram Sabha.

4. PESA Act, 1996: Scope and Applicability

The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 extends Part IX of the Constitution to the Scheduled Areas referred to in Article 244(1), subject to the modifications specified in the Act.

Areas covered

PESA applies to areas declared as Scheduled Areas under the Fifth Schedule. It does not automatically apply to every tribal-populated area in India.

The Scheduled Areas are notified under the constitutional framework. Their identification and extent are distinct from the general presence of a Scheduled Tribe population in a district or State.

Areas not covered by PESA

  • Sixth Schedule tribal areas governed by the autonomous district and regional council framework.
  • States and areas constitutionally excluded from the relevant extension of Part IX.
  • Tribal-majority areas that have not been notified as Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas, merely by reason of their tribal population.

State legislation

State Panchayat laws applicable to Scheduled Areas must be consistent with the mandatory features of PESA. The Act required States to bring their laws into conformity within one year of its commencement.

5. Meaning and Importance of the Gram Sabha

The Gram Sabha is the foundational institution of village-level democratic participation. Under the constitutional framework, it consists of persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village within the area of a Panchayat at the village level.

PESA gives particular importance to the Gram Sabha in Scheduled Areas by recognising the role of community institutions and customary practices.

Village under PESA

For the purposes of PESA, a village ordinarily consists of a habitation, or a group of habitations, or a hamlet, or a group of hamlets comprising a community that manages its affairs according to traditions and customs.

This conception is important because it does not necessarily equate a village with an administrative revenue village. It recognises the social and customary community as a basis for self-governance.

Why the Gram Sabha matters

  • It provides a forum for direct democratic participation.
  • It recognises customary institutions and community practices.
  • It enables local deliberation on development priorities.
  • It strengthens community oversight over public resources.
  • It can protect vulnerable communities against exclusion and displacement.
  • It links local development with social accountability.

6. Core Principles of PESA

  • Respect for customary law: State legislation should be consistent with customary law, social and religious practices and traditional management practices of community resources.
  • Community-centred governance: The Gram Sabha is recognised as a central institution for local decision-making.
  • Democratic participation: Local plans and development decisions should involve affected communities.
  • Protection of community resources: Tribal communities should have meaningful authority over resources and local institutions within the statutory framework.
  • Social justice: Local governance should protect vulnerable groups and prevent exploitation.
  • Subsidiarity: Decisions should be taken at the lowest appropriate level, consistent with law and the capacity of local institutions.

7. Powers and Functions of the Gram Sabha and Panchayats

Section 4 of PESA specifies the principles and mandatory features governing the extension of Part IX to Scheduled Areas.

A. Safeguarding traditions and community identity

State legislation must be consistent with customary law, social and religious practices and traditional management practices of community resources. The Gram Sabha is competent to safeguard and preserve:

  • Traditions and customs of the people.
  • Their cultural identity.
  • Community resources.
  • Customary modes of dispute resolution.

B. Approval of development plans

The Gram Sabha or the appropriate level of Panchayat must approve plans, programmes and projects for social and economic development before they are taken up for implementation by the Panchayat at the village level.

C. Identification of beneficiaries

The Gram Sabha is responsible for identifying or selecting beneficiaries under poverty alleviation and other programmes, in accordance with the statutory framework.

D. Certification of utilisation of funds

The Gram Sabha is responsible for certifying the utilisation of funds by the Panchayat for plans, programmes and projects referred to in the Act.

E. Consultation in land acquisition and rehabilitation

The Gram Sabha or the appropriate level of Panchayat must be consulted before land acquisition in Scheduled Areas for development projects and before resettling or rehabilitating persons affected by such projects.

The statutory language requires consultation. It should not be casually described as an unconditional veto or as a universal consent requirement under PESA itself. Other applicable laws may prescribe additional consent or consultation safeguards.

F. Minor water bodies

Planning and management of minor water bodies in Scheduled Areas are to be entrusted to Panchayats at the appropriate level, in accordance with State law.

G. Minor minerals

The recommendations of the Gram Sabha or the appropriate level of Panchayat are mandatory before the grant of prospecting licences or mining leases for minor minerals in Scheduled Areas, as provided by PESA.

H. Minor forest produce

PESA provides for the ownership of minor forest produce to Panchayats at the appropriate level and the Gram Sabha, subject to the statutory framework.

I. Prevention of land alienation

PESA requires State legislation to endow Panchayats at the appropriate level and the Gram Sabha with powers to prevent alienation of land in Scheduled Areas and to take appropriate action to restore unlawfully alienated land of Scheduled Tribes.

J. Village markets

The management of village markets is to be entrusted to Panchayats at the appropriate level. This can help local communities regulate market practices and reduce exploitation by intermediaries.

K. Money lending

State legislation must provide powers to Gram Sabhas and Panchayats at the appropriate level to exercise control over money lending to Scheduled Tribes.

L. Social sector institutions and functionaries

PESA requires State legislation to confer control over institutions and functionaries in social sectors on Panchayats at the appropriate level.

M. Local plans and resources

Panchayats at the appropriate level are to be entrusted with control over local plans and resources for such plans, including tribal sub-plans, subject to the statutory framework.

8. Reservation and Representation under PESA

PESA modifies the ordinary Panchayat reservation framework to reflect the demographic and constitutional position of Scheduled Tribes in Scheduled Areas.

  • Reservation of seats for Scheduled Tribes in Panchayats in Scheduled Areas shall not be less than one-half of the total number of seats.
  • All seats of Chairpersons of Panchayats at all levels shall be reserved for Scheduled Tribes.
  • Where Scheduled Tribes do not have representation in an intermediate or district-level Panchayat, the State may nominate members in accordance with the Act, subject to the statutory limit.
  • The number of nominated members shall not exceed one-tenth of the total members to be elected in that Panchayat.

These provisions seek to strengthen representation and leadership of tribal communities in institutions governing Scheduled Areas.

9. PESA and Natural Resource Governance

Natural resources are closely connected with tribal livelihoods, cultural identity and community autonomy. PESA seeks to strengthen community participation in their management.

Land

  • Prevention of alienation of tribal land.
  • Action for restoration of unlawfully alienated land.
  • Consultation before specified land acquisition and rehabilitation processes.
  • Recognition of the relationship between land, culture and community identity.

Forests

  • Ownership of minor forest produce under PESA.
  • Protection of community resource management practices.
  • Complementarity with the Forest Rights Act, 2006.

Water

  • Planning and management of minor water bodies.
  • Community participation in the management of local water resources.
  • Recognition of local livelihood and ecological requirements.

Minerals

  • Mandatory recommendations before specified minor mineral licences and leases.
  • Participation of affected communities in resource-related decisions.
  • Need for transparency in revenue, environmental safeguards and rehabilitation.

10. PESA and the Forest Rights Act, 2006

The Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006, commonly known as the Forest Rights Act (FRA), recognises forest rights of eligible Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers.

Important forest rights

  • Individual rights over forest land under the conditions specified in the Act.
  • Community rights such as nistar and access to customary resources.
  • Rights over minor forest produce.
  • Community forest resource rights.
  • Rights relating to habitat and habitation of Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups and pre-agricultural communities.
  • Rights to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage community forest resources.

Role of the Gram Sabha under FRA

The Gram Sabha initiates the process for determining the nature and extent of individual and community forest rights. It receives, consolidates and verifies claims and passes resolutions for consideration by the subsequent statutory committees.

The Gram Sabha's role under FRA is therefore important but operates within the statutory claims and verification process.

PESA and FRA: relationship

Dimension PESA, 1996 Forest Rights Act, 2006
Primary focus Self-governance in Fifth Schedule areas Recognition and vesting of forest rights
Legal character Parliamentary legislation extending Part IX with modifications Parliamentary legislation recognising forest rights
Key institution Gram Sabha and Panchayats Gram Sabha and statutory forest rights committees
Resource focus Community resources, minor forest produce, land, water and local markets Individual and community forest rights and community forest resource management
Geographical scope Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas Eligible forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribes and other traditional forest dwellers, subject to the Act

PESA and FRA are complementary but not interchangeable. PESA concerns local self-government in Fifth Schedule areas, while FRA recognises specified forest rights and establishes a process for their determination.

11. PESA and the Land Acquisition Framework

Land acquisition in tribal areas raises concerns involving livelihood, displacement, community identity, cultural practices and access to natural resources.

PESA safeguard

PESA requires consultation with the Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat before land acquisition in Scheduled Areas for development projects and before resettlement or rehabilitation of persons affected by such projects.

Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act, 2013

The 2013 land acquisition law contains special safeguards for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, including provisions concerning acquisition in Scheduled Areas, consent or consultation requirements in specified circumstances, rehabilitation and resettlement and restoration-related protections.

The applicable requirement must be determined from the relevant statutory provisions and circumstances. PESA consultation should not be treated as a substitute for any additional requirement under the 2013 Act or other applicable law.

12. PESA and the Sixth Schedule: A Comparison

Both the Fifth and Sixth Schedules provide special constitutional arrangements for tribal areas, but their institutional structures are different.

Dimension Fifth Schedule and PESA Sixth Schedule
Constitutional basis Article 244(1) and Fifth Schedule Article 244(2) and Sixth Schedule
Principal institutional framework Panchayats and Gram Sabhas under PESA Autonomous District Councils and Regional Councils
Geographical application Notified Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas Specified tribal areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram
Legislative powers Local powers within the Panchayat and State legislative framework Specified legislative powers over listed subjects, subject to constitutional provisions
Judicial arrangements Customary dispute resolution may be safeguarded within the legal framework Specified powers relating to village and district council courts
Relationship with Part IX Part IX extended with PESA modifications Sixth Schedule areas are constitutionally excluded from ordinary Part IX application

The two frameworks reflect different constitutional approaches to tribal self-governance. PESA does not create an Autonomous District Council system identical to that under the Sixth Schedule.

13. PESA and Other Tribal Safeguards

Article 46

Article 46 directs the State to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and protect them from social injustice and exploitation.

Article 275(1)

Article 275(1) provides for grants-in-aid from the Union to States for promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and raising the level of administration in Scheduled Areas, as provided by the constitutional framework.

Article 338A

The National Commission for Scheduled Tribes monitors constitutional and legal safeguards, investigates complaints and advises on measures for the protection and development of Scheduled Tribes.

Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989

This legislation provides criminal law protections against specified offences and atrocities. It is distinct from PESA, which concerns local self-governance in Scheduled Areas.

Tribal Sub-Plan and development planning

Tribal development planning and budgetary allocation are important instruments for addressing development gaps. PESA's recognition of local control over plans and resources supports the need to align development expenditure with community priorities.

14. Important Judicial Decisions

Samatha v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1997)

The Supreme Court considered the legality of mining leases in Scheduled Areas under the applicable Andhra Pradesh Scheduled Areas Land Transfer Regulation. The judgment is important for the protection of tribal land and restrictions on transfer of land in Scheduled Areas.

The case preceded PESA. It should not be described as a direct interpretation of the PESA Act.

Orissa Mining Corporation Ltd. v. Ministry of Environment and Forests (2013)

In the Niyamgiri matter, the Supreme Court recognised the role of Gram Sabhas in determining claims relating to religious and community rights under the Forest Rights Act. The decision reinforced the importance of community participation and statutory Gram Sabha processes in decisions affecting forest-dwelling communities.

The judgment principally concerned the Forest Rights Act and related legal protections; it should not be represented as a general ruling that PESA creates a universal veto over all mining activity.

15. Implementation of PESA: Institutional and Administrative Challenges

  1. Incomplete State conformity: Some State laws and rules have not fully incorporated the mandatory features of PESA.
  2. Weak Gram Sabha authority: Administrative practice may treat the Gram Sabha as a formal meeting rather than an institution with meaningful decision-making responsibilities.
  3. Ambiguity in village boundaries: Differences between customary habitations and administrative village boundaries can affect representation and participation.
  4. Limited awareness: Community members may not know their statutory rights and powers.
  5. Administrative resistance: Departments and agencies may be reluctant to transfer authority over local institutions and resources.
  6. Resource conflicts: Mining, infrastructure and commercial activities can create conflicts over land, forests and water.
  7. Weak financial devolution: Gram Sabhas and Panchayats may lack predictable funds and technical support.
  8. Capacity constraints: Elected representatives and community institutions may lack training in legal, financial and administrative matters.
  9. Elite capture: Local decision-making may be dominated by influential groups, weakening participation of women and marginalised households.
  10. Coordination gaps: PESA, FRA, land acquisition laws and departmental programmes may be implemented without adequate institutional coordination.

16. Gender and Inclusion in Tribal Self-Governance

Tribal self-governance must ensure that the recognition of community institutions does not reproduce exclusion within those institutions.

  • Ensure meaningful participation of tribal women in Gram Sabha deliberations.
  • Strengthen women's representation in elected Panchayat institutions.
  • Protect the participation of Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups and remote communities.
  • Ensure accessible meeting procedures and information in local languages.
  • Address barriers arising from poverty, distance, literacy and social hierarchy.
  • Ensure that customary practices operate consistently with constitutional rights and statutory protections.

17. PESA and Development: Balancing Rights and Public Interest

Scheduled Areas may contain minerals, forests, water resources and other assets important to national and regional development. At the same time, these resources may sustain tribal livelihoods, cultural practices and community identity.

Effective governance must balance:

  • Constitutional protection of tribal communities.
  • Community participation in development decisions.
  • Environmental protection and sustainable resource use.
  • Infrastructure and public service requirements.
  • Fair compensation and effective rehabilitation where legally applicable.
  • Recognition of customary rights and community institutions.

Development in Scheduled Areas should be participatory, rights-based and sensitive to local ecological and social conditions. Consultation should be informed, timely and meaningful rather than merely procedural.

18. Reforms and the Way Forward

  1. Harmonise State laws: Bring State Panchayat laws, rules and administrative procedures into conformity with the mandatory provisions of PESA.
  2. Recognise customary villages: Ensure that village identification and Gram Sabha boundaries reflect the statutory definition and local community realities.
  3. Strengthen Gram Sabha authority: Make approval, consultation, recommendations and certification processes effective and transparent.
  4. Build legal awareness: Provide accessible information about PESA rights in tribal languages and through community institutions.
  5. Deepen fiscal devolution: Provide adequate and predictable financial resources to local institutions.
  6. Strengthen resource governance: Ensure meaningful community participation in decisions concerning land, minor forest produce, water bodies and minor minerals.
  7. Improve coordination: Align PESA implementation with FRA, land acquisition safeguards, environmental laws and tribal development programmes.
  8. Build institutional capacity: Train elected representatives, Gram Sabha members and local officials in planning, accounts, legal procedures and resource management.
  9. Ensure social inclusion: Strengthen the participation of women, vulnerable groups and remote communities.
  10. Establish accountability mechanisms: Publish records of Gram Sabha decisions, development plans, expenditure and action taken by authorities.
  11. Monitor outcomes: Evaluate implementation through the extent of actual community control, protection of rights and improvement in local development.

20. Key Conceptual Distinctions

  • Fifth Schedule and Sixth Schedule: Distinct constitutional arrangements for tribal administration.
  • PESA and FRA: PESA concerns self-governance in Fifth Schedule areas; FRA recognises specified forest rights.
  • Gram Sabha and Gram Panchayat: The Gram Sabha is the body of registered electors at village level; the Gram Panchayat is the elected local government institution.
  • Consultation and consent: Consultation under PESA must not be automatically equated with a universal consent requirement. Other laws may impose additional requirements.
  • Minor forest produce and minor minerals: PESA recognises ownership of minor forest produce and mandates recommendations before specified minor mineral licences and leases.
  • Tribal welfare and tribal self-governance: Welfare programmes provide services and resources; self-governance also requires meaningful community authority over decisions.

21. Integrated Revision Framework

PESA can be understood through five connected dimensions:

  1. Constitutional foundation: Article 244, the Fifth Schedule and Article 243M.
  2. Institutional foundation: Gram Sabha, Panchayats and customary community institutions.
  3. Decision-making powers: Development plans, beneficiaries, land acquisition consultation, minor minerals, markets and local resources.
  4. Rights and safeguards: Protection of land, forests, community resources, culture and tribal identity.
  5. Implementation: State conformity, fiscal and administrative capacity, awareness, inclusion and accountability.

The central idea is that tribal development should not be reduced to the delivery of government schemes. It must also protect the capacity of tribal communities to participate in and shape decisions affecting their lives, resources and institutions.
