1. Constitutional Basis and Scope

Legislative relations determine how law-making authority is distributed between Parliament and State legislatures. The Constitution establishes this distribution primarily through Part XI, particularly Articles 245–255, read with the Seventh Schedule.

The scheme seeks to balance national uniformity with regional autonomy. It identifies subjects on which Parliament and State legislatures may legislate, provides mechanisms for resolving conflicts, and permits Parliament to legislate on certain State subjects in constitutionally specified circumstances.

  • Article 245: Extent of laws made by Parliament and State legislatures.
  • Article 246: Subject-matter of laws made by Parliament and State legislatures.
  • Article 246A: Special provision concerning GST legislation.
  • Article 247: Power of Parliament to establish additional courts for better administration of laws made by Parliament or existing laws concerning matters in the Union List.
  • Article 248: Residuary legislative powers of Parliament.
  • Articles 249–253: Circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on matters in the State List.
  • Articles 254–255: Repugnancy between Union and State laws and requirements concerning recommendations and previous sanctions.

2. Territorial Extent of Legislative Laws — Article 245

Article 245 defines the territorial reach of legislative authority.

Parliament

Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India. A Parliamentary law is not invalid merely because it has extra-territorial operation.

State legislatures

A State legislature may make laws for the whole or any part of that State. A State law ordinarily operates within the territorial jurisdiction of the State, subject to the constitutional principles governing territorial connection.

Doctrine of territorial nexus

A State law may affect persons, property or transactions outside the State where there is a real and sufficient territorial connection between the State and the subject of the law. The connection must be genuine and not illusory.

Case reference: State of Bombay v. R.M.D. Chamarbaugwala (1957) is an important authority concerning territorial nexus.

3. Distribution of Legislative Subjects — Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule

The Seventh Schedule contains three legislative lists. Article 246 establishes the general distribution of legislative competence among Parliament and State legislatures.

3.1 Union List — List I

Parliament has exclusive legislative competence over subjects in the Union List, subject to the Constitution.

These subjects generally require national uniformity, central coordination or expertise at the national level.

Illustrative subjects:

  • Defence of India and armed forces.
  • Foreign affairs and diplomatic relations.
  • Atomic energy.
  • Railways.
  • Currency, coinage and legal tender.
  • Banking and insurance.
  • Citizenship, naturalisation and aliens.
  • Inter-State trade and commerce.
  • Posts, telegraphs and specified communication matters.

3.2 State List — List II

State legislatures ordinarily have exclusive competence over subjects in the State List, subject to constitutional exceptions that permit Parliamentary legislation.

Illustrative subjects:

  • Public order.
  • Police.
  • Public health and sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries.
  • Agriculture.
  • Land and land rights, subject to constitutional limitations.
  • Markets and fairs.
  • Betting and gambling.
  • Local government.
  • State public services and State public service commissions.

3.3 Concurrent List — List III

Both Parliament and State legislatures may legislate on subjects in the Concurrent List. The list enables national policy frameworks while allowing State-level adaptation.

Illustrative subjects:

  • Criminal law and criminal procedure, subject to the entries and constitutional provisions.
  • Marriage and divorce.
  • Contracts.
  • Bankruptcy and insolvency.
  • Education.
  • Forests.
  • Protection of wild animals and birds.
  • Trade unions and industrial disputes.
  • Economic and social planning.
  • Electricity.

4. Residuary Legislative Powers — Article 248

Residuary subjects are matters not enumerated in the State List or Concurrent List. Under Article 248, Parliament has exclusive power to legislate on such matters, including the power to impose a tax not mentioned in either list, subject to the Constitution.

Entry 97 of the Union List also covers matters not enumerated in List II or List III, including taxes not mentioned in those lists.

This allocation strengthens the Union's legislative position and distinguishes India's model from federations where residuary powers are assigned to constituent units.

Illustration: Parliament has legislated on emerging areas such as aspects of digital and technological regulation through its constitutionally available legislative powers. Whether a particular subject is residuary must be determined by interpreting the relevant entries and constitutional provisions, not merely by its novelty.

5. GST and Special Legislative Arrangement — Article 246A

The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 introduced a special framework for Goods and Services Tax.

  • Parliament and State legislatures have power to make laws with respect to GST imposed by the Union or by the State, subject to the constitutional provisions.
  • Parliament has exclusive power to legislate on GST where the supply of goods or services, or both, takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Article 269A provides for the levy and collection of GST on inter-State supplies and its apportionment between the Union and States.
  • Article 279A establishes the GST Council to make recommendations on specified GST matters.

Article 246A is a special provision and should not be treated simply as an ordinary Concurrent List entry.

Federal significance: GST requires sustained coordination between the Union and States. It illustrates both shared legislative authority and the institutional importance of cooperative fiscal federalism.

6. Parliament's Power to Legislate on State List Matters

Although State legislatures ordinarily legislate on State List subjects, the Constitution recognises certain situations in which Parliament may legislate on those matters.

6.1 National interest — Article 249

  • Rajya Sabha must pass a resolution declaring it necessary or expedient in the national interest for Parliament to legislate on a specified State List matter.
  • The resolution must be supported by not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.
  • The resolution remains in force for a period not exceeding one year, but may be renewed for further periods of one year at a time.
  • A Parliamentary law made under this provision ceases to have effect, to the extent of the Parliament's lack of competence, six months after the resolution ceases to operate, except as regards things done or omitted to be done before that cessation.

Federal significance: Rajya Sabha's special role connects the provision with representation of States in Parliament.

6.2 National Emergency — Article 250

  • While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, Parliament may legislate on matters in the State List.
  • Such a Parliamentary law ceases to have effect, to the extent of Parliament's lack of competence, six months after the Emergency ceases to operate, except for past acts and omissions.

This provision allows temporary legislative centralisation during an exceptional national situation.

6.3 Consent of States — Article 252

  • Two or more State legislatures may pass resolutions requesting Parliament to legislate on a matter in the State List.
  • Parliament may then enact a law for the participating States and for other States that subsequently adopt it through a resolution.
  • Such a law may be amended or repealed by Parliament, but not by the legislatures of the participating States themselves.

Significance: Article 252 is an example of States voluntarily enabling Parliament to legislate in a field otherwise within their competence.

6.4 International agreements — Article 253

Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of India to implement treaties, agreements or conventions with other countries, or decisions made at international conferences, associations or other bodies.

This power is not confined by the ordinary division of subjects in the Seventh Schedule. It enables India to fulfil international obligations through domestic legislation.

6.5 Failure of constitutional machinery — Article 356

When a Proclamation under Article 356 is in operation in a State, Parliament may exercise the legislative powers of the State legislature. The Constitution also permits Parliament to confer the State legislature's legislative power on the President, subject to the provisions of Article 357.

Such a situation is exceptional and must be understood alongside judicial safeguards against misuse of President's Rule.

7. Conflict Between Union and State Laws — Article 254

Repugnancy arises when a valid Union law and a valid State law on a Concurrent List matter are inconsistent with each other in a manner that cannot be reconciled.

7.1 General rule — Article 254(1)

If a provision of a State law is repugnant to a provision of a law made by Parliament, or an existing law, on a matter in the Concurrent List, the Parliamentary law generally prevails. The State law is void to the extent of the repugnancy.

7.2 Exception — Article 254(2)

Where a State law on a Concurrent List matter is reserved for the consideration of the President and receives presidential assent, it may prevail within that State over an earlier Parliamentary law or existing law with which it conflicts.

However, Parliament retains the power to enact a subsequent law adding to, amending, varying or repealing the State law.

7.3 Conditions and limits

  • The State law must concern a Concurrent List matter.
  • It must be reserved for the President's consideration and receive assent.
  • The overriding effect is ordinarily limited to the State concerned.
  • Parliament may subsequently override the State law.
  • Presidential assent does not cure a State law that is otherwise beyond the State legislature's legislative competence.

8. How Courts Determine Legislative Competence

Legislative entries are not interpreted as isolated compartments. Courts apply established principles to identify the true nature and constitutional competence of legislation.

8.1 Doctrine of pith and substance

The doctrine examines the true nature and character of a law, including its purpose and legal effect. If the law substantially falls within the legislative competence of the legislature that enacted it, incidental encroachment into another list does not automatically invalidate it.

Case reference: State of Bombay v. F.N. Balsara (1951) is a significant illustration of the doctrine.

8.2 Doctrine of colourable legislation

The doctrine asks whether a legislature has indirectly attempted to do something that it cannot directly do under the Constitution. The focus is on legislative competence rather than the political motive of the legislature.

The principle is often expressed as: what cannot be done directly cannot be done indirectly.

8.3 Harmonious construction

Where entries in different lists appear to overlap, courts attempt to interpret them harmoniously so that each retains meaningful operation. The Constitution should not be read as creating unnecessary conflict between legislative fields.

8.4 Incidental encroachment

A law validly enacted within one legislative field may have incidental effects on a subject assigned to another field. Such incidental overlap does not necessarily invalidate the law.

8.5 Occupied field and repugnancy

In appropriate cases, courts examine whether Parliament has intended to occupy a legislative field and whether a State law is inconsistent with the Parliamentary enactment. The inquiry depends on the constitutional list involved, the statutory provisions and the nature of the conflict.

8.6 Reading down and severability

Where legally permissible, courts may interpret a provision narrowly to preserve its constitutionality or sever invalid provisions from the remainder of a law. These are general constitutional interpretation techniques, not substitutes for legislative competence.

10. Legislative Relations and Cooperative Federalism

The distribution of legislative powers does not require the Union and States to function independently in every policy area. Many contemporary subjects require coordinated legislation and administration.

  • GST Council: Provides a constitutional forum for the Union and States to discuss and recommend GST-related measures.
  • Inter-State Council: Provides a forum for coordination and discussion on common interests and disputes.
  • Article 252: Allows States to request common Parliamentary legislation on State List matters.
  • Concurrent List: Allows both levels to legislate in areas requiring national standards and regional adaptation.
  • Constitutional consultation: Legislative proposals with significant federal implications benefit from meaningful consultation with affected States.

Cooperation is most effective when consultation is timely, information is shared, responsibilities are clear and the fiscal implications of legislation are considered.

11. Contemporary Issues and Reform Priorities

11.1 Expanding central legislation in shared policy fields

When Parliament legislates extensively in Concurrent List areas, concerns may arise about State participation and the practical space available for regional policy innovation.

Reform direction: Strengthen pre-legislative consultation, publish impact assessments and provide meaningful opportunities for State input.

11.2 Legislative overlap and uncertainty

Modern policy fields such as digital platforms, environmental regulation, public health and emerging technologies may involve several constitutional entries.

Reform direction: Use clear legislative drafting, constitutional competence analysis and intergovernmental consultation before enactment.

11.3 Concurrent List coordination

Differences between national legislation and State laws can create uncertainty for citizens, businesses and public institutions.

Reform direction: Encourage structured coordination and clear conflict-resolution provisions while respecting the constitutional allocation of powers.

11.4 GST and fiscal autonomy

The GST framework has created a shared institutional structure, but it has also changed the nature of State-level indirect taxation powers.

Reform direction: Strengthen evidence-based deliberation in the GST Council, improve transparency and ensure predictable settlement and compensation-related arrangements as applicable under law.

11.5 International commitments and domestic federalism

Article 253 enables Parliament to implement international obligations, including in areas that may otherwise fall within State legislative fields. This can create coordination challenges where States are responsible for implementation.

Reform direction: Consult affected States during treaty implementation where their legislative or administrative responsibilities are substantially involved.

13. Key Distinctions

  • Article 249 vs Article 250: Article 249 requires a Rajya Sabha resolution in national interest; Article 250 operates during a Proclamation of Emergency.
  • Article 252 vs Article 253: Article 252 is based on State resolutions requesting Parliamentary legislation; Article 253 concerns implementation of international obligations and related decisions.
  • Article 246 vs Article 246A: Article 246 distributes ordinary legislative subjects through the three lists; Article 246A creates a special GST legislative framework.
  • Article 248 vs Article 253: Article 248 concerns residuary legislative competence; Article 253 is a specific power to implement international commitments.
  • Article 254(1) vs Article 254(2): The first establishes the general Parliamentary supremacy rule in cases of repugnancy; the second provides a conditional State-law exception following presidential assent, subject to Parliament's later power.

14. Integrated Understanding

Legislative relations are not merely a division of subjects. They are a constitutional mechanism for managing diversity, national priorities and shared responsibilities.

A sound understanding requires connecting:

  1. The three legislative lists and residuary powers.
  2. Exceptional Parliamentary powers over State List matters.
  3. GST and other shared legislative arrangements.
  4. Repugnancy and judicial tests of legislative competence.
  5. Cooperative federalism and institutional consultation.

The central constitutional objective is to preserve the authority of each level of government while ensuring that national coordination and effective governance remain possible.
