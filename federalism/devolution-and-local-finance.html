1. Meaning and Concept of Devolution

Devolution is the transfer of political, administrative and financial authority from higher levels of government to elected institutions at a lower level. In India, it principally concerns Panchayats and Municipalities as institutions of local self-government.

Devolution is more substantive than administrative delegation. It seeks to give local institutions the authority, resources and personnel required to identify local priorities, make decisions, implement programmes and remain accountable to their communities.

Three dimensions of devolution

  • Political devolution: Elected local representatives, regular elections, participation and accountability.
  • Administrative devolution: Transfer of functions, staff, technical capacity and operational authority.
  • Fiscal devolution: Transfer of revenue powers, predictable funds, grants and expenditure autonomy.

These dimensions are interdependent. Assigning responsibilities without staff or finance produces unfunded mandates. Providing grants without decision-making authority can reduce local institutions to implementing agencies.

2. Decentralisation: Forms and Distinctions

Decentralisation is a broad concept involving the redistribution of governmental authority. Its principal forms are:

  • Deconcentration: Redistribution of administrative responsibilities within the same government, usually to its field offices. Authority remains within the departmental hierarchy.
  • Delegation: Transfer of specified tasks or decision-making powers to semi-autonomous agencies or subordinate bodies, subject to conditions imposed by the delegating authority.
  • Devolution: Transfer of meaningful authority to elected subnational institutions with a degree of independent decision-making and accountability.
  • Privatisation: Transfer of selected activities or service delivery responsibilities to private entities. It does not itself constitute democratic decentralisation.
Form Recipient Nature of authority
Deconcentration Field offices of the same government Internal administrative redistribution
Delegation Agency or subordinate body Specified and conditional authority
Devolution Elected local government Political, administrative and fiscal authority
Privatisation Private entity Contractual or market-based service delivery

3. Constitutional Basis of Local Devolution

73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The 73rd Amendment inserted Part IX of the Constitution, comprising Articles 243 to 243O, and the Eleventh Schedule. It gave constitutional recognition to Panchayati Raj Institutions. It came into force on 24 April 1993.

74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The 74th Amendment inserted Part IXA, comprising Articles 243P to 243ZG, and the Twelfth Schedule. It gave constitutional recognition to Municipalities. It came into force on 1 June 1993.

5. The 3Fs: Functions, Funds and Functionaries

The 3Fs framework is a useful way to assess whether constitutional recognition has translated into effective local self-government.

Functions

Local institutions must receive clearly defined responsibilities for planning, implementation and monitoring. Functions should be allocated according to the principle of subsidiarity: decisions should be taken at the lowest competent level.

Funds

Local bodies require adequate, predictable and flexible financial resources to discharge their responsibilities. These resources may come from own-source revenues, assigned taxes, shared revenues, grants and permissible borrowing.

Functionaries

Local bodies need appropriately trained personnel, technical staff and administrative authority. Staff who remain accountable only to State departments can weaken the ability of elected local institutions to implement their decisions.

Element Core question Typical weakness
Functions What can the local body decide and deliver? Unclear or incomplete assignment of subjects
Funds What resources can it mobilise and spend? Inadequate revenue autonomy and delayed transfers
Functionaries Does it control staff and technical capacity? Staff shortages and departmental control

Devolution is incomplete when any one of the 3Fs is missing. Effective local governance requires their simultaneous and coordinated transfer.

6. Local Government Finance: Meaning and Principles

Local government finance concerns the mobilisation, allocation, transfer, expenditure and accountability of financial resources available to Panchayats and Municipalities.

Principles of sound local finance

  • Adequacy: Resources should be sufficient to meet assigned responsibilities.
  • Predictability: Transfers should follow transparent and stable rules and schedules.
  • Autonomy: Local institutions should have meaningful discretion over appropriate revenue and expenditure decisions.
  • Equity: Fiscal arrangements should account for differences in local needs and revenue capacity.
  • Efficiency: Revenue collection and public expenditure should minimise waste and improve service outcomes.
  • Transparency: Budgets, accounts, contracts and audit findings should be accessible to citizens.
  • Accountability: Local governments should explain how public resources are raised and spent.

7. Sources of Revenue of Local Governments

A. Own-source revenue

Own-source revenue is collected directly by the local body under powers conferred by State law. It is important because it can strengthen financial autonomy and accountability to residents.

Panchayat revenue

  • Taxes, duties, tolls and fees authorised by State law.
  • Fees for licences and local services.
  • Charges relating to markets, fairs and community facilities.
  • Income from local assets and permissible rents.
  • Other receipts authorised by State legislation.

Municipal revenue

  • Property tax.
  • Fees and licence charges.
  • User charges for water, sanitation and other services.
  • Building permission and development-related charges where authorised.
  • Advertisement fees and other permissible municipal receipts.
  • Income from municipal property and assets.

B. Assigned revenues

A State may assign a share of specified taxes or revenues to local bodies. Such arrangements can provide a regular revenue stream without requiring the local body to independently administer the tax.

C. Shared taxes

State legislation or recommendations of the State Finance Commission may provide for sharing specified State revenues with Panchayats and Municipalities.

D. Grants-in-aid

Grants are transfers from higher levels of government to support local expenditure. They may be unconditional, tied to particular purposes or linked to specified performance and reporting requirements.

E. Borrowing and capital finance

Subject to applicable State laws and financial regulations, eligible local bodies may raise funds through loans, bonds or other permissible financing arrangements. Borrowing is generally more suitable for capital investment than routine operating expenditure.

8. Article 243H and Article 243X: Taxation Powers

Article 243H: Panchayats

A State Legislature may authorise Panchayats to:

  • Levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees in accordance with law.
  • Receive taxes, duties, tolls and fees levied and collected by the State Government and assign them to Panchayats.
  • Receive grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Establish funds for crediting money received by or on behalf of Panchayats and for its withdrawal and use.

Article 243X: Municipalities

It provides a corresponding framework for State Legislatures to authorise Municipalities to levy and collect taxes, receive assigned revenues and grants, and establish municipal funds.

Neither Article creates an identical, directly exercisable tax list for every local body. The relevant authority depends on State legislation.

9. Property Tax and Municipal Revenue Reform

Property tax is a major potential source of own-source revenue for urban local bodies. It is linked to immovable property and can provide a relatively stable local revenue base.

Common weaknesses

  • Incomplete coverage of taxable properties.
  • Outdated property registers and valuation methods.
  • Weak enforcement and collection systems.
  • Exemptions and concessions that erode the tax base.
  • Inadequate public communication about tax assessment.
  • Limited use of reliable geographic and property data.

Reform measures

  • Undertake periodic property surveys and update municipal registers.
  • Use transparent and legally authorised valuation systems.
  • Link property records with geographic information systems where feasible.
  • Improve billing, online payment and grievance mechanisms.
  • Strengthen collection while providing safeguards for vulnerable households.
  • Publish collection performance and use of property tax revenue.

Revenue reform should be accompanied by better services. Citizens are more likely to comply when assessment is fair, collection is transparent and public services are reliable.

10. State Finance Commission

Constitutional basis: Articles 243I and 243Y

Article 243I requires the Governor to constitute a State Finance Commission within one year from the commencement of the 73rd Amendment and thereafter at the expiration of every fifth year.

Article 243Y provides that the State Finance Commission constituted under Article 243I also reviews the financial position of Municipalities and makes recommendations on the matters specified in that Article.

Composition

The State Legislature determines the composition, qualifications of members and manner of selection through State law.

Functions and recommendations

  • Principles governing the distribution between the State and local bodies of the net proceeds of specified taxes, duties, tolls and fees.
  • Determination of taxes, duties, tolls and fees that may be assigned to or appropriated by local bodies.
  • Grants-in-aid to local bodies from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Measures needed to improve the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Other matters referred by the Governor in the interests of sound local finance.

Procedure and reporting

The Governor causes the recommendations of the Commission, together with an explanatory memorandum on the action taken, to be laid before the State Legislature.

Importance

  • Creates a regular constitutional mechanism for reviewing local finances.
  • Links the revenue capacity of local bodies with their expenditure responsibilities.
  • Can reduce disparities among local governments.
  • Provides a basis for predictable fiscal transfers.
  • Encourages evidence-based local fiscal reform.

Common weaknesses

  • Delayed constitution of Commissions.
  • Delays in accepting or implementing recommendations.
  • Insufficient quality and comparability of local financial data.
  • Weak follow-up and monitoring of recommendations.
  • Limited coordination with State budget processes.

11. Union Finance Commission and Local Bodies

Article 280 establishes the Union Finance Commission. The constitutional role of the Commission in relation to local bodies is specified in Article 280(3)(bb) and Article 280(3)(c).

  • Article 280(3)(bb): Measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats in the State, based on the recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  • Article 280(3)(c): Measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Municipalities in the State, based on the recommendations of the State Finance Commission.

Relationship between the two Finance Commissions

The State Finance Commission reviews the financial position of local bodies and recommends arrangements within the State. The Union Finance Commission recommends measures to augment State resources for supplementing local body resources.

These constitutional mechanisms are distinct but connected. Their effectiveness depends on timely State Finance Commissions, reliable local data and implementation of recommendations.

12. Grants to Local Governments

Untied grants

Untied grants provide local bodies with discretion to allocate funds according to locally determined priorities, subject to applicable law and financial accountability.

Tied grants

Tied grants must be used for specified purposes. They can help ensure spending on essential services but may limit the ability of local bodies to respond to other local priorities.

Performance-linked grants

These grants are linked to specified conditions such as accounting, reporting, service delivery or institutional reforms. Their design should avoid excluding weaker local bodies that lack the capacity to meet complex conditions.

Principles for grant design

  • Use transparent and objective allocation criteria.
  • Account for population, area, service gaps and fiscal capacity.
  • Balance basic service guarantees with local discretion.
  • Ensure timely release and predictable grant cycles.
  • Keep compliance and reporting requirements proportionate to local capacity.
  • Provide technical support to weaker local bodies.

13. Local Expenditure and Budgeting

Local budgets translate development priorities into expenditure decisions. They should reflect the responsibilities assigned to the local body and available financial resources.

Revenue expenditure

Revenue expenditure generally covers routine operations, salaries, maintenance, utilities and service delivery.

Capital expenditure

Capital expenditure creates or improves long-term assets such as roads, water systems, drainage networks, public buildings and other infrastructure.

Participatory budgeting

Participatory budgeting involves citizens in identifying priorities and influencing the allocation of public resources. It can improve responsiveness and public understanding of budget trade-offs.

Gender-responsive and inclusive budgeting

Local budgets can assess how expenditure affects women, children, older persons, persons with disabilities, Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other vulnerable groups.

Good budgeting practices

  • Prepare realistic revenue estimates.
  • Link expenditure to assigned functions and measurable outcomes.
  • Provide for operation and maintenance of existing assets.
  • Disclose budget proposals and approved budgets.
  • Monitor expenditure and physical progress together.
  • Publish audited accounts and action-taken reports.

14. Accounting, Audit and Financial Accountability

Constitutional provisions

  • Article 243J enables State Legislatures to provide for maintenance and audit of Panchayat accounts.
  • Article 243Z enables State Legislatures to provide for maintenance and audit of municipal accounts.

Why local audits matter

  • They verify whether public funds were spent lawfully.
  • They identify financial irregularities and control weaknesses.
  • They improve the reliability of financial statements.
  • They help assess whether expenditure achieved intended results.
  • They strengthen legislative and citizen oversight.

Social audit

Social audit is a process through which citizens examine public expenditure, records and implementation against actual experience on the ground. It complements formal financial audit by bringing community scrutiny into public programme accountability.

Public disclosure

Local bodies should make budgets, accounts, grants, procurement information, service standards and audit findings accessible. Disclosure should be understandable to ordinary residents, not merely technically compliant.

15. Panchayat Finance: Specific Issues

Revenue challenges

  • Limited taxation powers and narrow local tax bases.
  • Low collection capacity in smaller Gram Panchayats.
  • Dependence on State transfers and scheme-based grants.
  • Inadequate assessment of local assets and revenue potential.
  • Shortage of trained accounting and finance personnel.

Gram Sabha and fiscal accountability

The Gram Sabha can provide a forum for local residents to deliberate on development priorities, scrutinise implementation and hold Panchayat representatives accountable, subject to the constitutional and State legal framework.

Planning and budgeting

Gram Panchayat Development Plans provide a framework for identifying local needs and integrating available resources with development priorities. Effective planning requires participation, reliable data, technical support and coordination with higher Panchayat levels.

Special considerations

  • Remote and sparsely populated areas may face high service delivery costs.
  • Tribal areas require attention to constitutional protections and customary institutions.
  • Local bodies need technical support for infrastructure planning and maintenance.
  • Fiscal transfers should account for differences in local revenue capacity.

16. Municipal Finance: Specific Issues

Expenditure responsibilities

Urban local bodies face substantial costs for roads, drainage, water supply, sanitation, solid waste management, street lighting, public health and local amenities.

Revenue and borrowing constraints

  • Property tax collection may be below potential.
  • User charges may not cover service costs.
  • Transfers may be delayed or tied to specific schemes.
  • Smaller Municipalities may lack access to capital markets.
  • Weak accounts and creditworthiness can restrict borrowing.

Municipal bonds

Municipal bonds are debt instruments through which eligible urban local bodies can mobilise market resources for permissible purposes. Their success depends on sound financial management and credible repayment capacity.

Conditions for sustainable municipal borrowing

  • Reliable audited financial statements.
  • Predictable revenue streams.
  • Sound project appraisal and cash-flow estimates.
  • Transparent debt and liability disclosure.
  • Strong procurement and contract management.
  • Clear safeguards against unsustainable borrowing.

17. Fiscal Federalism and Local Government

Fiscal federalism concerns the allocation of taxation powers, expenditure responsibilities and intergovernmental transfers among different levels of government.

Vertical fiscal imbalance

Vertical fiscal imbalance arises when the expenditure responsibilities of a level of government are not matched by its own revenue-raising capacity. Local governments often depend on transfers because many major revenue sources are controlled by higher levels of government.

Horizontal fiscal imbalance

Horizontal fiscal imbalance refers to differences in revenue capacity and expenditure needs among governments at the same level. Differences in population, geography, economic activity and service delivery costs can create substantial disparities among local bodies.

Equalisation transfers

Equalisation transfers seek to reduce disparities by directing greater support towards local bodies with weaker revenue capacity or greater expenditure needs.

Subsidiarity

The principle of subsidiarity suggests that public functions should be performed by the lowest level of government capable of performing them effectively. Higher levels should support, coordinate and address issues that extend beyond local boundaries.

18. Local Government and Centrally Sponsored Schemes

Centrally Sponsored Schemes and other government programmes frequently involve local bodies in planning, beneficiary identification, implementation and monitoring.

Potential benefits

  • Additional resources for local development.
  • National minimum standards in important services.
  • Technical support and common implementation frameworks.
  • Opportunities to improve coverage of public programmes.

Potential problems

  • Excessive dependence on scheme-specific funds.
  • Limited discretion to respond to local priorities.
  • Parallel implementation structures.
  • Fragmented reporting and compliance requirements.
  • Weak integration with local development plans.

Schemes should strengthen the institutional capacity of local bodies and align their activities with local plans rather than bypassing elected institutions.

19. PESA and Devolution in Scheduled Areas

The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996, commonly called PESA, extends the Panchayat framework to Fifth Schedule areas with modifications and special safeguards.

Important features

  • Recognition of the Gram Sabha's role in safeguarding traditions, customs and community resources.
  • Protection of customary modes of dispute resolution, subject to the constitutional and legal framework.
  • Consultation with the Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat before land acquisition and resettlement in the circumstances specified by law.
  • Recommendations of the Gram Sabha or appropriate Panchayat before granting prospecting licences or mining leases for minor minerals, as provided by PESA.
  • Ownership of minor forest produce, subject to the statutory framework.
  • Prevention of alienation of tribal land and restoration of unlawfully alienated land in accordance with law.
  • Control over village markets and specified local resources.
  • Protection of community resources and local development priorities.

Importance for fiscal and administrative devolution

PESA highlights that decentralisation must respect local institutions, customary practices and community control over resources. Devolution in Scheduled Areas cannot be assessed only through the transfer of ordinary administrative responsibilities.

Implementation challenges

  • Incomplete alignment of State laws with PESA.
  • Limited awareness and capacity of Gram Sabhas.
  • Conflicts between statutory agencies and community institutions.
  • Weak recognition of community resource rights.
  • Inadequate institutional and financial support.

20. Local Finance, Transparency and Technology

Digital accounting and financial management

Digital systems can improve the recording of receipts and expenditure, standardise accounts, support timely reporting and facilitate comparison across local bodies.

Geographic information systems

Geographic information systems can help local governments map assets, property records, service coverage and infrastructure gaps.

Public dashboards

Public dashboards can disclose budgets, grant releases, project progress, service performance and grievance resolution.

Risks and safeguards

  • Digital tools should not exclude residents with limited connectivity or digital literacy.
  • Data quality and regular updating are essential.
  • Personal information must be protected.
  • Technology should support, not replace, elected decision-making.
  • Local staff need training and ongoing technical support.

21. Challenges in Effective Devolution

  1. Incomplete transfer of functions: State departments may retain control over important subjects listed in the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules.
  2. Weak fiscal autonomy: Local bodies may have limited authority to levy taxes or determine revenue rates.
  3. Dependence on grants: Scheme-based transfers can restrict flexibility and long-term planning.
  4. Shortage of functionaries: Technical and administrative staff may be insufficient or controlled by State departments.
  5. Weak State Finance Commissions: Delayed constitution and limited implementation reduce their effectiveness.
  6. Unclear accountability: Multiple agencies and overlapping functions make it difficult to identify responsibility.
  7. Unequal local capacity: Smaller and poorer local bodies often lack the personnel and systems required for effective planning.
  8. Inadequate financial information: Inconsistent accounts and weak asset registers make resource planning difficult.
  9. Limited participation: Gram Sabhas, Ward Committees and other participatory forums may not exercise meaningful oversight.
  10. Political and administrative resistance: Higher levels of government may be reluctant to transfer control over resources, personnel and decisions.

22. Way Forward: Deepening Fiscal and Democratic Devolution

  1. Prepare clear activity maps: Specify which level of local government is responsible for each function and the supporting departments.
  2. Transfer the 3Fs together: Assign functions, funds and personnel in a coordinated and measurable manner.
  3. Strengthen State Finance Commissions: Constitute them on time, provide adequate technical support and publish action-taken reports.
  4. Improve own-source revenue: Modernise local tax administration, maintain asset registers and improve collection systems.
  5. Make transfers predictable: Adopt transparent formulas and ensure timely release of funds.
  6. Improve equalisation: Consider local fiscal capacity, population, geography, poverty and service delivery costs in allocation.
  7. Build local capacity: Provide trained accountants, engineers, planners and other technical personnel.
  8. Strengthen participatory planning: Integrate Gram Sabha, Ward Committee and citizen priorities into local budgets and development plans.
  9. Improve audit and disclosure: Publish accounts, expenditure information, audit findings and action-taken reports.
  10. Align schemes with local plans: Use government programmes to strengthen local institutions and avoid parallel structures.
  11. Support weaker local bodies: Provide differentiated technical and fiscal assistance based on local capacity.
  12. Measure outcomes: Evaluate devolution through service quality, accessibility, responsiveness and citizen satisfaction, not merely expenditure.

24. Integrated Revision Framework

Assess local devolution through the following connected questions:

  1. Constitutional authority: What do Parts IX and IXA provide?
  2. Functional assignment: Have subjects from the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules been effectively transferred?
  3. Fiscal capacity: Can local bodies raise and receive adequate funds?
  4. Administrative capacity: Do local bodies control the personnel and expertise needed to discharge their responsibilities?
  5. Intergovernmental transfers: Are State and Union transfers predictable, equitable and transparent?
  6. Accountability: Can residents scrutinise budgets, expenditure and service outcomes?

The central principle is that local self-government requires more than elected representatives. It requires a workable alignment between responsibilities, resources, personnel and public accountability.
