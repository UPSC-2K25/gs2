1. Introduction: Institutional Cooperation in Indian Federalism

Indian federalism requires continuous coordination between the Union and the States. Constitutional distribution of legislative, administrative and financial powers does not by itself resolve every intergovernmental disagreement. Differences may arise over public policy, development priorities, natural resources, internal security, infrastructure, migration and the implementation of national programmes.

The Constitution and parliamentary legislation therefore provide institutional mechanisms through which governments can deliberate, coordinate and seek solutions. The Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils and North Eastern Council are important forums for this purpose.

These institutions primarily promote consultation and cooperation. They are not substitutes for courts, constitutional adjudication or the legislative process.

2. Inter-State Council: Constitutional Basis

Article 263

Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by doing so. The President may define the nature of the Council's duties and its organisation and procedure.

Article 263 identifies three broad functions that may be assigned to such a Council:

  1. Inquiring into and advising upon disputes that may have arisen between States.
  2. Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all States, or the Union and one or more States, have a common interest.
  3. Making recommendations for better coordination of policy and action concerning such subjects.

The President may also define the nature of other duties assigned to the Council. Article 263 is therefore an enabling constitutional provision; it does not itself create a permanently functioning Council with a fixed membership.

Background and establishment

  • The Constitution made provision for an Inter-State Council to strengthen coordination among constitutional units.
  • The Administrative Reforms Commission and the Sarkaria Commission examined the need for stronger intergovernmental consultation.
  • The Sarkaria Commission recommended that the Council should be established as a permanent institution under Article 263.
  • The Inter-State Council was established through a Presidential Order dated 28 May 1990.

The Council was created under Article 263 rather than by an Act of Parliament. Its establishment is therefore constitutional in basis, while its specific organisation and functioning are governed by the Presidential Order and subsequent amendments.

3. Composition and Organisation of the Inter-State Council

The Inter-State Council is chaired by the Prime Minister. Its composition is designed to bring the Union and State political leadership into a common forum.

Principal composition

  • Prime Minister — Chairperson.
  • Chief Ministers of all States.
  • Chief Ministers of Union Territories having Legislative Assemblies.
  • Administrators of Union Territories without Legislative Assemblies.
  • Governors of States under President's Rule, as provided by the applicable Presidential Order.
  • Six Union Cabinet Ministers nominated by the Chairperson, including the Union Home Minister.

The applicable Presidential Order also provides for participation or invitation in specified circumstances. The precise membership and participation arrangements should therefore be read with the Order and its amendments rather than treated as an unchanging constitutional list.

Standing Committee

A Standing Committee was constituted in 1996 to support the work of the Council and enable more continuous examination of issues.

  • It is chaired by the Union Home Minister.
  • It includes Union Ministers and Chief Ministers representing States.
  • It examines matters referred to it and helps prepare issues for consideration by the full Council.
  • It supports follow-up and coordination on matters requiring intergovernmental attention.

The Standing Committee is an institutional support mechanism. It does not replace the full Council or independently exercise the Council's constitutional role.

4. Functions and Significance of the Inter-State Council

Major functions

  • Discussing subjects of common interest between the Union and States.
  • Facilitating coordination in policy formulation and implementation.
  • Providing a forum for States to communicate concerns directly to the Union.
  • Promoting discussion on inter-State issues and possible approaches to their resolution.
  • Examining recommendations of commissions and committees dealing with Centre-State relations.
  • Encouraging cooperation in national development, administration and public service delivery.

Importance for Indian federalism

The Council institutionalises consultation in a federal system where the Union and States have constitutionally distinct responsibilities but frequently share policy objectives.

  • It strengthens cooperative federalism by creating a regular institutional space for dialogue.
  • It gives States an opportunity to raise concerns regarding national policies and their implementation.
  • It can reduce administrative friction by encouraging discussion before disagreements become entrenched.
  • It helps align national priorities with regional circumstances and developmental needs.
  • It can improve trust and communication between different levels of government.

5. Nature and Limitations of the Inter-State Council

  • The Council is primarily a deliberative and recommendatory institution.
  • Its recommendations do not automatically operate as binding judicial orders or legislation.
  • It cannot amend the Constitution or override the legislative powers of Parliament and State Legislatures.
  • It does not exercise the judicial jurisdiction of the Supreme Court under Article 131.
  • It is not a substitute for specialised statutory mechanisms dealing with matters such as inter-State river-water disputes.

Its effectiveness depends substantially on the quality of deliberation, frequency of meetings, political willingness, administrative follow-up and implementation of agreed recommendations.

6. Zonal Councils: Origin and Legal Basis

Zonal Councils are regional advisory bodies established under Part III of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. They were conceived as forums to encourage cooperation among neighbouring States and improve coordination between the Union and States.

The idea was associated with the reorganisation of States on linguistic lines. Regional forums were considered useful for reducing tensions, promoting mutual understanding and addressing common administrative and developmental concerns.

Legal provisions

  • Sections 15–22 of the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 provide the statutory framework for Zonal Councils.
  • Section 15 provides for the establishment of five Zonal Councils.
  • Section 16 deals with their composition.
  • Section 17 deals with meetings and procedure.
  • Sections 18–22 deal with associated committees, staff, offices, expenses and related matters.

Zonal Councils are statutory bodies, unlike the Inter-State Council, which is established under Article 263 through a Presidential Order.

7. Five Zonal Councils and Their Jurisdiction

The five Zonal Councils are Northern, Central, Eastern, Western and Southern. Their membership reflects subsequent changes in State and Union Territory boundaries and reorganisation.

Northern Zonal Council

Its present regional coverage includes Haryana, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Punjab and Rajasthan, along with the National Capital Territory of Delhi and the Union Territory of Chandigarh.

Central Zonal Council

It comprises Chhattisgarh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttarakhand and Uttar Pradesh.

Eastern Zonal Council

Its present membership includes Bihar, Jharkhand, Odisha and West Bengal. Sikkim is part of the North Eastern Council framework.

Western Zonal Council

It comprises Goa, Gujarat and Maharashtra, together with the Union Territories of Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu.

Southern Zonal Council

It comprises Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, together with the Union Territory of Puducherry.

Membership and territorial arrangements should be understood in light of subsequent reorganisation legislation and official notifications. The historical composition in the original 1956 Act is not the same as the present geographical composition.

8. Composition and Leadership of Zonal Councils

Chairperson

The Union Home Minister is the Chairperson of each of the five Zonal Councils.

Vice-Chairperson

The Chief Ministers of the States included in the respective zone act as Vice-Chairperson by rotation, with each holding the position for one year at a time.

Members

  • Chief Minister of each member State.
  • Two other Ministers from each member State, nominated by the Governor.
  • Representatives from the Union Territories included in the zone, in accordance with the Act and applicable arrangements.

Advisers and administrative support

Chief Secretaries and other senior administrative officers support the work of the Councils. Each Zonal Council also has a Standing Committee at the level of Chief Secretaries.

These Standing Committees examine issues, undertake preparatory work and help resolve administrative matters before they are placed before the full Council.

9. Functions of Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils are intended to promote cooperation among States in the same region and between regional governments and the Union.

  • Discussing matters of common interest in economic and social planning.
  • Addressing inter-State boundary and regional coordination issues.
  • Discussing linguistic minority concerns.
  • Promoting coordination in inter-State transport and communications.
  • Considering issues arising from the reorganisation of States.
  • Encouraging cooperation in internal security and public order-related coordination.
  • Facilitating cooperation in disaster management, infrastructure and regional development.
  • Promoting information sharing and administrative coordination among member governments.

Their deliberations can help identify common problems, build consensus and improve coordination across State boundaries. Their recommendations are generally advisory and do not displace the constitutional authority of the governments concerned.

10. North Eastern Council: A Distinct Regional Institution

The North Eastern Council is a separate statutory institution created under the North Eastern Council Act, 1971. It is not one of the five Zonal Councils established under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.

It was established to address the distinctive developmental, geographical, connectivity, security and administrative challenges of the North Eastern region.

Membership

The North Eastern Council covers the eight North Eastern States:

  • Arunachal Pradesh
  • Assam
  • Manipur
  • Meghalaya
  • Mizoram
  • Nagaland
  • Sikkim
  • Tripura

Constitutional and statutory development

  • The North Eastern Council Act was enacted in 1971.
  • The Council became operational in 1972.
  • The North Eastern Council (Amendment) Act, 2002 brought Sikkim into its membership.
  • The Union Home Minister serves as Chairperson under the amended institutional arrangement, with the Minister responsible for the Development of the North Eastern Region serving as Vice-Chairperson.

Functions and significance

  • Promoting coordinated regional planning.
  • Supporting infrastructure and connectivity.
  • Facilitating development programmes in the North Eastern region.
  • Encouraging cooperation among the eight member States.
  • Addressing regional concerns involving transport, communication and economic development.
  • Supporting regional integration and balanced development.

The North Eastern Council is important because the region has distinctive geographical, ethnic, cultural, connectivity and security considerations that require region-specific coordination.

12. Relationship with Other Federal Institutions

Article 131: Supreme Court's original jurisdiction

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court original jurisdiction in specified disputes between the Union and States or between States, where the dispute involves a question on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

The Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils provide deliberative forums. They do not replace the Supreme Court's constitutional jurisdiction.

Article 262: Inter-State river-water disputes

Parliament may provide by law for adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution or control of waters of inter-State rivers and river valleys. It may also provide for excluding the jurisdiction of courts, including the Supreme Court, in respect of such disputes.

The Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils may facilitate broader cooperation, but statutory river-water dispute mechanisms perform the specialised adjudicatory role.

Article 263 and administrative coordination

The Inter-State Council provides a national forum for consultation. Zonal Councils and the North Eastern Council provide regional platforms. Together, they support the cooperative dimension of Indian federalism.

GST Council under Article 279A

The GST Council is a specialised constitutional forum for recommendations on Goods and Services Tax. It is distinct from the Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils.

Its specialised fiscal role illustrates how the Constitution provides different institutions for different dimensions of intergovernmental coordination.

13. Cooperative and Competitive Federalism

These institutions contribute primarily to cooperative federalism by encouraging consultation, information sharing and coordinated action.

They can also indirectly support competitive federalism. Regional discussions allow governments to compare administrative approaches, identify successful practices and learn from the performance of other States.

Cooperative federalism

  • Joint deliberation on shared problems.
  • Consultation before major policy decisions.
  • Coordination in implementation.
  • Mutual assistance during emergencies.
  • Consensus-building among governments.

Competitive federalism

  • Sharing examples of effective governance.
  • Comparing development and service-delivery outcomes.
  • Encouraging innovation by State governments.
  • Promoting performance-based improvements.

14. Challenges and Institutional Limitations

  • Infrequent deliberation: Long gaps between meetings can weaken institutional continuity and reduce the value of consultation.
  • Political differences: Differences between the Union and State governments may affect the willingness to participate in consensus-building.
  • Advisory character: Recommendations may not translate into action without clear implementation responsibilities.
  • Weak follow-up: Decisions and recommendations require systematic monitoring and reporting.
  • Overlapping forums: Multiple institutions may discuss related matters, creating duplication unless their roles are clear.
  • Regional disparities: Differences in administrative capacity and development levels may complicate consensus.
  • Limited public visibility: Insufficient public access to agendas, proceedings and follow-up can reduce transparency.
  • Implementation gaps: Agreements require cooperation from departments and governments beyond the meeting itself.

15. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Regular meetings: Establish predictable meeting schedules for the Inter-State Council and regional councils.
  2. Stronger secretariat support: Improve research, documentation, coordination and follow-up capacity.
  3. Action-taken reporting: Maintain a transparent system for tracking recommendations and implementation.
  4. State-driven agendas: Allow States to place significant regional and national concerns before the appropriate forums.
  5. Evidence-based deliberation: Use data, expert inputs and impact assessments to support informed decisions.
  6. Clear institutional coordination: Define the respective roles of the Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, North Eastern Council and specialised statutory bodies.
  7. Inclusive participation: Ensure that smaller States, Union Territories and regions with distinctive concerns have meaningful opportunities to contribute.
  8. Transparency: Improve access to agendas, recommendations and non-confidential proceedings.
  9. Administrative follow-through: Assign responsible departments and timelines for agreed actions wherever appropriate.

The objective should be to make these institutions active forums of continuous federal engagement, rather than occasional ceremonial meetings.

17. Important Conceptual Distinctions

  • Inter-State Council vs Zonal Councils: The former is established under Article 263; the latter are statutory regional bodies under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
  • Zonal Councils vs North Eastern Council: Five Zonal Councils operate under the 1956 Act; the North Eastern Council is a separate institution under the 1971 Act.
  • Inter-State Council vs Supreme Court: The Council facilitates consultation; the Supreme Court exercises judicial jurisdiction under the Constitution.
  • Inter-State Council vs GST Council: The former is a broad intergovernmental forum; the latter is a specialised constitutional institution dealing with GST.
  • Advisory forum vs adjudicatory body: Deliberative councils promote consensus; adjudicatory institutions determine disputes within their legally assigned jurisdiction.

18. Integrated Revision Framework

Remember the institutional structure through three levels:

  1. National constitutional coordination: Inter-State Council under Article 263.
  2. Regional inter-State coordination: Five Zonal Councils under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
  3. Specialised North Eastern coordination: North Eastern Council under the North Eastern Council Act, 1971.

Connect these institutions with the broader constitutional framework:

  • Article 131 — Judicial resolution of specified federal disputes.
  • Article 262 — River-water dispute framework.
  • Article 263 — Intergovernmental consultation.
  • Article 279A — Fiscal coordination through the GST Council.

The central analytical theme is that Indian federalism depends not only on the formal distribution of powers but also on effective institutions for consultation, coordination and implementation.
