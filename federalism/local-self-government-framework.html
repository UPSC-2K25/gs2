1. Meaning and Evolution of Local Self-Government

Local self-government refers to institutions through which people participate in managing local affairs and delivering public services within a defined territorial area. It brings government closer to citizens and enables decisions to reflect local needs, priorities and circumstances.

Decentralisation is broader than the mere creation of local bodies. It involves the transfer of functions, authority, personnel and financial resources to institutions that can exercise meaningful decision-making powers.

Historical evolution

  • Ancient and traditional Indian communities had different forms of village-level collective decision-making.
  • Lord Ripon's Resolution of 1882 is commonly regarded as an important landmark in the development of local self-government under British rule.
  • The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced local self-government as a transferred subject under provincial dyarchy.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 further expanded provincial autonomy and the role of local institutions.
  • After Independence, Article 40 of the Constitution directed the State to organise village Panchayats and endow them with powers necessary to function as units of self-government.
  • The Balwant Rai Mehta Committee (1957) recommended democratic decentralisation through a three-tier Panchayati Raj structure.
  • The Ashok Mehta Committee (1978) recommended a two-tier structure and stronger political and administrative decentralisation.
  • The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, 1992 gave constitutional status to rural and urban local governments respectively.

2. Constitutional Vision of Decentralisation

Article 40: Directive Principle

Article 40, under Part IV of the Constitution, directs the State to organise village Panchayats and endow them with such powers and authority as may be necessary to enable them to function as units of self-government.

Article 40 provides the constitutional foundation for rural local government, but it does not itself prescribe the detailed institutional framework that now exists under Part IX.

Democratic decentralisation

Democratic decentralisation seeks to move decision-making closer to citizens through elected institutions, public participation and accountability.

  • Political decentralisation — elected local representatives.
  • Administrative decentralisation — transfer of responsibilities and staff.
  • Fiscal decentralisation — local revenue powers and intergovernmental transfers.
  • Participatory decentralisation — Gram Sabhas, ward committees and public deliberation.

These dimensions are interdependent. Elected local bodies without funds, staff or assigned functions may remain institutions of representation rather than effective institutions of self-government.

3. The 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IX, titled "The Panchayats", into the Constitution. It came into force on 24 April 1993.

The amendment sought to establish a constitutional framework for rural local government, regular elections, representation of disadvantaged groups, financial review and functional decentralisation.

Important provisions

  • Part IX — Articles 243 to 243O.
  • Eleventh Schedule — 29 subjects.
  • Constitutional recognition of Panchayati Raj institutions.
  • Regular elections and a five-year term.
  • Reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • At least one-third reservation for women under the constitutional minimum.
  • State Election Commission.
  • State Finance Commission.

4. Part IX: Constitutional Framework of Panchayats

Article 243: Definitions

Article 243 provides definitions for terms used in Part IX, including district, Gram Sabha, intermediate level, Panchayat, Panchayat area and village.

Article 243A: Gram Sabha

A Gram Sabha consists of persons registered in the electoral rolls relating to a village comprised within the area of a Panchayat at the village level.

The State Legislature may, by law, provide for the powers and functions of the Gram Sabha at the village level.

The Gram Sabha provides a foundation for direct participation in local governance. Its effectiveness depends on regular meetings, access to information, inclusion of marginalised groups and meaningful consideration of its decisions.

Article 243B: Constitution of Panchayats

Panchayats are to be constituted at the village, intermediate and district levels in every State. A State with a population not exceeding twenty lakh may choose not to constitute Panchayats at the intermediate level.

Article 243C: Composition

The State Legislature may determine the composition of Panchayats, subject to constitutional requirements. Seats are generally filled through direct election from territorial constituencies.

The Chairperson of a Panchayat at the village level is elected in the manner provided by State law. The Chairpersons at intermediate and district levels are elected by and from among the elected members as prescribed by the Constitution.

Article 243D: Reservation

  • Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population in the Panchayat area.
  • Not less than one-third of the total seats reserved for direct election are reserved for women, including women belonging to Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Not less than one-third of the offices of Chairpersons at each level are reserved for women, as provided by the Constitution.
  • State Legislatures may provide reservation for backward classes.

Many States have legislated for 50 per cent reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions. This is a State-level enhancement beyond the constitutional minimum.

Article 243E: Duration

Every Panchayat continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting, unless sooner dissolved under law.

Elections must be completed before the expiry of the five-year term. Where a Panchayat is dissolved prematurely, elections must ordinarily be completed within six months, subject to the constitutional exception where the remainder of the term is less than six months.

A Panchayat constituted after premature dissolution ordinarily continues only for the remainder of the original term.

Article 243F: Disqualifications

A person may be disqualified for membership of a Panchayat on grounds specified by State law, subject to the constitutional framework. The minimum age for contesting Panchayat elections is 21 years.

Article 243G: Powers, authority and responsibilities

State Legislatures may endow Panchayats with powers and authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government. This may include:

  • Preparation of plans for economic development and social justice.
  • Implementation of schemes for economic development and social justice.
  • Responsibilities relating to matters listed in the Eleventh Schedule.

Article 243G is enabling in nature. The actual extent of devolution depends on State legislation and administrative arrangements.

Article 243H: Powers to impose taxes and funds

State Legislatures may authorise Panchayats to levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees. They may also assign revenues, provide grants-in-aid and establish funds for Panchayat finances.

Article 243I: State Finance Commission

The Governor must constitute a Finance Commission within one year of the commencement of the 73rd Amendment and thereafter at the expiration of every fifth year.

The Commission reviews the financial position of Panchayats and recommends principles concerning distribution of revenues, assignment of taxes, grants-in-aid and measures to improve their financial position.

Article 243J: Audit of accounts

State Legislatures may make provisions concerning the maintenance and audit of Panchayat accounts.

Article 243K: Elections

The superintendence, direction and control of preparation of electoral rolls and conduct of Panchayat elections are vested in the State Election Commission.

The State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor. The Commissioner cannot be removed except in the manner and on the grounds prescribed for a Judge of a High Court, and service conditions cannot be varied to their disadvantage after appointment.

Article 243L: Application to Union Territories

Part IX applies to Union Territories, subject to the constitutional provisions governing their administration and modifications specified by the President.

Article 243M: Exceptions

Part IX does not automatically apply to certain areas and States specified in Article 243M, including Scheduled Areas and tribal areas covered by the Sixth Schedule.

Parliament may extend Part IX to Scheduled Areas and tribal areas with modifications and exceptions through legislation. This constitutional route led to the PESA Act.

Article 243N: Continuance of existing laws

Existing State laws relating to Panchayats continued for the constitutionally specified transitional period, unless amended or repealed earlier.

Article 243O: Bar to interference by courts

The validity of laws relating to delimitation of constituencies or allotment of seats cannot ordinarily be questioned in court. Panchayat elections can be challenged only through an election petition presented in the manner provided by State law.

5. Three-Tier Panchayati Raj Structure

Village level: Gram Panchayat

The Gram Panchayat is the elected executive institution at the village level. Its functions may include sanitation, drinking water, local roads, street lighting, maintenance of community assets and implementation of development schemes, depending on State law and devolution.

Intermediate level: Panchayat Samiti

The intermediate-level institution coordinates Panchayats within a block or equivalent area. It may support planning, implementation and monitoring of development programmes across villages.

District level: Zila Parishad

The Zila Parishad operates at the district level and may coordinate development plans, consolidate local priorities and facilitate implementation across intermediate-level Panchayats.

The exact nomenclature, composition and division of functions vary among States.

6. Eleventh Schedule: 29 Subjects

The Eleventh Schedule was added by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment. It lists 29 subjects that may be devolved to Panchayats under Article 243G.

  1. Agriculture, including agricultural extension.
  2. Land improvement, implementation of land reforms, land consolidation and soil conservation.
  3. Minor irrigation, water management and watershed development.
  4. Animal husbandry, dairying and poultry.
  5. Fisheries.
  6. Social forestry and farm forestry.
  7. Minor forest produce.
  8. Small-scale industries, including food-processing industries.
  9. Khadi, village and cottage industries.
  10. Rural housing.
  11. Drinking water.
  12. Fuel and fodder.
  13. Roads, culverts, bridges, ferries, waterways and other means of communication.
  14. Rural electrification, including distribution of electricity.
  15. Non-conventional energy sources.
  16. Poverty alleviation programmes.
  17. Education, including primary and secondary schools.
  18. Technical training and vocational education.
  19. Adult and non-formal education.
  20. Libraries.
  21. Cultural activities.
  22. Markets and fairs.
  23. Health and sanitation, including hospitals, primary health centres and dispensaries.
  24. Family welfare.
  25. Women and child development.
  26. Social welfare, including welfare of persons with disabilities.
  27. Welfare of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  28. Public distribution system.
  29. Maintenance of community assets.

Inclusion in the Eleventh Schedule does not automatically transfer every listed subject to Panchayats. Effective devolution requires State legislation, administrative orders, funds and personnel.

7. The 74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The Constitution (Seventy-fourth Amendment) Act, 1992 inserted Part IXA, titled "The Municipalities", into the Constitution. It came into force on 1 June 1993.

It provided constitutional recognition to urban local bodies and sought to strengthen democratic urban governance.

Important provisions

  • Part IXA — Articles 243P to 243ZG.
  • Twelfth Schedule — 18 subjects.
  • Constitutional recognition of Municipalities.
  • Regular elections and five-year tenure.
  • Reservation for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • At least one-third reservation for women under the constitutional minimum.
  • State Election Commission.
  • State Finance Commission.
  • Ward Committees in specified municipalities.
  • District Planning Committees and Metropolitan Planning Committees.

8. Part IXA: Constitutional Framework of Municipalities

Article 243P: Definitions

Article 243P defines important terms, including metropolitan area, municipality, municipal area, population and related concepts.

Article 243Q: Constitution of Municipalities

Three categories of urban local bodies are envisaged:

  • Nagar Panchayat — for a transitional area moving from rural to urban status.
  • Municipal Council — for a smaller urban area.
  • Municipal Corporation — for a larger urban area.

The Governor may specify the relevant transitional, smaller or larger urban area after considering factors such as population, density, revenue generated, non-agricultural employment and economic importance.

Industrial townships may be treated differently where municipal services are provided or proposed to be provided by an industrial establishment.

Article 243R: Composition

Members are generally elected directly from territorial constituencies known as wards. State law may provide for representation of persons with special knowledge or experience, Members of Parliament and State Legislatures representing the relevant areas, and other specified categories.

Article 243S: Ward Committees

Ward Committees are to be constituted in municipalities with a population of three lakh or more. State law may provide for their composition and territorial jurisdiction.

They are intended to strengthen neighbourhood-level participation and improve accountability in municipal service delivery.

Article 243T: Reservation

  • Seats are reserved for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in proportion to their population.
  • Not less than one-third of directly elected seats are reserved for women.
  • Not less than one-third of offices of Chairpersons are reserved for women in the manner provided by State law.
  • State Legislatures may provide reservation for backward classes.

Article 243U: Duration

Every Municipality continues for five years from the date appointed for its first meeting unless sooner dissolved. Elections must be completed before expiry of its term, subject to the constitutional provisions governing premature dissolution.

Article 243V: Disqualifications

A person may be disqualified from membership on grounds specified by law, subject to constitutional requirements. The minimum age for contesting municipal elections is 21 years.

Article 243W: Powers and responsibilities

State Legislatures may endow Municipalities with powers and authority necessary to function as institutions of self-government. These may include:

  • Preparation of plans for economic development and social justice.
  • Performance of functions relating to the matters in the Twelfth Schedule.
  • Implementation of schemes entrusted to them.

Article 243X: Municipal taxation and funds

State Legislatures may authorise Municipalities to levy, collect and appropriate taxes, duties, tolls and fees. They may also assign revenues, provide grants-in-aid and establish municipal funds.

Article 243Y: Finance Commission

The State Finance Commission constituted under Article 243I also reviews the financial position of Municipalities and recommends principles relating to revenue distribution, assigned taxes and grants-in-aid.

Article 243Z: Audit of accounts

State Legislatures may make provisions regarding the maintenance and audit of municipal accounts.

Article 243ZA: Municipal elections

The State Election Commission supervises, directs and controls the preparation of electoral rolls and conduct of municipal elections.

Article 243ZB: Application to Union Territories

Part IXA applies to Union Territories, subject to constitutional provisions and modifications specified by the President.

Article 243ZC: Exceptions

Part IXA does not automatically apply to certain areas and institutions specified in the Constitution, including areas covered by Article 244 and specified tribal areas.

Article 243ZD: District Planning Committee

A District Planning Committee is to be constituted in every State at the district level to consolidate plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities and prepare a draft development plan for the district as a whole.

Article 243ZE: Metropolitan Planning Committee

A Metropolitan Planning Committee is to be constituted in every metropolitan area to prepare a draft development plan for the metropolitan area as a whole.

Article 243ZF: Continuance of existing laws

Existing municipal laws continued during the constitutionally specified transitional period, unless amended or repealed earlier.

Article 243ZG: Bar to interference by courts

Delimitation and allotment of seats cannot ordinarily be questioned in court. Municipal elections may be challenged through election petitions presented in the manner provided by State law.

9. Twelfth Schedule: 18 Municipal Functions

The Twelfth Schedule lists 18 subjects that may be entrusted to Municipalities under Article 243W.

  1. Urban planning, including town planning.
  2. Regulation of land use and construction of buildings.
  3. Planning for economic and social development.
  4. Roads and bridges.
  5. Water supply for domestic, industrial and commercial purposes.
  6. Public health, sanitation, conservancy and solid waste management.
  7. Fire services.
  8. Urban forestry, protection of the environment and promotion of ecological aspects.
  9. Safeguarding the interests of weaker sections, including persons with disabilities.
  10. Slum improvement and upgradation.
  11. Urban poverty alleviation.
  12. Provision of urban amenities and facilities such as parks, gardens and playgrounds.
  13. Promotion of cultural, educational and aesthetic aspects.
  14. Burials, burial grounds, cremations, cremation grounds and electric crematoriums.
  15. Cattle pounds, prevention of cruelty to animals.
  16. Vital statistics, including registration of births and deaths.
  17. Public amenities, including street lighting, parking lots, bus stops and public conveniences.
  18. Regulation of slaughterhouses and tanneries.

As with the Eleventh Schedule, inclusion in the Twelfth Schedule does not automatically devolve every function. State legislation and administrative arrangements determine the actual powers exercised by Municipalities.

11. District Planning Committee and Metropolitan Planning Committee

District Planning Committee: Article 243ZD

The District Planning Committee consolidates plans prepared by Panchayats and Municipalities in the district and prepares a draft development plan for the district as a whole.

Not less than four-fifths of the total members of the Committee are to be elected by and from among the elected members of the district-level Panchayat and Municipalities in proportion to the ratio between rural and urban populations in the district.

The State Legislature determines the manner of election and representation, subject to the constitutional framework.

The Committee considers common interests between rural and urban areas, including spatial planning, sharing of water and other physical and natural resources, infrastructure development and environmental conservation.

Metropolitan Planning Committee: Article 243ZE

A Metropolitan Planning Committee prepares a draft development plan for the metropolitan area as a whole.

Not less than two-thirds of its members are to be elected by and from among the elected members of Municipalities and Chairpersons of Panchayats in the metropolitan area, in proportion to the ratio between the population of municipal and Panchayat areas.

The Committee considers coordinated planning across the metropolitan area, including spatial planning, resource sharing, infrastructure and environmental concerns.

Importance

  • Promotes integrated planning across rural and urban jurisdictions.
  • Reduces fragmented development decisions.
  • Connects local plans with broader regional priorities.
  • Supports coordinated infrastructure and environmental planning.

12. State Election Commission

Articles 243K and 243ZA establish the constitutional role of the State Election Commission in local body elections.

  • It supervises, directs and controls the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections to Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • The State Election Commissioner is appointed by the Governor.
  • The Commissioner enjoys constitutionally protected conditions of removal and service.
  • The Commission is institutionally distinct from the Election Commission of India.

Importance

An independent State Election Commission is necessary to ensure regular elections, continuity of representative local government and democratic accountability.

Challenges

  • Delays in delimitation and reservation processes.
  • Inadequate staff and logistical resources.
  • Disputes over electoral rolls and ward boundaries.
  • Delayed elections and extensions of administrative arrangements.
  • Dependence on State administrative machinery.

13. State Finance Commission

Articles 243I and 243Y provide for the State Finance Commission to review the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.

Composition and appointment

The Governor constitutes the Commission at the expiration of every fifth year. Its composition, qualifications and manner of selection are determined by State law.

Functions

  • Recommending principles governing distribution of taxes, duties, tolls and fees between the State and local bodies.
  • Recommending assignment of taxes, duties, tolls and fees to local bodies.
  • Recommending grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of the State.
  • Suggesting measures to improve the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Addressing other matters referred by the Governor in the interests of sound local finances.

Recommendations and accountability

The Governor is required to lay the recommendations of the Commission, together with an explanatory memorandum on action taken, before the State Legislature.

The Commission's recommendations are important for fiscal decentralisation, but their practical effect depends on State-level acceptance, implementation and predictable transfers.

14. Local Government and the Three Fs

The effectiveness of local self-government is often evaluated through the transfer of three essential elements: functions, funds and functionaries.

Functions

Local bodies need clearly assigned responsibilities and adequate authority to plan and implement local development and public services.

Funds

Local bodies require reliable own-source revenue, predictable transfers, grants and access to funds that match their assigned responsibilities.

Functionaries

Effective devolution requires adequately trained staff, clear administrative control and accountability to elected local institutions.

Why the three Fs matter

Transfer of functions without funds creates unfunded mandates. Transfer of funds without local decision-making weakens autonomy. Transfer of responsibilities without personnel reduces implementation capacity.

The three Fs must therefore be devolved together to create substantive local self-government.

15. Local Government Finances

Own-source revenue

Local bodies may raise revenue through taxes, duties, tolls, fees and other charges authorised by State law.

Important municipal revenue sources may include property tax, user charges, fees, licences and selected service-related levies.

Assigned revenues

State governments may assign specified taxes or shares of revenue to local bodies under the constitutional and statutory framework.

Grants-in-aid

State grants and transfers from the Union provide important support for local development and service delivery.

Finance Commission transfers

The Union Finance Commission recommends measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.

Article 280 provides the constitutional framework for the Union Finance Commission's recommendations.

Persistent financial constraints

  • Limited own-source revenue mobilisation.
  • Low property tax coverage and weak assessment systems in many urban areas.
  • Dependence on tied grants.
  • Irregular and delayed transfers.
  • Weak accounting and audit capacity.
  • Mismatch between assigned responsibilities and available resources.

16. PESA Act, 1996: Self-Governance in Scheduled Areas

The Provisions of the Panchayats (Extension to the Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996, commonly known as PESA, was enacted to extend Part IX of the Constitution to Fifth Schedule Areas with specified modifications and exceptions.

PESA recognises the importance of Gram Sabhas and traditional community institutions in tribal self-governance.

Important features

  • State Panchayat laws must be consistent with customary law, social and religious practices and traditional management practices of community resources.
  • The Gram Sabha safeguards traditions, cultural identity, community resources and customary dispute-resolution practices.
  • Gram Sabhas approve village development plans and identify beneficiaries under relevant programmes.
  • Consultation is required before land acquisition and resettlement in the manner specified by the Act.
  • Gram Sabhas or Panchayats at the appropriate level are entrusted with powers concerning minor forest produce, village markets and other specified matters.
  • State legislation must provide for safeguards against alienation of tribal land and appropriate action to restore unlawfully alienated land.
  • The reservation of seats in Scheduled Areas follows the special requirements laid down in the Act, including the reservation of seats for Scheduled Tribes.

Significance

  • Strengthens tribal self-governance.
  • Recognises customary institutions and community resources.
  • Promotes participation in local development decisions.
  • Supports protection against land alienation and exploitation.
  • Connects constitutional decentralisation with indigenous governance practices.

Implementation challenges

  • Uneven adoption of conforming State laws.
  • Overlap between Gram Sabhas and line departments.
  • Weak recognition of community control over resources.
  • Limited awareness and administrative capacity.
  • Conflicts between customary practices and other statutory frameworks.

17. Local Self-Government and the Sixth Schedule

The Sixth Schedule establishes Autonomous District Councils and Regional Councils in specified tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram.

These institutions have constitutionally recognised legislative, judicial, administrative and financial powers over specified subjects.

Their structure differs from the Panchayati Raj system under Part IX. The Sixth Schedule provides a distinct model of tribal autonomy, while PESA extends the Part IX framework to Fifth Schedule Areas with modifications.

18. Committees and Commissions on Local Government

Balwant Rai Mehta Committee, 1957

  • Recommended democratic decentralisation.
  • Recommended a three-tier Panchayati Raj structure.
  • Emphasised the role of elected institutions in development administration.
  • Supported transfer of responsibility to local institutions.

V. T. Krishnamachari and early development administration reviews

Early reviews of community development and local administration helped shape the debate on democratic participation and institutional decentralisation.

Ashok Mehta Committee, 1978

  • Recommended a two-tier structure.
  • Emphasised the district as an important unit of decentralised planning.
  • Recommended stronger political participation and accountability.
  • Suggested constitutional recognition for Panchayati Raj institutions.

G. V. K. Rao Committee, 1985

  • Emphasised the central role of Panchayati Raj institutions in rural development.
  • Recommended strengthening district-level planning and administration.
  • Highlighted the need to reduce excessive bureaucratic control.

L. M. Singhvi Committee, 1986

  • Recommended constitutional recognition for Panchayats.
  • Emphasised the Gram Sabha as the foundation of local democracy.
  • Supported judicial protection for local institutions.

Second Administrative Reforms Commission

The Commission examined local governance, decentralised planning, accountability, service delivery and the relationship between local bodies and administrative institutions.

Union Finance Commissions

Successive Finance Commissions have recommended grants and institutional measures to improve the financial capacity of Panchayats and Municipalities. Their recommendations have contributed to the development of local public finance and accountability systems.

19. Importance of Local Self-Government

  • Democratic deepening: Extends representative government to the grassroots.
  • Citizen participation: Provides opportunities for citizens to influence local decisions.
  • Social inclusion: Reservation creates political representation for women, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Responsive administration: Local institutions are better placed to identify many location-specific needs.
  • Accountability: Citizens can more directly question elected local representatives.
  • Development planning: Local priorities can inform district and regional plans.
  • Federalism: Strengthens the third tier of governance and gives practical effect to decentralisation.
  • Innovation: Local bodies can develop context-specific solutions and share successful practices.

20. Challenges in the Functioning of Local Bodies

Inadequate devolution

Many local bodies lack effective control over functions listed in the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules. Departments may continue to exercise authority over responsibilities that could be managed locally.

Financial weakness

Limited tax bases, poor collection systems and dependence on higher-level grants constrain local decision-making.

Administrative dependence

Local bodies may lack adequate technical and administrative personnel. Officials may remain accountable primarily to State departments rather than elected local institutions.

Elite capture

Local power structures may influence beneficiary selection, resource distribution and decision-making, limiting the participation of disadvantaged groups.

Proxy representation

In some places, elected women representatives face informal control by family members or other local power holders. This weakens substantive political empowerment.

Weak Gram Sabha and ward participation

Poor attendance, inadequate information and limited follow-up can reduce participatory institutions to formal procedures.

Irregular elections

Delimitation disputes, reservation litigation and administrative delays can disrupt regular local democratic processes.

Fragmented urban governance

Municipalities often share responsibilities with development authorities, parastatal agencies and State departments. This can create overlapping accountability and fragmented service delivery.

Weak planning capacity

District and Metropolitan Planning Committees may not function effectively, weakening integrated development planning.

21. Urban Governance: Additional Institutional Issues

Urban local governments face distinctive challenges because cities require integrated planning, large-scale infrastructure, reliable services and coordination across multiple agencies.

Fragmentation of authority

Urban development authorities, water and sewerage boards, transport agencies and other specialised bodies may exercise significant functions within municipal areas.

Where these agencies operate independently of elected Municipalities, responsibility for outcomes becomes difficult for citizens to identify.

Mayoral leadership

The role, powers and tenure of Mayors differ across States. In some systems, executive authority rests substantially with municipal commissioners and State administrative structures.

Stronger mayoral leadership, clear responsibility and effective council oversight can improve urban accountability.

Metropolitan coordination

Urban growth often crosses municipal boundaries. Metropolitan Planning Committees can support coordinated transport, land-use, water, housing and environmental planning.

22. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Meaningful devolution: Transfer functions, funds and functionaries in a coordinated manner.
  2. Activity mapping: Clearly identify which level of government is responsible for each function and service.
  3. Strengthen local revenues: Improve property tax systems, user charges, revenue assessment and collection efficiency.
  4. Timely Finance Commissions: Constitute State Finance Commissions regularly and act on their recommendations.
  5. Independent elections: Ensure adequate resources and institutional independence for State Election Commissions.
  6. Professional capacity: Build local planning, engineering, accounting and public management capabilities.
  7. Strengthen Gram Sabhas: Improve public access to information, social audits, meeting regularity and participation.
  8. Empower women representatives: Provide training, institutional support and protection against proxy control.
  9. Integrated urban governance: Clarify the relationship between Municipalities and parastatal agencies.
  10. Effective district planning: Make District and Metropolitan Planning Committees functional and inclusive.
  11. Transparent public finance: Strengthen local budgets, accounts, audits and public disclosure.
  12. Outcome-based accountability: Evaluate local bodies on service quality, inclusion and citizen satisfaction rather than expenditure alone.

24. Integrated Revision Framework

Local self-government can be understood through five connected dimensions:

  1. Constitutional recognition: Articles 40, Part IX and Part IXA.
  2. Democratic representation: Elections, reservation, Gram Sabhas and Ward Committees.
  3. Functional devolution: Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules.
  4. Fiscal and administrative capacity: State Finance Commissions, local revenues, funds and staff.
  5. Integrated planning and accountability: District Planning Committees, Metropolitan Planning Committees, audits and citizen participation.

The central analytical issue is the gap between constitutional recognition and substantive decentralisation. The effectiveness of local government depends on the real transfer of authority, resources and accountability.
