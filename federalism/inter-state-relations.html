1. Meaning and Constitutional Framework

Inter-State relations concern the constitutional, political, administrative, economic and legal relationships among the States of the Indian Union. They include cooperation on common interests, management of shared resources, resolution of disputes and maintenance of national economic integration.

India's federal structure combines State autonomy with constitutional mechanisms for coordination and dispute settlement. Since rivers, transport networks, markets, environmental systems and populations frequently cross State boundaries, effective governance requires sustained intergovernmental cooperation.

Important constitutional provisions

  • Article 131: Original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in specified federal disputes.
  • Article 261: Recognition of public acts, records and judicial proceedings throughout India.
  • Article 262: Adjudication of inter-State river-water disputes.
  • Article 263: Inter-State Council.
  • Articles 301–307: Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse within India.
  • Article 3: Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States.

2. Constitutional Principles Governing Inter-State Relations

Federalism and constitutional supremacy

States exercise powers assigned by the Constitution. Their relations with one another are governed by constitutional provisions, valid legislation and judicial interpretation. Neither a State nor the Union may act outside the limits of the Constitution.

Cooperative federalism

Cooperative federalism emphasises consultation, coordination and joint action. It is particularly important where a policy problem cannot be effectively addressed by one State alone.

Comity between States

Constitutional governments are expected to respect the legitimate interests and legal authority of other States. This principle supports peaceful dispute resolution, mutual recognition of legal acts and avoidance of unnecessary intergovernmental confrontation.

National integration and common market

The Constitution seeks to preserve the unity of India while allowing regional diversity. The provisions on inter-State trade and commerce help prevent arbitrary barriers between State economies.

3. Article 131: Supreme Court's Original Jurisdiction

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction in specified disputes involving the Union and States or disputes between States, where the existence or extent of a legal right is involved.

Categories of disputes

  1. A dispute between the Government of India and one or more States.
  2. A dispute between the Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other.
  3. A dispute between two or more States.

Essential conditions

  • The dispute must involve the existence or extent of a legal right.
  • The parties must fall within the categories specified in Article 131.
  • The dispute must not be excluded by the Constitution or a valid constitutional provision.

Important limitation

Article 131 is not a general forum for every political disagreement between governments. A dispute must satisfy the constitutional requirements of original jurisdiction.

The Supreme Court's jurisdiction under Article 131 is also subject to the constitutional arrangements concerning certain disputes, including those governed by Article 262.

4. Article 262: Inter-State River-Water Disputes

Article 262 empowers Parliament to provide for the adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution or control of waters of inter-State rivers or river valleys.

Two constitutional powers

  • Parliament may provide by law for adjudication of such disputes.
  • Parliament may provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction over such disputes, subject to the constitutional scheme.

Important legislation

Parliament enacted the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 to provide a statutory framework for adjudication through tribunals.

The River Boards Act, 1956 provides for the establishment of river boards to advise on the regulation and development of inter-State rivers and river valleys.

Role of tribunals

  • A tribunal may be constituted under the statutory framework when the prescribed conditions are met.
  • It examines the competing claims and relevant evidence concerning the river or river system.
  • It determines the dispute in accordance with the governing law and the terms of reference.
  • The statutory framework provides for the publication and implementation of tribunal decisions.

Why water disputes are complex

  • Rivers cross political and administrative boundaries.
  • Rainfall and water availability vary across seasons and years.
  • States have competing irrigation, drinking-water and industrial needs.
  • Reservoir operation and dam management affect downstream users.
  • Population growth and urbanisation increase demand.
  • Climate variability adds uncertainty to long-term allocation.

5. Major Inter-State River-Water Disputes

River-water disputes illustrate the difficulty of balancing regional interests with equitable and sustainable resource management.

Cauvery dispute

  • The dispute principally concerns the sharing of Cauvery waters among Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Kerala and the Union Territory of Puducherry.
  • Historical agreements, basin hydrology, irrigation requirements and drinking-water needs have been central to the dispute.
  • The Cauvery Water Disputes Tribunal delivered its final award in 2007.
  • The Supreme Court delivered its judgment in 2018, modifying aspects of the allocation.
  • The Cauvery Water Management Authority was established to support implementation of the allocation framework.

Krishna dispute

  • The Krishna basin involves Maharashtra, Karnataka, Telangana and Andhra Pradesh.
  • Water allocation, project approvals and reservoir management have been important areas of disagreement.
  • The Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal framework has evolved through successive adjudicatory arrangements and changes following State reorganisation.

Ravi-Beas dispute

  • The dispute has involved Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan and other relevant interests.
  • The Sutlej-Yamuna Link canal issue has been a major element of the disagreement.
  • It raises questions concerning water allocation, implementation of agreements and constitutional responsibilities.

Mahadayi (Mandovi) dispute

The dispute involves Goa, Karnataka and Maharashtra. It concerns the use and diversion of waters of the Mahadayi river system, with implications for drinking-water supply, ecology and regional development.

Mahanadi dispute

The dispute between Chhattisgarh and Odisha concerns water utilisation, upstream projects, downstream flows and the interests of communities dependent on the river basin.

Common lessons

  • Reliable and shared hydrological data are essential.
  • Tribunal decisions require effective implementation mechanisms.
  • Consultation should begin before disputes become acute.
  • Environmental flows and ecological sustainability must be considered.
  • Basin-wide planning is preferable to isolated project decisions.

6. Article 263: Inter-State Council

Article 263 empowers the President to establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by its establishment.

Functions

  1. Inquiring into and advising upon disputes that may have arisen between States.
  2. Investigating and discussing subjects in which some or all States, or the Union and one or more States, have a common interest.
  3. Making recommendations for better coordination of policy and action on such subjects.

Establishment

The Inter-State Council was established by a Presidential Order in 1990 following the recommendations of the Sarkaria Commission.

Composition and functioning

The Prime Minister chairs the Council. Chief Ministers of States and the relevant Union Territory representatives participate along with designated Union Ministers and other members specified in the establishing order.

The Council provides a forum for political and administrative dialogue on matters of common interest.

Nature and significance

  • It is a constitutional institution when established under Article 263.
  • It is consultative and recommendatory in nature.
  • It is not a judicial tribunal.
  • It promotes cooperative and collaborative federalism.
  • It can help prevent disputes through early consultation.

7. Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils are statutory bodies established under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. They provide regional forums for cooperation among States and between States and the Union.

Five Zonal Councils

  1. Northern Zonal Council
  2. Central Zonal Council
  3. Eastern Zonal Council
  4. Western Zonal Council
  5. Southern Zonal Council

Composition and leadership

  • The Union Home Minister is the Chairperson of each Zonal Council.
  • Chief Ministers of the States in the zone are members, along with other representatives specified by the Act.
  • The Chief Minister of one of the member States serves as Vice-Chairperson by rotation, according to the statutory framework.

Areas of discussion

  • Inter-State boundary and regional disputes.
  • Economic and social planning.
  • Transport and communication.
  • Border-area development.
  • Public security and administrative coordination.
  • Linguistic minorities and related concerns.

Significance

Zonal Councils provide a practical regional mechanism for resolving issues before they escalate into larger political or legal disputes. Their regional focus complements the nationwide role of the Inter-State Council.

8. North Eastern Council

The North Eastern Council was established under the North Eastern Council Act, 1971. It provides a regional institution for coordinated planning and development in the North-Eastern region.

Role and functions

  • Promotes balanced regional development.
  • Supports coordination on infrastructure and connectivity.
  • Facilitates cooperation among participating States.
  • Addresses common regional economic and social concerns.
  • Encourages coordination with the Union Government.

Regional significance

The North-Eastern region has distinctive geographical, ethnic, ecological and strategic characteristics. A regional coordination institution helps address development and connectivity concerns that extend beyond individual State boundaries.

9. Inter-State Boundary Disputes

Boundary disputes arise from disagreements concerning the location or interpretation of State boundaries, often involving historical administrative arrangements, linguistic identities, local claims and resource interests.

Constitutional position

Article 3 empowers Parliament to form new States and alter the areas, boundaries or names of existing States, subject to the prescribed constitutional procedure.

A Bill for this purpose requires the President's recommendation. Where the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President refers it to the legislature of that State for expressing its views within the specified period.

The State Legislature's views are not binding on Parliament. The Constitution places the final legislative authority for such changes with Parliament.

Examples of boundary-related disagreements

  • Assam and neighbouring North-Eastern States.
  • Maharashtra and Karnataka.
  • Other disputes arising from historical boundary demarcation and local administrative claims.

Challenges

  • Conflicting historical records and maps.
  • Local identity and linguistic concerns.
  • Political mobilisation around territorial claims.
  • Disputes over land, forests and natural resources.
  • Effects on policing, public services and local communities.

Way forward

  • Use joint surveys and mutually accepted mapping methods.
  • Promote negotiation through institutional forums.
  • Protect the rights and livelihoods of affected communities.
  • Use mediation and judicial processes where appropriate.
  • Avoid unilateral action that may aggravate tensions.

10. Article 261: Public Acts, Records and Judicial Proceedings

Article 261 promotes legal continuity and mutual recognition across India.

  • Full faith and credit must be given throughout India to public acts, records and judicial proceedings of the Union and every State.
  • Parliament may determine by law the manner in which such acts, records and proceedings are proved and their effect determined.
  • Final civil judgments or orders delivered by civil courts anywhere in India are capable of execution throughout India according to law.

This provision reduces legal fragmentation and helps individuals and institutions rely on public records and judicial outcomes across State boundaries.

11. Freedom of Inter-State Trade, Commerce and Intercourse

Part XIII of the Constitution, comprising Articles 301–307, seeks to preserve economic integration by regulating restrictions on trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India.

Article 301: Freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse

Subject to the other provisions of Part XIII, trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India shall be free.

The provision seeks to prevent the creation of arbitrary economic barriers between States and supports the idea of a common national market.

Article 302: Parliament's power to impose restrictions

Parliament may impose restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse between one State and another or within any part of India, as may be required in the public interest.

Article 303: Restrictions on legislative preference

Neither Parliament nor a State Legislature may ordinarily make a law giving preference to one State over another or discriminating between States in matters relating to trade and commerce.

Parliament may make a law giving preference or discrimination where it is declared to be necessary for dealing with a situation arising from scarcity of goods in any part of India.

Article 304: Restrictions by States

Article 304 permits States to impose certain restrictions, subject to constitutional conditions.

  • Under clause (a), a State may impose taxes on goods imported from other States or Union Territories if similar goods produced or manufactured within that State are subject to the same tax, so as not to discriminate between imported and local goods.
  • Under clause (b), a State may impose reasonable restrictions on the freedom of trade, commerce or intercourse with or within that State in the public interest, subject to the requirement of the President's previous sanction for the introduction or movement of the relevant Bill.

Article 305: Saving of existing laws and State monopolies

Article 305 protects specified existing laws and laws providing for State monopolies, subject to its constitutional conditions.

Article 306: Omitted provision

Article 306 was omitted by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956. It is relevant as part of the historical evolution of Part XIII.

Article 307: Authority for carrying out Articles 301–304

Parliament may by law appoint an authority to carry out the purposes of Articles 301–304 and confer on it the powers and duties considered necessary.

12. Judicial Interpretation of Trade and Commerce Provisions

Atiabari Tea Co. Ltd. v. State of Assam (1961)

The Supreme Court examined the scope of Article 301 and the constitutional protection of freedom of trade and commerce. The judgment is an important starting point for understanding the restrictions that may affect the movement of trade.

Automobile Transport (Rajasthan) Ltd. v. State of Rajasthan (1962)

The Court considered the relationship between Article 301 and regulatory measures. The decision is significant for distinguishing restrictions on trade from measures that facilitate and regulate its movement.

Jindal Stainless Ltd. v. State of Haryana (2016)

The Constitution Bench considered the constitutional validity of State taxes affecting inter-State trade under Part XIII. The judgment clarified the approach to discriminatory taxation and the operation of Article 304(a).

Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022)

Although primarily concerning GST and legislative powers, the judgment is relevant to the broader relationship between national economic integration, fiscal federalism and the constitutional autonomy of the Union and States.

13. Inter-State Migration and Labour Mobility

Migration across State boundaries is an important dimension of inter-State relations. Workers and families often move between States for employment, education, healthcare and livelihood opportunities.

Governance concerns

  • Portability of welfare benefits and public services.
  • Access to food security and social protection.
  • Registration and protection of migrant workers.
  • Housing, sanitation and urban service pressures.
  • Language barriers and social integration.
  • Coordination between origin and destination States.

Constitutional and policy dimensions

Freedom of movement and residence under Article 19(1)(d) and Article 19(1)(e), subject to constitutional restrictions, supports mobility within India for citizens.

Effective inter-State governance requires coordination among labour departments, local authorities, welfare institutions and public service providers.

Way forward

  • Improve portability of eligible welfare benefits.
  • Strengthen inter-State information and registration systems.
  • Ensure access to essential services for migrant households.
  • Promote coordination between origin and destination States.
  • Protect workers against exploitation and unsafe conditions.

14. Inter-State Environmental and Public Health Governance

Environmental and public health challenges frequently cross State boundaries. Pollution, river-basin degradation, forest loss, wildlife movement, epidemics and disaster risks cannot always be managed through isolated State action.

Environmental coordination

  • Air pollution can move across administrative boundaries.
  • River pollution affects downstream States.
  • Forest and wildlife ecosystems often span several States.
  • Disaster management may require shared resources and coordinated response.

Public health coordination

  • Communicable diseases can spread across State borders.
  • Surveillance and information-sharing require coordination.
  • Transport and migration networks influence disease transmission.
  • Joint preparedness strengthens emergency response.

Way forward

  • Adopt basin-level and ecosystem-based planning.
  • Share reliable environmental and health data.
  • Strengthen joint monitoring and enforcement.
  • Use regional institutions to coordinate policy and implementation.
  • Promote community participation in shared-resource management.

15. Major Challenges in Inter-State Relations

1. Political competition

Differences in political leadership and party interests can affect negotiations and delay the resolution of disputes.

2. Weak institutional follow-through

Consultative bodies may be underutilised, while recommendations may not receive timely implementation.

3. Delayed dispute resolution

Complex technical evidence, prolonged proceedings and difficulties in implementing decisions can make disputes persistent.

4. Data asymmetry

Inconsistent or contested information on river flows, water use, population and environmental conditions can weaken intergovernmental trust.

5. Resource competition

Competition over water, energy, land, infrastructure and economic opportunities can create tensions between neighbouring States.

6. Regional inequalities

Differences in fiscal capacity, infrastructure and development levels can affect cooperation and perceptions of fairness.

7. Administrative fragmentation

Different departmental procedures and priorities can obstruct coordinated implementation of inter-State agreements.

16. Reforms and the Way Forward

  1. Strengthen constitutional forums: Make regular and meaningful use of the Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils.
  2. Promote early dispute prevention: Use consultation, mediation and joint technical committees before disagreements become prolonged disputes.
  3. Improve water governance: Strengthen basin-level planning, data transparency, ecological safeguards and implementation mechanisms.
  4. Build shared information systems: Develop reliable and mutually accepted databases for water, environment, transport and public health.
  5. Respect constitutional processes: Use Article 3 and other relevant provisions in accordance with their prescribed procedures.
  6. Protect the common market: Avoid discriminatory barriers and ensure compliance with Part XIII.
  7. Strengthen regional institutions: Improve the capacity and follow-up mechanisms of Zonal Councils and the North Eastern Council.
  8. Institutionalise cooperative governance: Encourage regular dialogue among Chief Ministers, departments and local authorities.
  9. Prioritise citizens' interests: Ensure that disputes do not unnecessarily disrupt access to essential services, livelihoods or public infrastructure.

18. Important Distinctions

Concepts Distinction
Article 131 and Article 262 Article 131 provides original jurisdiction in specified federal legal disputes; Article 262 enables a special framework for inter-State river-water adjudication and possible exclusion of court jurisdiction.
Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils The Inter-State Council is based on Article 263; Zonal Councils are statutory regional bodies under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956.
Article 262 and Article 263 Article 262 concerns river-water dispute adjudication; Article 263 concerns consultation and coordination on common interests and disputes.
Article 301 and Article 302 Article 301 declares freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse; Article 302 permits Parliament to impose restrictions in the public interest.
Article 303 and Article 304 Article 303 restricts legislative preference or discrimination between States, subject to its exception; Article 304 permits specified State taxation and restrictions subject to constitutional conditions.
Judicial and consultative mechanisms Courts and tribunals adjudicate disputes within their jurisdiction; councils primarily facilitate dialogue and recommendations.

19. Integrated Revision Framework

Inter-State relations can be understood through five connected dimensions:

  1. Adjudication: Article 131 and Article 262 provide constitutional routes for specified disputes.
  2. Consultation: Article 263 and regional councils promote intergovernmental dialogue.
  3. Legal integration: Article 261 supports recognition of public acts, records and judicial proceedings.
  4. Economic integration: Articles 301–307 regulate freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse.
  5. Regional cooperation: Statutory institutions and joint mechanisms support the management of shared resources and common challenges.

The central analytical issue is how to balance State interests and autonomy with national integration, equitable resource use and peaceful resolution of disputes.
