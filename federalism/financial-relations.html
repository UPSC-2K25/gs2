1. Meaning and Constitutional Framework

Financial relations refer to the constitutional distribution of taxation powers, allocation of tax revenues, transfer of resources, grants-in-aid and borrowing powers between the Union and the States.

India's federal structure assigns important revenue-raising powers to the Union while requiring substantial expenditure by State governments. This creates a structural need for financial transfers and institutional coordination.

The principal provisions are contained in Part XII of the Constitution, particularly Articles 264–293. They must be read with the Seventh Schedule, the GST framework, the Finance Commission provisions and the constitutional distribution of executive and legislative responsibilities.

Core dimensions

  • Tax assignment: Which level of government can impose particular taxes?
  • Tax sharing: How are specified Union tax proceeds distributed?
  • Grants: How does the Union transfer resources to States?
  • Institutional allocation: What role does the Finance Commission play?
  • Fiscal coordination: How do governments coordinate through the GST Council and other mechanisms?
  • Borrowing: What constitutional limits govern public debt?

2. Constitutional Distribution of Taxing Powers

The Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects between Parliament and State Legislatures. The distribution of taxation powers is a major component of this arrangement.

Union taxation powers

Parliament has exclusive legislative competence over the taxation subjects assigned to the Union List, subject to the Constitution. Important examples include customs duties, corporation tax, taxes on income other than agricultural income, and specified excise duties.

State taxation powers

State Legislatures have competence over taxation subjects assigned to the State List, subject to constitutional limitations. Examples include taxes on agricultural income, land and buildings, electricity, and specified taxes on vehicles and professions.

Concurrent and shared arrangements

The Constitution does not create a general concurrent taxation list. However, some taxation arrangements involve shared legislative or revenue responsibilities. GST is the most significant example, with Parliament and State Legislatures exercising concurrent powers under Article 246A, subject to the special constitutional framework.

3. Taxation Powers Before and After GST

The Constitution's original taxation framework relied on separate Union and State tax fields. The 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 introduced the Goods and Services Tax framework and substantially altered this distribution.

Important changes

  • Article 246A created special legislative powers for GST.
  • Parliament has exclusive power to make laws concerning GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Parliament and State Legislatures have power to make laws concerning GST on intra-State supplies, subject to the constitutional provisions.
  • Article 269A provides for the levy and collection of GST on inter-State supplies and the apportionment of proceeds between the Union and States.
  • Article 279A established the GST Council.
  • Several earlier Union and State indirect taxes were subsumed into the GST system, subject to constitutional exclusions and transitional arrangements.

Important exclusions and continuing taxes

GST did not subsume every tax. Alcoholic liquor for human consumption remains outside GST. The Constitution also provides a special arrangement for specified petroleum products, with GST to apply from a date recommended by the GST Council.

Basic customs duty continues separately. Certain other taxes and duties also remain outside the GST framework according to the Constitution and applicable laws.

4. Articles 268–271: Distribution and Assignment of Taxes

Article 268: Duties levied by the Union but collected and appropriated by States

Article 268 concerns specified stamp duties and duties of excise on medicinal and toilet preparations. These duties are levied by the Union but, in the cases covered by the provision, collected and appropriated by the States within which they are leviable.

Article 269: Taxes levied and collected by the Union but assigned to States

Article 269 deals with specified taxes on the sale or purchase of goods and on the consignment of goods in the course of inter-State trade or commerce, as provided by the constitutional framework.

The provision must be read in light of the GST-related amendments. Inter-State GST is now principally governed by Article 269A.

Article 269A: GST on inter-State supplies

  • GST on supplies in the course of inter-State trade or commerce is levied and collected by the Government of India.
  • The tax is apportioned between the Union and the States in the manner provided by Parliament by law on the recommendations of the GST Council.
  • Parliament may formulate principles for determining when a supply takes place in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Import of goods or services into India is treated as an inter-State supply for the purposes of GST.

Article 270: Taxes levied and distributed between the Union and States

Article 270 provides for distribution between the Union and States of specified Union taxes and duties. The constitutionally prescribed share of States is determined through the Finance Commission framework, subject to the provisions and exclusions in the Article.

The divisible pool is not simply the whole of the Union's tax revenue. Certain taxes, cesses and surcharges are excluded under the constitutional framework.

Article 271: Surcharge for Union purposes

Parliament may increase specified duties and taxes referred to in Articles 269 and 270 by a surcharge for Union purposes. The proceeds of such a surcharge form part of the Consolidated Fund of India.

The Article's current text excludes goods and services tax under Article 246A from this surcharge power.

Article 268A and Article 272: Historical provisions

Article 268A, relating to service tax, was omitted by the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016 as part of the transition to GST.

Article 272, which dealt with distribution of certain Union excise duties, was omitted by the Constitution (Eightieth Amendment) Act, 2000.

5. Other Important Taxation Provisions

Article 274: Bills affecting taxation in which States are interested

A Bill or amendment imposing or varying a tax or duty in which States are interested, or otherwise falling within the specified categories, requires the President's recommendation before introduction or movement in Parliament.

Article 276: Taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments

State Legislatures and local authorities may impose taxes on professions, trades, callings and employments, subject to the constitutional ceiling and other conditions in Article 276.

The current constitutional ceiling is ₹2,500 per person per annum. This is distinct from income tax.

Article 277: Savings

Article 277 protects certain taxes, duties, cesses or fees that were lawfully levied immediately before the commencement of the Constitution, subject to the conditions and limitations contained in the Article.

Article 279: Calculation of net proceeds

  • Net proceeds mean the proceeds of a tax or duty reduced by the cost of collection.
  • The calculation and certification of net proceeds are determined by the Comptroller and Auditor General of India.
  • The CAG's certificate on the calculation of net proceeds is final under the Article.

6. GST Council: Article 279A

Article 279A provides for the establishment of the GST Council by the President. It is a constitutional forum for the Union and States to make recommendations and coordinate decisions concerning GST.

Composition

  • The Union Finance Minister is the Chairperson.
  • The Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance is a member.
  • Each State nominates a Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or another Minister nominated by the State Government, as a member.
  • The State members choose one among themselves to be the Vice-Chairperson, for a period they decide.

Functions

The Council makes recommendations on important GST matters, including:

  • Taxes, cesses and surcharges to be subsumed into GST.
  • Goods and services that may be subject to GST or exempted.
  • Model GST laws and principles governing the levy of GST.
  • Apportionment of GST levied on inter-State supplies.
  • Place-of-supply principles.
  • Threshold limits for exemption from GST.
  • Rates, including floor rates with bands, as appropriate.
  • Special rates for a specified period during natural calamities or disasters.
  • Special provisions for specified States.

Decision-making and voting

  • One-half of the total number of members constitutes the quorum.
  • Every decision requires at least three-fourths of the weighted votes of members present and voting.
  • The Union Government's vote has a weight of one-third of the total votes cast.
  • The votes of all State Governments taken together have a weight of two-thirds of the total votes cast.

Constitutional significance

  • Institutionalises fiscal cooperation.
  • Provides a common forum for indirect tax policy.
  • Encourages harmonisation of the national market.
  • Gives States a formal role in shaping GST policy.

Nature of GST Council recommendations

In Union of India v. Mohit Minerals Pvt. Ltd. (2022), the Supreme Court held that GST Council recommendations are not binding on Parliament and State Legislatures in the exercise of their legislative powers. They have persuasive value and are important to the cooperative federal framework.

The judgment emphasised that the constitutional scheme envisages dialogue and coordination rather than the complete surrender of legislative autonomy by either level of government.

7. Finance Commission: Articles 280 and 281

The Finance Commission is a constitutional body established by the President under Article 280. It recommends how specified financial resources should be distributed between the Union and States and among the States.

Constitutional composition

  • The President constitutes a Finance Commission every five years or earlier if considered necessary.
  • It consists of a Chairperson and four other members.
  • Parliament determines by law the qualifications of members and the manner of their selection.

Functions under Article 280

  1. Recommending the distribution between the Union and States of the net proceeds of taxes that are to be divided between them, and the allocation among States of their respective shares.
  2. Recommending the principles governing grants-in-aid of State revenues from the Consolidated Fund of India.
  3. Recommending measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  4. Recommending measures needed to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Municipalities, based on recommendations of the State Finance Commission.
  5. Considering any other matter referred to it by the President in the interests of sound finance.

Article 281: Recommendations to Parliament

The President causes every recommendation of the Finance Commission, together with an explanatory memorandum on the action taken, to be laid before each House of Parliament.

Finance Commission recommendations are not automatically self-executing legislation. Their implementation operates through the constitutional and governmental processes, including the action-taken memorandum.

8. Vertical and Horizontal Devolution

Vertical devolution

Vertical devolution concerns the distribution of the divisible pool of Union taxes between the Union and all States collectively.

The Finance Commission recommends the States' share in the divisible pool, taking account of the relevant constitutional framework and fiscal considerations.

Horizontal devolution

Horizontal devolution concerns the distribution of the States' collective share among individual States.

Finance Commissions have used different criteria to balance equity, need, efficiency and fiscal performance. The criteria and their relative weights vary between Finance Commission awards.

Common considerations in horizontal devolution

  • Income distance: Addresses differences in the fiscal capacity of States.
  • Population: Reflects the scale of public service responsibilities.
  • Area: Recognises the cost of providing services across geographically large territories.
  • Forest and ecology: Recognises ecological resources and conservation responsibilities.
  • Demographic performance: Rewards specified demographic outcomes, as defined by the relevant Commission.
  • Tax and fiscal effort: Can recognise States' efforts to mobilise their own revenues.

These are examples of criteria used in Finance Commission recommendations; the precise formula must always be taken from the relevant Commission's report.

9. Grants-in-Aid: Articles 275 and 282

Article 275: Statutory grants

Article 275 provides for grants-in-aid of the revenues of States that Parliament may provide to States in need of assistance. These grants are charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.

The Article also provides for special grants for promoting the welfare of Scheduled Tribes and improving administration in Scheduled Areas, in accordance with the constitutional provision.

The Finance Commission recommends the principles governing grants-in-aid under Article 280.

Article 282: Discretionary grants

The Union or a State may make grants for any public purpose, even where the purpose is not one with respect to which the legislature of that government may make laws.

Article 282 therefore provides a broad constitutional basis for grants beyond the specific statutory grants contemplated by Article 275.

10. Consolidated Funds, Contingency Funds and Public Accounts

The Constitution establishes financial accounts and procedures to ensure legislative control and accountability over public money.

Article 266: Consolidated Funds and Public Accounts

  • The Consolidated Fund of India receives revenues received by the Union, loans raised by it and repayments of loans.
  • Each State has its own Consolidated Fund, subject to the corresponding constitutional framework.
  • Other public money received by or on behalf of the Union or a State is credited to the relevant Public Account.
  • Money cannot be withdrawn from a Consolidated Fund except in accordance with the Constitution and law.

Article 267: Contingency Fund

Parliament and State Legislatures may establish Contingency Funds by law. These funds are placed at the disposal of the President or Governor, as applicable, to enable advances for unforeseen expenditure pending legislative authorisation.

Article 283: Custody of funds

Parliament and State Legislatures may regulate by law the custody of public funds, payment of money into and withdrawal from those funds, and related matters.

Article 284: Other public moneys

Article 284 addresses the custody of specified public moneys received by public servants and courts, other than revenues or public moneys covered by the Consolidated Fund or Public Account framework.

11. Borrowing Powers: Articles 292 and 293

Article 292: Borrowing by the Union

The Union may borrow upon the security of the Consolidated Fund of India within limits, if any, fixed by Parliament by law.

It may also give guarantees within limits fixed by Parliament.

Article 293: Borrowing by States

  • A State may borrow within India upon the security of its Consolidated Fund, subject to limits fixed by the State Legislature by law.
  • A State may not raise a loan outside India.
  • If a State is indebted to the Union, or if a loan guaranteed by the Union remains outstanding, the State must obtain the consent of the Government of India before raising a loan where the constitutional conditions apply.
  • The Union may impose conditions while giving consent to such borrowing.

Federal significance

Article 293 creates a relationship between State borrowing autonomy and the Union's role as a creditor or guarantor. It is particularly relevant when States have outstanding liabilities to the Union.

12. Tax Immunities: Articles 285 and 289

Article 285: Exemption of Union property from State taxation

The property of the Union is generally exempt from taxation imposed by a State or by an authority within a State, except as Parliament may otherwise provide.

Article 289: Exemption of State property and income from Union taxation

The property and income of a State are generally exempt from Union taxation.

The Constitution contains qualifications concerning trade or business carried on by or on behalf of a State and certain related matters. Parliament may also declare particular activities to be incidental to the ordinary functions of government.

Purpose

These provisions protect the financial and functional autonomy of governments by limiting the power of one level of government to tax the property and income of another.

13. State Finance Commissions and Local Government Finance

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments strengthened local self-government and created a constitutional framework for fiscal decentralisation.

Article 243-I: State Finance Commission for Panchayats

The Governor must constitute a Finance Commission every five years to review the financial position of Panchayats and make recommendations.

Article 243-Y: Finance Commission for Municipalities

The Finance Commission constituted under Article 243-I also reviews the financial position of Municipalities and makes recommendations concerning their resources.

Functions and recommendations

  • Distribution between the State and local bodies of the net proceeds of taxes, duties, tolls and fees leviable by the State.
  • Allocation of shares among Panchayats and Municipalities at different levels.
  • Assignment of taxes, duties, tolls and fees to local bodies.
  • Grants-in-aid to local bodies from the State's Consolidated Fund.
  • Measures to improve the financial position of local governments.

Link with the Union Finance Commission

Under Article 280, the Union Finance Commission recommends measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities, based on the recommendations of the State Finance Commission.

Challenges

  • Delayed constitution or submission of State Finance Commission reports.
  • Weak own-source revenue mobilisation by local bodies.
  • Unpredictable or inadequate transfers.
  • Insufficient administrative capacity and financial expertise.
  • Limited devolution of functions, funds and functionaries.

14. Fiscal Federalism: Meaning and Significance

Fiscal federalism concerns the allocation of taxing, spending and borrowing responsibilities among different levels of government.

Vertical fiscal imbalance

Vertical fiscal imbalance arises when the revenue resources available to one level of government do not adequately match its expenditure responsibilities. In India, States carry substantial responsibility for public services while important revenue sources are concentrated at the Union level.

Horizontal fiscal imbalance

Horizontal fiscal imbalance refers to differences in revenue capacity, expenditure needs and development levels among States.

Why fiscal transfers are necessary

  • To correct vertical and horizontal imbalances.
  • To support minimum standards of public services.
  • To enable poorer States to meet essential expenditure.
  • To address regional inequalities.
  • To support national priorities and public goods.
  • To maintain macroeconomic and fiscal stability.

15. Cesses and Surcharges: Implications for Fiscal Federalism

Cesses and surcharges are important components of Union revenue. Their treatment under the constitutional framework has significant consequences for the divisible pool.

Surcharge

A surcharge is an additional charge imposed on a specified tax or duty. Article 271 provides Parliament with a constitutional power to impose surcharges for Union purposes, subject to its current scope and limitations.

Cess

A cess is a levy generally imposed for a specified purpose under the relevant constitutional and statutory authority. Its legal treatment depends on the particular levy.

Why States raise concerns

  • Cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool do not form part of the tax revenue shared with States through ordinary tax devolution.
  • A growing reliance on such levies can affect the effective share of States in Union tax collections.
  • States may have limited influence over the design and use of certain Union levies.

Reform considerations

  • Improve transparency about collections and utilisation.
  • Ensure levies are used consistently with their stated purposes.
  • Maintain a predictable and equitable tax-sharing framework.
  • Strengthen consultation with States on major fiscal changes.

17. Important Finance Commission Developments

Finance Commissions are constituted periodically. Their recommendations reflect the fiscal and developmental circumstances of their respective award periods.

Areas commonly examined

  • Vertical tax devolution.
  • Horizontal distribution among States.
  • Revenue deficit grants, where recommended.
  • Sector-specific and State-specific grants.
  • Disaster risk management financing.
  • Local government grants.
  • Fiscal consolidation and debt sustainability.

15th Finance Commission

The Fifteenth Finance Commission recommended a 41 per cent share of the divisible pool of Union taxes for States for the 2021–26 award period. The reduction from the previous 42 per cent recommendation reflected the change in the treatment of Jammu and Kashmir following its reorganisation into Union Territories.

Its recommendations also addressed local government grants, disaster management financing, health and other fiscal issues.

16th Finance Commission

The Sixteenth Finance Commission was constituted to make recommendations for the award period beginning 1 April 2026. Its report and the government's action on its recommendations should be consulted for the current award-period details.

Do not assume that the criteria, weights or recommendations of one Finance Commission automatically continue into the next award period.

18. Major Challenges in Union-State Financial Relations

1. Vertical fiscal imbalance

States often have significant expenditure responsibilities in areas such as health, education, agriculture, policing and local development, while many high-yield tax bases are assigned to the Union.

2. Horizontal disparities

Differences in economic development, geography, population, revenue capacity and service-delivery costs create unequal fiscal capacities among States.

3. Dependence on transfers

Heavy dependence on Union transfers can reduce the predictability of State budgets and may constrain independent expenditure choices.

4. Cesses and surcharges

Increased reliance on cesses and surcharges can reduce the relative size of the divisible pool and create concerns about the effective fiscal space available to States.

5. GST-related concerns

  • Concerns about the autonomy of States over indirect taxation.
  • Differences over GST rates and exemptions.
  • Issues relating to compensation and revenue predictability.
  • Complexity of compliance and input tax credit administration.
  • Disagreements over the interpretation of GST Council recommendations.

6. Conditional grants

Centrally sponsored schemes and conditional transfers may impose implementation requirements that do not always match State-specific priorities or capacities.

7. State debt and fiscal sustainability

Rising debt, guarantees, off-budget liabilities and interest expenditure can constrain development spending and create wider macroeconomic risks.

8. Weak local government finances

Inadequate devolution of funds and limited own-source revenue constrain the ability of Panchayats and Municipalities to deliver local public services.

19. Reforms and the Way Forward

  1. Strengthen the Finance Commission process: Maintain transparent criteria, reliable data and predictable fiscal transfers.
  2. Improve GST Council functioning: Encourage timely meetings, evidence-based decisions, consultation and transparent follow-up.
  3. Enhance State revenue capacity: Improve tax administration, compliance, property taxation and non-tax revenue mobilisation.
  4. Review cesses and surcharges: Promote transparency, accountability and a fair balance between Union fiscal requirements and State revenue interests.
  5. Rationalise conditional transfers: Provide States with adequate flexibility to adapt national programmes to local needs.
  6. Improve fiscal transparency: Disclose guarantees, off-budget borrowings and contingent liabilities comprehensively.
  7. Strengthen local fiscal autonomy: Ensure timely State Finance Commissions, predictable transfers and meaningful devolution of functions, funds and functionaries.
  8. Promote cooperative fiscal federalism: Treat the Union and States as constitutional partners in managing public finances.

22. Integrated Revision Framework

Financial relations can be understood through six connected dimensions:

  1. Tax assignment: The Seventh Schedule and Article 246 establish the general legislative distribution, while Article 246A creates the GST framework.
  2. Tax collection and sharing: Articles 268–271 and 279 govern the assignment, distribution and calculation of tax proceeds.
  3. Institutional coordination: Article 279A establishes the GST Council.
  4. Fiscal transfers: Articles 275, 280, 281 and 282 govern grants and the Finance Commission framework.
  5. Financial accountability: Articles 265–267 and 283–284 regulate taxation, public funds and expenditure procedures.
  6. Fiscal autonomy and constraints: Articles 285, 289, 292 and 293 address tax immunities and borrowing powers.

The central constitutional challenge is to reconcile adequate State fiscal autonomy with national redistribution, macroeconomic stability and equitable public service delivery.
