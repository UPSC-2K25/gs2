1. Strategic Significance

India-China relations are among the most consequential bilateral relationships in Asia. The two countries are major civilisational States, nuclear-armed neighbours, large developing economies and influential participants in global governance. Their relationship combines economic interdependence, diplomatic engagement, unresolved territorial disputes and strategic competition.

Stability in the relationship is important for Asian security, regional connectivity, global economic governance and India's development. At the same time, differences over the boundary, military deployments, regional influence and economic dependencies create persistent tensions.

2. Historical Background

  • 1950: India recognised the People's Republic of China.
  • 1954: The two countries signed an agreement on trade and intercourse between the Tibet region of China and India, associated with the Panchsheel principles.
  • 1962: A border war caused a major breakdown in trust and shaped India's security outlook.
  • 1967: Armed clashes occurred at Nathu La and Cho La.
  • 1976: Ambassador-level diplomatic relations were restored.
  • 1988: Prime Minister Rajiv Gandhi's visit initiated a process of normalisation and institutional dialogue.
  • 1993: The Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity along the Line of Actual Control established an important framework for border management.
  • 1996: An agreement on confidence-building measures in the military field along the LAC was concluded.
  • 2005: The two countries agreed on political parameters and guiding principles for settlement of the boundary question.
  • 2017: The Doklam standoff highlighted the strategic sensitivity of the India-Bhutan-China tri-junction area.
  • 2020: The Galwan Valley clash and wider eastern Ladakh tensions caused a serious deterioration in relations.

The historical trajectory demonstrates that economic and diplomatic engagement cannot by itself resolve a fundamental security dispute.

3. The Boundary Dispute

The India-China boundary has not been formally demarcated. The two sides have differing perceptions of parts of the Line of Actual Control, creating the possibility of patrol confrontations and escalation.

Western sector

The western sector includes Aksai Chin, which is administered by China and claimed by India as part of the Union Territory of Ladakh. The region is strategically important because of its location and the road link connecting Xinjiang and Tibet.

Middle sector

The middle sector covers areas along the boundaries of Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand. It has generally experienced fewer serious confrontations than the western and eastern sectors.

Eastern sector

The eastern sector includes Arunachal Pradesh, which India administers as a State and China claims in substantial part. Tawang is particularly sensitive because of its strategic, cultural and religious significance.

Line of Actual Control

The LAC is the practical military control line pending a final boundary settlement. It is not a mutually agreed, jointly demarcated international boundary. Differing perceptions of its alignment complicate patrols and border management.

4. Border Agreements and Confidence-building Mechanisms

  • 1993 Agreement: Agreement on the Maintenance of Peace and Tranquillity along the LAC in the India-China Border Areas.
  • 1996 Agreement: Confidence-building measures in the military field along the LAC, including provisions concerning military activities and restraint.
  • 2005 Agreement: Political parameters and guiding principles for settlement of the boundary question.
  • 2012 Working Mechanism: Consultation and coordination on India-China border affairs.
  • 2013 Border Defence Cooperation Agreement: Measures intended to strengthen communication and manage situations along the border.

These arrangements seek to prevent incidents from escalating. They do not themselves settle the boundary dispute. Their effectiveness depends on implementation, communication, restraint and mutual compliance.

5. Galwan and the Post-2020 Security Environment

The 2020 eastern Ladakh crisis, including the violent clash in the Galwan Valley, fundamentally affected bilateral trust. Military deployments, infrastructure development and patrol arrangements became central issues in the relationship.

The crisis demonstrated several vulnerabilities:

  • Unresolved LAC perceptions can create recurring face-offs.
  • Infrastructure development on both sides changes the operational environment.
  • Localised incidents can rapidly acquire national and strategic significance.
  • Economic interdependence does not necessarily prevent military confrontation.
  • Border stability is a prerequisite for sustained political normalisation.

Disengagement at particular friction points, de-escalation of forces and broader border normalisation are distinct processes. Progress in one area should not automatically be treated as resolution of the wider boundary dispute.

6. Tibet and the Strategic Dimension

Tibet is central to the geography and security dimensions of India-China relations. The Tibetan plateau is a major source region for several transboundary rivers and has strategic importance for military deployments and connectivity.

  • India hosts the Dalai Lama and a Tibetan refugee community.
  • China regards Tibet as an integral part of its territory and is sensitive to external political activity concerning Tibet.
  • Developments in Tibet affect the Himalayan security environment and India's border concerns.
  • Water-related developments on rivers originating in the Tibetan plateau have implications for downstream countries.

India must balance its humanitarian and cultural connections with Tibetans with the management of its broader diplomatic relationship with China.

7. Economic Relations and Trade Imbalance

China is an important source of manufactured goods, machinery, electronics, chemicals, pharmaceutical inputs and industrial components for India. Bilateral trade has expanded, but it is characterised by a substantial Indian trade deficit and dependence in certain supply chains.

Areas of economic interdependence

  • Electronics and electrical equipment.
  • Pharmaceutical active ingredients and intermediates.
  • Machinery and industrial capital goods.
  • Chemicals, solar equipment and other intermediate inputs.

Concerns

  • Concentration of imports in critical sectors.
  • Trade deficit and limited export diversification.
  • Risks arising from supply-chain disruptions.
  • Technology, data security and investment-screening concerns.
  • Difficulty of replacing low-cost inputs in the short term.

Economic resilience requires diversification, domestic manufacturing capacity, competitive exports and carefully designed screening of investments in sensitive sectors. Broad restrictions that raise costs for domestic producers may also create unintended consequences.

8. Regional and Global Strategic Competition

  • South Asia: China's infrastructure, defence and diplomatic engagement with India's neighbours affects the regional strategic balance.
  • Indian Ocean: China's growing maritime presence and relationships with littoral States have implications for India's maritime security.
  • Indo-Pacific: Different approaches to maritime order, regional institutions and security partnerships create strategic competition.
  • Technology: Competition involves telecommunications, semiconductors, artificial intelligence, cyber security and critical infrastructure.
  • Global governance: Both countries seek influence in multilateral institutions, although they sometimes cooperate on issues involving developing countries.

9. Areas of Cooperation

Despite strategic differences, India and China have shared interests in several areas:

  • Climate change negotiations and sustainable development.
  • Multilateral trade and development discussions.
  • Reform of international financial governance in selected areas.
  • Combating piracy and protecting maritime commerce where interests converge.
  • People-to-people, academic and cultural exchanges, subject to prevailing conditions.
  • Dialogue on regional and global issues through bilateral and multilateral channels.

Cooperation should be pragmatic and based on clearly defined mutual interests rather than assumptions that all disagreements can be compartmentalised.

10. India's Policy Options

  • Maintain deterrence: Improve border infrastructure, surveillance, logistics and military preparedness.
  • Strengthen diplomacy: Maintain military and diplomatic communication channels to prevent miscalculation.
  • Insist on border stability: Link the broader development of bilateral relations to peace and tranquillity along the border.
  • Reduce critical dependencies: Diversify suppliers and strengthen domestic industrial and technological capabilities.
  • Deepen regional partnerships: Build credible economic, development and security cooperation with neighbouring countries.
  • Strengthen maritime capacity: Improve domain awareness, naval capabilities and cooperation with Indian Ocean partners.
  • Use multilateral platforms: Engage China where interests converge while protecting India's independent positions.
  • Improve strategic assessment: Invest in language expertise, intelligence analysis, economic research and regional knowledge.

11. Challenges and Way Forward

The central challenge is to manage competition without allowing it to become uncontrolled confrontation. India must protect territorial integrity while preserving diplomatic options and economic development opportunities.

  1. Ensure effective implementation of border agreements and communication mechanisms.
  2. Work towards clarification of differing LAC perceptions where feasible.
  3. Maintain credible military preparedness and infrastructure in border areas.
  4. Build economic resilience without creating unnecessary disruption to domestic industry.
  5. Strengthen partnerships in the Indo-Pacific and with regional neighbours.
  6. Maintain dialogue on transboundary rivers, trade, climate and global governance.
  7. Develop a long-term strategy that combines deterrence, diplomacy and domestic capability.
