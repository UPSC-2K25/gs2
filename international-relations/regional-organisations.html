Regional Organisations and India's Regional Engagement

1. Meaning, Evolution and Importance of Regionalism

Regionalism refers to structured cooperation among countries connected by geography, economic interdependence, shared security concerns, historical links or common political interests. It may range from informal diplomatic consultation to highly integrated institutions with common rules and supranational bodies.

Regional organisations have become important because many contemporary challenges cannot be addressed effectively by individual states acting alone. Cross-border trade, migration, terrorism, maritime security, climate change, pandemics, energy networks and infrastructure corridors require coordination among neighbouring and connected countries.

Major objectives

  • Peace and security: Prevent conflict, build confidence, promote dialogue and coordinate responses to transnational threats.
  • Economic integration: Reduce trade barriers, improve market access, attract investment and connect production networks.
  • Connectivity: Develop road, rail, port, inland-waterway, energy and digital links.
  • Collective bargaining: Strengthen the negotiating position of member states in global institutions.
  • Human development: Cooperate in health, education, food security, disaster management and environmental protection.
  • Regional identity: Build habits of cooperation and a shared sense of regional responsibility.

Why regionalism matters for India

  • India's immediate neighbourhood affects its internal security, border management, migration, trade and development.
  • India's location gives it a central role in South Asian, Bay of Bengal and Indian Ocean cooperation.
  • Regional markets can support exports, tourism, investment, energy security and employment.
  • Connectivity with Southeast Asia and Central Asia can reduce geographic disadvantages and diversify trade routes.
  • Regional institutions provide platforms to manage strategic competition and promote an inclusive Indo-Pacific.

2. Types of Regional Arrangements: Important Distinctions

Not every grouping is an international organisation or a trade bloc. Their legal status, institutional structure and commitments differ substantially. This distinction is important for both factual questions and analytical answers.

  • Treaty-based regional organisation: Established through a constitutive treaty or charter, with defined membership and institutional bodies. Examples include ASEAN, the EU and the SCO.
  • Intergovernmental association: A platform for cooperation in which states retain control over decisions. SAARC and BIMSTEC are examples.
  • Dialogue forum: Provides consultation and strategic discussion without necessarily creating binding obligations. The East Asia Summit and ASEAN Regional Forum are examples.
  • Subregional cooperation mechanism: Brings together a smaller group of geographically connected states for practical projects. BBIN is an example.
  • Free trade agreement: Reduces or removes specified trade barriers among participating economies. SAFTA and the ASEAN Free Trade Area are examples.
  • Customs union: Combines internal free trade with a common external tariff. The EU customs union is an example.
  • Common market: Seeks freer movement of goods, services, capital and, in some cases, labour.
  • Security alliance: Establishes defence or security commitments among members. It is distinct from a broad diplomatic forum.
  • Connectivity initiative: Coordinates infrastructure or transport corridors but may not create a permanent regional organisation. BBIN transport cooperation and the India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway illustrate project-based connectivity.

How to assess an organisation

Study its founding instrument, membership, objectives, decision-making procedure, institutional bodies, funding, implementation record, dispute-settlement arrangements and actual impact. A summit declaration does not automatically create a binding treaty obligation. Likewise, a free trade agreement does not necessarily create a customs union or common market.

3. South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)

SAARC was established on 8 December 1985 through the Dhaka Declaration. It was created to promote the welfare of South Asian populations, accelerate economic and social development and strengthen regional cooperation. Its Secretariat is located in Kathmandu, Nepal.

Membership

Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka.

Objectives and areas of cooperation

  • Improve the quality of life and welfare of the people of South Asia.
  • Promote economic growth, social progress and cultural development.
  • Strengthen collective self-reliance and mutual assistance.
  • Cooperate in agriculture, rural development, health, education, science and technology.
  • Promote people-to-people contact and regional understanding.

Institutional structure and operating principles

  • Summit: The highest political-level forum, comprising heads of state or government.
  • Council of Ministers: Foreign ministers responsible for policy formulation and review.
  • Standing Committee: Foreign secretaries coordinate and monitor cooperation.
  • Technical Committees: Support sector-specific cooperation.
  • Secretariat: Located in Kathmandu and headed by a Secretary-General.
  • Unanimity: Decisions on substantive matters are taken unanimously.
  • Bilateral and contentious issues: Excluded from formal deliberations under the SAARC Charter.

South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA)

SAFTA was signed in 2004 and entered into force in 2006. It seeks to promote intra-regional trade by reducing tariffs according to agreed schedules. Its framework includes tariff liberalisation, rules of origin, sensitive lists and institutional arrangements for implementation.

Rules of origin determine whether a product qualifies for preferential tariff treatment. Sensitive lists identify products for which members retain specified protection. These provisions are intended to balance trade liberalisation with domestic economic concerns, but extensive exclusions and non-tariff barriers can limit the benefits of an FTA.

Other important initiatives

  • SAARC Development Fund: Supports regional projects in social, economic and infrastructure-related areas.
  • South Asian University: An institution intended to promote regional higher education and academic cooperation.
  • SAARC Food Bank: Aims to support regional food security through a shared food reserve arrangement.
  • SAARC Disaster Management Centre: Supports knowledge-sharing and regional disaster-risk cooperation through evolving institutional arrangements.

Achievements

  • Created a formal institutional framework for South Asian cooperation.
  • Established trade, education, food security and development cooperation mechanisms.
  • Promoted dialogue among governments and civil society institutions.
  • Recognised the shared developmental challenges of South Asia.

Why SAARC has underperformed

  • Political tensions: India-Pakistan relations have repeatedly obstructed regional engagement.
  • Unanimity: Any substantive disagreement can prevent collective decisions.
  • Low economic integration: Intra-regional trade remains constrained by tariffs, sensitive lists, non-tariff barriers and poor logistics.
  • Connectivity deficits: Border infrastructure, transit restrictions and limited transport links raise transaction costs.
  • Implementation gap: Agreements and declarations are not always translated into measurable outcomes.
  • Asymmetry: Differences in economic size and capacity create concerns about unequal gains.

India's approach

India has supported regional cooperation while increasingly emphasising bilateral and subregional initiatives where practical progress is possible. The challenge is to avoid allowing political deadlock to permanently undermine cooperation in health, climate, disaster response, trade and people-to-people relations.

4. BIMSTEC: Bay of Bengal Regional Cooperation

The Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation (BIMSTEC) was established on 6 June 1997 through the Bangkok Declaration. It links South Asia with Southeast Asia and provides a framework for cooperation among countries around the Bay of Bengal.

Membership

Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Thailand.

Institutional development

  • It began as BIST-EC, involving Bangladesh, India, Sri Lanka and Thailand.
  • Myanmar joined in 1997, and Nepal and Bhutan joined in 2004.
  • The organisation adopted a Charter, which entered into force in 2024.
  • Its Permanent Secretariat is located in Dhaka, Bangladesh.
  • Its institutional framework includes a Summit, Ministerial Meeting, Senior Officials' Meeting and sectoral mechanisms.

Priority areas

BIMSTEC reorganised its cooperation into seven broad sectors, each led by a member state:

  • Trade, Investment and Development: Bangladesh.
  • Environment and Climate Change: Bhutan.
  • Security: India.
  • Agriculture and Food Security: Myanmar.
  • People-to-People Contact: Nepal.
  • Science, Technology and Innovation: Sri Lanka.
  • Connectivity: Thailand.

Importance for India

  • Connects India's eastern and north-eastern regions with Southeast Asia.
  • Supports India's Act East Policy and Neighbourhood First approach.
  • Provides a platform for maritime security and Bay of Bengal cooperation.
  • Offers opportunities for energy trade, port development and regional supply chains.
  • Allows India to cooperate with neighbouring countries without requiring the participation of every SAARC member.

Economic and connectivity significance

The Bay of Bengal is a major maritime space connecting South Asia, Southeast Asia and the wider Indo-Pacific. Better port links, customs coordination, coastal shipping, road and rail networks can lower logistics costs and support regional value chains.

The BIMSTEC Master Plan for Transport Connectivity provides a framework for improving physical, institutional and people-to-people links. Its effectiveness depends on project financing, coordinated standards, customs modernisation, cross-border procedures and timely implementation.

Challenges

  • Political instability and security challenges, particularly in Myanmar.
  • Slow implementation of connectivity projects.
  • Unequal infrastructure and administrative capacity.
  • Limited intra-regional trade and regulatory differences.
  • Need for stronger institutional resources and project monitoring.

Way forward

Prioritise a limited number of bankable cross-border projects, improve customs and transit arrangements, strengthen coastal shipping, develop energy interconnections and establish measurable implementation targets. BIMSTEC should complement rather than simply replace wider South Asian cooperation.

5. BBIN: Practical Subregional Cooperation

The Bangladesh-Bhutan-India-Nepal (BBIN) initiative is a subregional cooperation framework focused on connectivity, transport, energy and trade facilitation. It is not a conventional regional organisation with the same institutional structure as ASEAN or the EU.

BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement

The BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement was signed in 2015. It seeks to facilitate the movement of passenger, personal and cargo vehicles across participating countries through agreed protocols and implementation arrangements.

Bhutan has faced domestic constraints in ratifying the agreement in its original form. The other participating countries have pursued practical arrangements and bilateral or trilateral implementation where feasible. The distinction between signing an agreement and bringing it into operational effect is important.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Cross-border passenger and freight movement.
  • Road corridors, border facilities and integrated check posts.
  • Cross-border electricity trade and hydropower cooperation.
  • Tourism and people-to-people movement.
  • Transit arrangements and regional logistics.
  • Cooperation in water resources and environmental management.

Importance for India

  • Improves access between mainland India and the North-Eastern Region.
  • Reduces transport costs and transit time for regional trade.
  • Supports energy trade, particularly hydropower imports and electricity exports.
  • Creates practical cooperation even when wider regional arrangements are stalled.

Challenges

  • Different vehicle, customs and transit regulations.
  • Border infrastructure and procedural bottlenecks.
  • Environmental and social concerns surrounding infrastructure projects.
  • Political and domestic constraints on implementation.

6. Indian Ocean Rim Association (IORA)

IORA was established in 1997 to promote regional cooperation and sustainable development among countries bordering the Indian Ocean. Its Secretariat is located in Mauritius. It provides a broad platform for economic cooperation, maritime security and ocean governance.

Core areas of cooperation

  • Maritime safety and security.
  • Trade and investment facilitation.
  • Fisheries management and the blue economy.
  • Disaster risk management.
  • Academic, scientific and technological cooperation.
  • Tourism and cultural exchange.
  • Women's economic empowerment.

Maritime security and the blue economy

The Indian Ocean carries a substantial share of global trade and energy shipments. Its security is affected by piracy, trafficking, illegal fishing, maritime terrorism, natural disasters and strategic competition.

The blue economy refers to sustainable economic activity linked to oceans, seas and coastal resources. It includes fisheries, aquaculture, ports, shipping, marine biotechnology, coastal tourism and offshore renewable energy. Sustainable ocean governance requires balancing economic use with marine conservation and the rights of coastal communities.

Importance for India

  • Supports India's maritime outlook and SAGAR—Security and Growth for All in the Region.
  • Strengthens engagement with African, Gulf and island countries.
  • Provides a platform for maritime domain awareness and cooperation against non-traditional threats.
  • Supports trade, investment, fisheries and sustainable ocean-based development.
  • Complements India's naval diplomacy and capacity-building partnerships.

Challenges and way forward

  • Large geographic coverage and diverse national capacities.
  • Limited implementation resources and uneven institutional engagement.
  • Strategic competition and differing maritime priorities.
  • Need for stronger cooperation on marine pollution, illegal fishing and climate resilience.

IORA can become more effective by focusing on implementable projects, shared maritime information, sustainable fisheries, port resilience and disaster response rather than relying only on broad declarations.

7. ASEAN: The Central Regional Institution of Southeast Asia

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN) was established on 8 August 1967 through the Bangkok Declaration. Its founding members were Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Singapore and Thailand. It has evolved into the principal institution for Southeast Asian regional cooperation.

Membership

ASEAN has 11 members: Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, Indonesia, Lao PDR, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam and Timor-Leste. Timor-Leste became the 11th member on 26 October 2025.

ASEAN Charter and institutional structure

  • ASEAN Summit: The supreme policy-making body, comprising heads of state or government.
  • ASEAN Coordinating Council: Comprises foreign ministers and coordinates implementation of summit decisions.
  • Community Councils: Coordinate the three ASEAN Community pillars.
  • Sectoral Ministerial Bodies: Support cooperation in specialised fields.
  • Committee of Permanent Representatives: Supports coordination and the work of the Secretariat.
  • ASEAN Secretariat: Based in Jakarta, Indonesia, and headed by the Secretary-General.

Three pillars of the ASEAN Community

  • ASEAN Political-Security Community (APSC): Promotes peace, regional stability, political dialogue, confidence-building and cooperation on security challenges.
  • ASEAN Economic Community (AEC): Seeks a more integrated and connected regional economy through trade, investment, services, skilled mobility and production networks.
  • ASEAN Socio-Cultural Community (ASCC): Promotes cooperation in education, health, environment, social protection, culture and people-to-people relations.

ASEAN's operating principles

  • Respect for sovereignty, independence and territorial integrity.
  • Non-interference in the internal affairs of member states as a traditional principle.
  • Consensus-based decision-making.
  • Peaceful settlement of disputes.
  • Consultation and gradual institution-building.
  • ASEAN centrality in the wider regional architecture.

ASEAN Way

The ASEAN Way refers to a style of regional cooperation based on consultation, consensus, informality, gradualism and sensitivity to national sovereignty. It helps states with different political systems and levels of development cooperate without requiring extensive surrender of national authority.

However, consensus and non-interference can also constrain collective action when member states disagree on internal political crises, human rights or regional security. The approach therefore creates both flexibility and limitations.

ASEAN economic integration

The ASEAN Economic Community seeks to develop a more integrated regional production and trading space. It is not a fully unified single market comparable to the European Union. Members retain important national regulatory and economic powers.

  • ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA): Established to reduce tariffs and promote trade among ASEAN members.
  • ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement (ATIGA): Consolidates commitments concerning trade in goods, tariff reduction, rules of origin and trade facilitation.
  • ASEAN Framework Agreement on Services: Supports progressive liberalisation of services trade.
  • ASEAN Comprehensive Investment Agreement: Provides a framework for investment liberalisation, protection, facilitation and promotion.
  • ASEAN Single Window: Supports electronic exchange of trade documents and customs information to reduce clearance delays.
  • Mutual Recognition Arrangements: Facilitate recognition of specified professional qualifications, subject to national regulations.

Why ASEAN matters economically

  • It is an important manufacturing and export hub in electronics, automobiles, textiles, machinery and other sectors.
  • Its economies are integrated into global value chains and regional production networks.
  • Its location connects the Indian and Pacific Oceans through major maritime routes and straits.
  • Its trade agreements link Southeast Asia with major global markets.
  • Its economic integration offers opportunities for Indian exporters, service providers and investors.

India-ASEAN relations

India began formal sectoral engagement with ASEAN in 1992, became a dialogue partner in 1995 and developed the relationship into a strategic partnership. India and ASEAN have cooperation arrangements covering political dialogue, trade, investment, maritime security, education, culture, connectivity and digital technologies.

  • Act East Policy: ASEAN is a central pillar of India's engagement with Southeast Asia and the Indo-Pacific.
  • India-ASEAN Trade in Goods Agreement: Entered into force in 2010 and provides preferential tariff treatment for goods, subject to rules of origin and other conditions.
  • Services and Investment Agreements: Provide additional frameworks for economic engagement.
  • Connectivity: India supports road, port, maritime and digital connectivity, including projects linking India's North-East with Southeast Asia.
  • Maritime cooperation: Includes exercises, capacity-building, maritime domain awareness and cooperation against transnational threats.

India-ASEAN trade: Opportunities and concerns

Trade agreements can expand market access, but the actual gains depend on competitiveness, logistics, product standards and effective use of rules of origin.

  • India can benefit from access to regional markets for pharmaceuticals, engineering goods, chemicals, agricultural products and services.
  • Indian firms can participate in regional manufacturing and supply chains.
  • Trade deficits in selected sectors and concerns about import surges have generated debate over the utilisation and review of the goods agreement.
  • Non-tariff measures, standards, certification and customs procedures can restrict trade even when tariffs are low.
  • India needs stronger export competitiveness, trade facilitation and integration into regional value chains.

Challenges facing ASEAN

  • South China Sea disputes and differences among member states.
  • Major-power competition and pressure to choose strategic alignments.
  • Political instability in Myanmar and disagreement over collective responses.
  • Development gaps among members, including the integration needs of newer members.
  • Non-tariff barriers and uneven implementation of economic commitments.
  • Need to balance regional consensus with timely and effective action.

8. ASEAN-Led Regional Forums

ASEAN has developed a wider network of dialogue mechanisms that connects Southeast Asia with major Indo-Pacific powers. These mechanisms are important because they provide inclusive platforms for strategic discussion even when members have competing interests.

ASEAN Regional Forum (ARF)

  • Established in 1994 as a platform for political and security dialogue in the Asia-Pacific and wider Indo-Pacific.
  • Brings together ASEAN states and external partners, including India, China, Japan, the United States, Russia, Australia and the European Union.
  • Focuses on confidence-building, preventive diplomacy and discussion of regional security issues.
  • Uses consensus and dialogue rather than collective defence obligations.

India's interest: The ARF provides a platform to discuss maritime security, terrorism, nuclear issues, cyber threats and regional stability with a broad range of partners.

East Asia Summit (EAS)

  • Established in 2005 as a leaders-led forum for strategic dialogue.
  • Includes the 11 ASEAN members and Australia, China, India, Japan, New Zealand, South Korea, Russia and the United States.
  • Discusses strategic, political, security and economic issues, including maritime cooperation, connectivity, energy and regional stability.
  • ASEAN plays the central convening role.

The EAS is important to India because it provides a high-level platform for engagement with major Indo-Pacific powers while supporting an ASEAN-centred regional order.

ASEAN Plus Three

ASEAN Plus Three brings together ASEAN and China, Japan and South Korea. It emerged from the regional response to the 1997 Asian financial crisis and has developed cooperation in finance, trade, food security, public health and other areas.

Its economic importance includes regional financial cooperation, supply-chain connectivity and mechanisms to strengthen resilience against financial instability.

ASEAN Defence Ministers' Meeting-Plus (ADMM-Plus)

ADMM-Plus connects ASEAN defence ministers with eight dialogue partners: Australia, China, India, Japan, New Zealand, South Korea, Russia and the United States. It promotes practical defence cooperation in areas such as maritime security, humanitarian assistance, disaster relief, counter-terrorism, peacekeeping and military medicine.

Distinguishing the forums

  • ARF: Broad political and security dialogue, including confidence-building and preventive diplomacy.
  • EAS: Leaders-level strategic and economic dialogue.
  • ASEAN Plus Three: ASEAN's cooperation with China, Japan and South Korea, with significant economic and financial dimensions.
  • ADMM-Plus: Practical defence and military cooperation.

9. Regional Trade Architecture: RCEP and Other Frameworks

Regional trade agreements can reduce tariffs, establish common rules and facilitate cross-border production. They can also create trade diversion, regulatory adjustment costs and competitive pressures on domestic industries.

Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP)

RCEP is a free trade agreement involving the ten ASEAN members and five partners: Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand and South Korea. It was signed in 2020 and entered into force in 2022 for its initial participating parties. It is one of the major trade frameworks in the Asia-Pacific.

Major features

  • Tariff reduction and market-access commitments for goods.
  • Rules of origin that can allow qualifying inputs from different member economies to be counted within regional production.
  • Commitments on services, investment and selected regulatory matters.
  • Trade facilitation and customs cooperation.
  • Frameworks addressing intellectual property, electronic commerce and other trade-related areas.

Why India did not join

India participated in negotiations but announced its decision not to join RCEP in 2019. Its concerns included the potential for import surges, trade deficits, inadequate safeguards, rules of origin and the impact on vulnerable domestic sectors.

India's decision reflects the challenge of balancing integration with domestic industrial development. Remaining outside may preserve policy space but can also limit preferential access to regional markets and reduce participation in regional production networks.

Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP)

The CPTPP is a high-standard free trade agreement that developed from the Trans-Pacific Partnership after the United States withdrew. It includes provisions on goods, services, investment, intellectual property, digital trade, labour, environment and state-owned enterprises. The United Kingdom joined in 2024.

India is not a member. The agreement is relevant because it illustrates how newer trade arrangements increasingly address regulatory and digital issues beyond conventional tariff reduction.

European Union and regional integration

The European Union represents a much deeper form of regional integration than most Asian arrangements. It combines a customs union and single market with common institutions and shared rules in several policy areas. Some members also participate in monetary union.

  • Customs union: Removes internal customs duties and applies a common external tariff to goods entering the union.
  • Single market: Seeks free movement of goods, services, capital and persons, subject to the applicable legal framework.
  • Common institutions: Include the European Commission, European Parliament, Council of the European Union and Court of Justice of the European Union.

The EU offers lessons on regulatory harmonisation, regional institutions and dispute settlement, but its model cannot be directly replicated in South Asia because of differences in political history, economic structure and sovereignty preferences.

India's trade policy considerations

  • Evaluate market access alongside the adjustment costs faced by domestic producers.
  • Use clear and enforceable rules of origin to prevent trade circumvention.
  • Address non-tariff barriers, standards, testing and certification.
  • Improve logistics, ports, customs and digital documentation.
  • Support small businesses in meeting international quality and compliance requirements.
  • Integrate Indian firms into regional and global value chains.

10. Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation was established on 15 June 2001. It evolved from the Shanghai Five mechanism, which initially focused on confidence-building and border-related issues among China, Russia and Central Asian states. The SCO has expanded into a broader organisation covering security, political dialogue and economic cooperation.

Membership and India's participation

India and Pakistan became full members in 2017. The organisation subsequently expanded further, including Iran and Belarus. Membership should be checked against the latest official list when a question requires an exact current count.

India's participation provides a platform for engagement with Central Asia, Russia, China and other Eurasian countries. India must balance the benefits of participation with its own security concerns and differences with other members.

Institutional structure

  • Council of Heads of State: The supreme decision-making body.
  • Council of Heads of Government: Deals with economic and other cooperation matters.
  • Council of Foreign Ministers: Coordinates foreign-policy consultation.
  • Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS): Supports cooperation against terrorism, separatism and extremism within the organisation's framework.
  • Secretariat: Located in Beijing, China.
  • Executive Committee of RATS: Located in Tashkent, Uzbekistan.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Counter-terrorism and regional security.
  • Border security and confidence-building.
  • Transport, logistics and connectivity.
  • Energy and economic cooperation.
  • Culture, education and people-to-people relations.

Importance for India

  • Provides institutional engagement with Central Asian states.
  • Supports discussion on terrorism, radicalisation and regional security.
  • Offers a platform for energy cooperation and economic engagement.
  • Allows India to participate in Eurasian diplomacy despite geopolitical differences.

Constraints and concerns

  • India's strategic and territorial differences with China and Pakistan.
  • Different national interpretations of security and regional connectivity.
  • Sanctions and geopolitical tensions affecting economic projects.
  • Limited direct land connectivity between India and Central Asia.
  • Risk that the organisation's security agenda may overshadow broader economic cooperation.

11. Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU)

The Eurasian Economic Union came into operation in 2015. Its members are Armenia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan and Russia. It seeks deeper economic integration among participating states through common rules and coordinated economic policies.

Objectives and mechanisms

  • Promote the free movement of goods, services, capital and labour within the union's framework.
  • Operate a customs union and common external tariff for goods covered by the relevant rules.
  • Coordinate selected economic, technical and regulatory policies.
  • Develop a common economic space and facilitate investment.

Relevance to India

  • Potential access to Eurasian markets and natural resources.
  • Opportunities in pharmaceuticals, engineering goods, agriculture, chemicals and services.
  • Scope for energy, industrial and transport cooperation.
  • Possibility of wider economic arrangements, subject to negotiations and commercial feasibility.

Any India-EAEU trade arrangement must account for tariff schedules, rules of origin, logistics, sanctions-related constraints, payment mechanisms and differences in regulatory standards.

12. Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)

The Gulf Cooperation Council was established in 1981 through its Charter. It brings together Bahrain, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. Its Secretariat is located in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.

Objectives and areas of cooperation

  • Coordination, integration and interconnection among member states.
  • Economic, financial, commercial and customs cooperation.
  • Energy and infrastructure cooperation.
  • Security coordination and regional consultation.
  • Cooperation in education, health, culture and social development.

Economic significance

The GCC is important in global energy markets because its members are major producers and exporters of oil and natural gas. It also has substantial sovereign investment capacity and growing interests in logistics, tourism, finance, technology and renewable energy.

Importance for India

  • Energy security through oil and gas imports.
  • Employment and welfare of Indian workers and professionals in Gulf countries.
  • Trade, investment, remittances and financial links.
  • Food security and agricultural supply-chain cooperation.
  • Maritime security and stability in the Arabian Sea and wider Indian Ocean.

Challenges

  • Regional rivalries and periodic diplomatic tensions.
  • Dependence on hydrocarbon exports and energy transition pressures.
  • Different national approaches to labour and economic diversification.
  • Need for stronger institutionalised economic cooperation with external partners.

13. African Union and Regional Integration in Africa

The African Union (AU) was launched in 2002 as the successor to the Organisation of African Unity. It brings together African states and seeks to promote continental unity, peace, security, development and stronger African participation in global affairs.

Major objectives and institutions

  • Promote unity, solidarity and political cooperation among African states.
  • Support peace, security and conflict prevention.
  • Advance economic integration and infrastructure connectivity.
  • Promote democracy, human rights and constitutional governance.
  • Coordinate continental development priorities through Agenda 2063.

Important initiatives

  • Agenda 2063: A long-term framework for inclusive growth, integration, peace and development in Africa.
  • African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA): Seeks to create a continent-wide market by reducing trade barriers and improving regional economic integration.
  • African Peace and Security Architecture: Supports conflict prevention, management and peace operations.

Importance for India

  • Trade and investment opportunities in energy, minerals, agriculture, pharmaceuticals, technology and infrastructure.
  • Development partnerships through capacity-building, training, concessional assistance and digital public infrastructure cooperation.
  • Cooperation in the Indian Ocean, maritime security and counter-piracy.
  • Support for reform of global institutions and greater representation of developing countries.
  • Partnership with African countries in multilateral forums, including the G20.

Challenges to African integration

  • Infrastructure deficits and high cross-border logistics costs.
  • Political instability, conflict and security challenges.
  • Dependence on commodity exports and vulnerability to external price shocks.
  • Different national tariff structures and regulatory systems.
  • Limited implementation capacity and uneven economic development.

14. European Union: A Distinctive Model of Regional Integration

The European Union is a unique regional political and economic union that developed through post-Second World War integration. It is more deeply institutionalised than most regional organisations and exercises shared or supranational authority in specified areas.

Historical evolution

  • European Coal and Steel Community (1951): Began sectoral economic integration in strategically important industries.
  • Treaties of Rome (1957): Established the European Economic Community and Euratom.
  • Single European Act (1986): Advanced the creation of the single market.
  • Maastricht Treaty (1992): Established the European Union and laid the foundations for economic and monetary union.
  • Lisbon Treaty (2007, in force 2009): Reformed the institutional and legal framework.

Key institutions

  • European Council: Provides overall political direction and priorities.
  • European Commission: Proposes legislation, implements policies and oversees compliance with EU law.
  • European Parliament: Directly elected institution that shares legislative and budgetary powers.
  • Council of the European Union: Represents member-state governments and shares legislative and budgetary functions.
  • Court of Justice of the European Union: Interprets EU law and ensures its uniform application.
  • European Central Bank: Manages monetary policy for the euro area.

Levels of economic integration

  • Free trade area: Internal tariffs are reduced or removed, but members retain separate external tariffs.
  • Customs union: Adds a common external tariff to internal free trade.
  • Common market: Seeks freer movement of goods, services, capital and persons.
  • Economic and monetary union: Includes coordination of economic policies and, for participating states, a common currency.

Relevance for India

  • The EU is an important trade and investment partner.
  • It is a major market for Indian goods and services.
  • Cooperation includes climate action, technology, research, maritime security and connectivity.
  • EU regulations and standards can influence Indian exporters, especially in sustainability, data, product safety and supply-chain due diligence.

Critical assessment

The EU demonstrates the benefits of institutionalised integration, common rules and dispute settlement. However, economic disparities, migration pressures, democratic accountability debates, enlargement questions and differences in national interests show that deeper integration requires sustained political consent.

15. Other Regional Organisations Relevant to India's Interests

Economic Cooperation Organisation (ECO)

ECO is a regional intergovernmental organisation whose members include Turkey, Iran, Pakistan and several Central Asian and neighbouring states. It seeks to promote economic cooperation, trade, transport, energy and regional connectivity. Its relevance to India is linked to wider Eurasian connectivity, although India's participation is not equivalent to membership.

Common Market for Eastern and Southern Africa (COMESA)

COMESA is an African regional economic community that promotes trade liberalisation, customs cooperation and economic integration among participating African states. It is relevant to India's engagement with African markets and regional value chains.

Southern African Development Community (SADC)

SADC promotes regional development, economic integration, peace and security in Southern Africa. It provides a framework for trade, infrastructure and sectoral cooperation among its members.

Pacific Islands Forum (PIF)

PIF is a regional political and economic organisation of Pacific island countries and Australia and New Zealand. It is important for climate diplomacy, ocean governance, sustainable development and the interests of small island developing states.

Organisation of American States (OAS)

The OAS is a regional organisation in the Americas that promotes democracy, human rights, security and development. It illustrates the role of regional institutions in political and normative cooperation beyond economic integration.

Why these organisations matter

India's diplomatic and economic engagement increasingly extends beyond its immediate neighbourhood. Understanding regional organisations in Africa, the Gulf, Eurasia and the Pacific helps explain India's approach to trade diversification, development partnerships, critical minerals, climate negotiations and reform of global governance.

16. Regional Connectivity and Economic Corridors

Regional integration depends not only on diplomatic institutions but also on physical infrastructure, interoperable regulations and predictable cross-border procedures. Transport corridors can reduce distance-related costs and connect production centres with markets.

India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway

The proposed highway is intended to connect India’s North-Eastern Region with Myanmar and Thailand. It is a major component of India's Act East connectivity agenda. Its progress is affected by difficult terrain, security conditions, construction delays and the need for complementary border and logistics infrastructure.

Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project

The Kaladan project is intended to connect India's eastern seaboard with the North-Eastern Region through maritime, inland-waterway and road links via Myanmar. It seeks to provide an alternative route to improve access to Mizoram and other parts of the North-East. Political instability and security conditions in Myanmar have affected implementation.

BBIN connectivity

BBIN transport and energy cooperation can improve access between India and Bangladesh, Bhutan and Nepal. Cross-border electricity trade, road connectivity and simplified customs procedures can support regional economic integration.

International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC)

INSTC is a multimodal transport network intended to connect India with Iran, the Caspian region, Russia and markets beyond. It uses combinations of sea, rail and road transport. Its potential benefits include shorter transit routes and trade diversification, while its development faces infrastructure, financing, customs and geopolitical constraints.

India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)

Announced through a memorandum of understanding at the 2023 G20 Summit, IMEC is a proposed connectivity initiative intended to link India, the Middle East and Europe through shipping, rail and associated infrastructure. It is not a functioning regional organisation or a conventional free trade agreement.

Its potential components include port connectivity, rail links, logistics infrastructure, energy networks and digital connectivity. Its realisation depends on financing, political stability, commercial viability, coordination among participating countries and the resolution of regional conflicts.

Economic benefits of connectivity corridors

  • Lower freight and transaction costs.
  • Improved access to ports and international markets.
  • Greater participation in global and regional value chains.
  • More reliable energy and commodity supply routes.
  • Development of industrial clusters, logistics hubs and border economies.
  • Improved people-to-people movement and tourism.

Risks and safeguards

  • Debt sustainability and transparent project financing.
  • Environmental and social impact assessment.
  • Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity.
  • Local community participation and fair benefit-sharing.
  • Security of critical infrastructure and digital networks.
  • Coordination of customs, transit, standards and border management.

17. Trade, Investment and Supply-Chain Issues in Regionalism

Regional organisations increasingly influence economic policy through trade agreements, standards, investment rules, transport networks and supply-chain cooperation. Their relevance to India extends beyond tariff reduction.

Tariff and non-tariff barriers

  • Tariffs: Customs duties imposed on imported goods. Regional trade agreements may reduce or eliminate tariffs on qualifying products.
  • Non-tariff measures: Regulations, licensing, quotas, technical standards, sanitary requirements and other measures affecting trade.
  • Rules of origin: Criteria used to determine the economic nationality of a product and its eligibility for preferential tariff treatment.
  • Trade facilitation: Simplification, modernisation and harmonisation of export-import procedures.
  • Trade remedies: Measures such as anti-dumping duties, countervailing duties and safeguards, subject to applicable rules.

Regional value chains

A regional value chain exists when production stages are distributed across multiple countries in a region. Components may be manufactured in one country, assembled in another and exported to a third market. Efficient customs, transport, standards and investment rules are necessary for these networks to function.

Supply-chain resilience

Supply-chain resilience refers to the ability of production and distribution networks to withstand and recover from disruptions. Pandemic-related interruptions, conflict, export restrictions, natural disasters and shipping bottlenecks have increased its importance.

  • Diversification of suppliers and markets.
  • Development of domestic manufacturing capacity in critical sectors.
  • Strategic reserves and improved logistics information.
  • Cooperation on critical minerals, semiconductors, medicines and energy.
  • Reliable transport corridors and secure digital infrastructure.

Trade diversion and trade creation

Trade creation occurs when a trade agreement replaces higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a partner. Trade diversion occurs when preferential tariffs shift imports from a more efficient non-member supplier to a less efficient member supplier. The net welfare effect depends on the scale of both effects and the wider economic context.

Implications for India

  • Regional agreements should be assessed for their effects on consumers, producers, workers and government revenue.
  • Export competitiveness depends on productivity, quality, logistics, standards and reliable market access.
  • India must balance strategic autonomy with participation in regional production networks.
  • Trade negotiations should address services, digital trade, investment, data, intellectual property and standards as well as tariffs.
  • Domestic reforms are essential if Indian firms are to benefit from external market access.

18. India's Regional Strategy: Principles and Policy Framework

Neighbourhood First

Neighbourhood First prioritises India's immediate neighbours through development partnerships, connectivity, trade, capacity-building, humanitarian assistance and diplomatic engagement. Its effectiveness depends on sensitivity to neighbouring countries' domestic priorities and perceptions of asymmetry.

Act East Policy

Act East builds on the earlier Look East Policy and seeks deeper political, economic, cultural and strategic engagement with Southeast Asia and the wider Indo-Pacific. ASEAN is central to this approach.

SAGAR and maritime cooperation

SAGAR—Security and Growth for All in the Region—reflects India's vision for maritime cooperation in the Indian Ocean. It includes maritime security, capacity-building, disaster response, sustainable development and cooperation with island and coastal states.

Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative

The Indo-Pacific Oceans Initiative (IPOI) is an Indian initiative intended to promote cooperation on maritime security, maritime ecology, maritime resources, capacity-building, disaster risk reduction, science and technology, and trade connectivity. It is a cooperative framework rather than a military alliance.

Strategic autonomy and multi-alignment

India engages with different regional institutions and partners according to issue-specific interests. It participates in arrangements that may include countries with which it has strategic disagreements. This approach seeks to preserve decision-making autonomy while expanding economic and diplomatic options.

Development partnership

India's regional engagement includes grants, lines of credit, technical assistance, training, digital public infrastructure, health cooperation and humanitarian support. Sustainable partnerships require consultation with recipient countries, local ownership, transparency and timely delivery.

Flexible and issue-based cooperation

Where large regional institutions face political constraints, smaller coalitions and project-specific arrangements may deliver practical outcomes. Such arrangements should complement broader regional institutions and avoid creating unnecessary institutional fragmentation.

19. Challenges Facing Regional Organisations

  • Political mistrust: Historical disputes and bilateral tensions weaken collective decision-making.
  • Major-power rivalry: Strategic competition can divide members and reduce institutional autonomy.
  • Institutional weakness: Limited funding, staffing and monitoring capacity undermine implementation.
  • Unequal development: Economic disparities can produce concerns about unequal gains and adjustment costs.
  • Protectionism: Tariffs, non-tariff barriers and domestic political pressures limit trade integration.
  • Connectivity gaps: Poor transport infrastructure, border delays and incompatible standards raise costs.
  • Consensus constraints: Unanimity can protect smaller states but may slow collective responses.
  • Overlapping memberships: Multiple institutions can create duplication, inconsistent rules and competing priorities.
  • Implementation deficit: Declarations and agreements may not translate into operational projects.
  • Non-traditional threats: Climate change, pandemics, cyber risks, illegal fishing and organised crime require sustained cooperation.

20. Way Forward for Effective Regional Cooperation

  1. Prioritise achievable, measurable and time-bound projects over broad declarations.
  2. Strengthen secretariats, technical bodies, funding and implementation monitoring.
  3. Improve customs cooperation, digital documentation, border infrastructure and trade facilitation.
  4. Reduce avoidable non-tariff barriers while preserving legitimate health, safety and environmental standards.
  5. Develop sustainable transport, energy and digital connectivity.
  6. Ensure transparent financing, environmental safeguards and local community participation.
  7. Strengthen regional disaster preparedness, public-health coordination and climate adaptation.
  8. Use flexible subregional cooperation to complement wider regional institutions.
  9. Respect sovereignty and build trust through consultation and predictable conduct.
  10. Promote inclusive regionalism that considers the interests of smaller states and less-developed regions.

22. Prelims Factual Reference Points

  • SAARC: Established in 1985; Secretariat in Kathmandu; eight members; SAFTA signed in 2004 and entered into force in 2006.
  • BIMSTEC: Established in 1997; seven members; Secretariat in Dhaka; Charter entered into force in 2024.
  • BBIN: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India and Nepal; Motor Vehicles Agreement signed in 2015.
  • IORA: Established in 1997; Secretariat in Mauritius.
  • ASEAN: Established on 8 August 1967; Secretariat in Jakarta; 11 members following Timor-Leste's accession in October 2025.
  • ARF: Established in 1994; focuses on regional political and security dialogue.
  • EAS: Established in 2005; leaders-led strategic forum; 19 members following Timor-Leste's ASEAN accession.
  • ASEAN Plus Three: ASEAN plus China, Japan and South Korea.
  • ADMM-Plus: ASEAN defence ministers and eight dialogue partners, including India.
  • SCO: Established in 2001; India became a full member in 2017; RATS is based in Tashkent.
  • EAEU: Came into operation in 2015; five members.
  • GCC: Established in 1981; six members; Secretariat in Riyadh.
  • EU: Based on treaties and common institutions; its single market and customs union distinguish it from ordinary dialogue forums.
  • RCEP: ASEAN-led trade agreement with five non-ASEAN partners; India is not a member.
  • CPTPP: High-standard trade agreement; India is not a member.

Membership and institutional arrangements can change. For current-affairs-based questions, verify the latest official membership lists and accession dates rather than relying on older textbooks or static summaries.

23. Mains Analytical Framework

How to assess the effectiveness of a regional organisation

  1. Mandate: Does the organisation have clear and realistic objectives?
  2. Institutional design: Are decision-making, dispute settlement and implementation mechanisms adequate?
  3. Political commitment: Do member states demonstrate sustained political will?
  4. Economic incentives: Are the expected benefits distributed in a manner acceptable to members?
  5. Implementation: Are agreements translated into projects, trade flows and measurable outcomes?
  6. External environment: Can the organisation manage major-power competition and global shocks?

SAARC versus BIMSTEC

SAARC has a broader South Asian identity but is constrained by political tensions and unanimity. BIMSTEC offers a more focused Bay of Bengal platform linking South and Southeast Asia. However, BIMSTEC also faces institutional, connectivity and implementation challenges. It should be viewed as a complementary platform, not an automatic substitute for SAARC.

ASEAN centrality and India's Indo-Pacific policy

ASEAN centrality provides an inclusive institutional foundation for Indo-Pacific cooperation. India benefits from supporting ASEAN-led forums while developing practical partnerships with individual countries and other groupings. The challenge is to ensure that strategic competition does not weaken regional autonomy or economic openness.

Regional trade agreements and India's development priorities

Trade agreements can expand markets and improve investment flows, but they can also expose domestic sectors to import competition. India should pursue calibrated integration supported by competitiveness reforms, logistics development, standards infrastructure, export diversification and effective safeguards.

Suggested conclusion

Regional organisations are most effective when they combine political trust with practical economic incentives and implementable projects. India's regional strategy should therefore pursue inclusive institutions, resilient connectivity, balanced trade integration and respect for the sovereignty and development priorities of partner countries.
