1. South Asia: Regional Profile

South Asia is a strategically important region linking the Indian subcontinent with Central Asia, Southeast Asia, West Asia and the Indian Ocean. Its countries share historical, cultural, linguistic, economic and ecological connections, but regional cooperation remains constrained by political disputes, security concerns and uneven economic integration.

India occupies a central geographic and economic position in South Asia. Its relations with neighbouring countries influence regional connectivity, energy security, maritime stability, trade and the wider balance of power.

  • Shared civilisational and cultural connections.
  • Cross-border rivers, watersheds and ecological systems.
  • Common challenges involving poverty, public health, climate vulnerability and disaster risk.
  • Trade and transport potential across land and maritime routes.
  • Security challenges involving terrorism, organised crime and political instability.

2. Regional Organisations and Cooperation

South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation (SAARC)

SAARC was established in 1985 through the Dhaka Declaration. It seeks to promote the welfare of the peoples of South Asia, improve their quality of life, accelerate economic growth and strengthen collective self-reliance through regional cooperation.

Its members are Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. Afghanistan joined in 2007.

  • SAARC operates on the principles of sovereign equality, territorial integrity, political independence, non-interference and mutual benefit.
  • Bilateral and contentious issues are excluded from deliberations under the SAARC Charter.
  • Decisions are taken on the basis of unanimity.
  • The organisation has promoted cooperation in areas such as agriculture, health, education, culture, disaster management and economic development.

SAARC's potential has been limited by political tensions, particularly India-Pakistan relations, the absence of regular high-level summits and weak regional economic integration.

South Asian Free Trade Area (SAFTA)

SAFTA was signed in 2004 and entered into force in 2006. It aims to reduce tariffs and promote trade among member States through a preferential regional trade framework.

Implementation has faced obstacles including sensitive lists, non-tariff barriers, weak transport connectivity, restrictive trade procedures and political tensions.

South Asian University

South Asian University was established through an agreement among SAARC member States to promote regional academic cooperation, research and people-to-people ties.

3. Limits of SAARC and the Rise of Subregional Cooperation

When region-wide cooperation becomes difficult, smaller groups of neighbouring countries can pursue functional cooperation in areas where interests converge. This approach is often described as subregionalism or variable geometry.

  • BIMSTEC: Connects South Asia and Southeast Asia through the Bay of Bengal and supports cooperation in security, connectivity, trade, technology and other agreed sectors.
  • BBIN: Bangladesh, Bhutan, India and Nepal cooperate on connectivity, transport facilitation, energy and related issues.
  • SASEC: The South Asia Subregional Economic Cooperation programme supports regional connectivity and economic cooperation, with Asian Development Bank support.
  • IORA: The Indian Ocean Rim Association provides a wider platform for cooperation among Indian Ocean countries.

Subregional cooperation can deliver practical results, but it should complement rather than automatically replace broader regional institutions. Overlapping initiatives require coordination to avoid duplication and fragmented standards.

4. Regional Economic Integration

South Asia remains less economically integrated than several other major regions despite geographic proximity and complementarities in production, labour, energy and markets.

Potential benefits

  • Lower trade and transport costs.
  • Expanded regional value chains and investment.
  • Improved access to ports, markets and energy.
  • Greater employment and economic opportunities in border regions.
  • Improved resilience through diversified supply chains.

Barriers

  • High tariffs and non-tariff barriers.
  • Inadequate roads, railways, ports and customs infrastructure.
  • Political disputes and security-related restrictions.
  • Limited trade finance and uneven regulatory standards.
  • Weak private-sector linkages and limited cross-border investment.

Regional integration requires trade facilitation, predictable rules, reliable infrastructure, financial connectivity and political confidence.

5. Connectivity and Transport Cooperation

Transport connectivity is a foundation for regional economic integration. South Asia's geography makes roads, railways, inland waterways, ports and multimodal transport especially important.

  • BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement: Intended to facilitate cross-border movement of passenger, personal and cargo vehicles among participating countries, subject to implementation arrangements.
  • Inland waterways: Can reduce transport costs and improve access for landlocked and riverine regions.
  • Rail connectivity: Can support trade, passenger movement and access to ports.
  • Border infrastructure: Integrated check posts, customs modernisation and coordinated procedures can reduce delays.
  • Energy connectivity: Cross-border electricity trade and grid links can improve regional energy security.
  • Digital connectivity: Cooperation in telecommunications, digital payments and information exchange can support trade and public services.

Connectivity projects must account for environmental impacts, local communities, security, financial sustainability and the capacity to maintain infrastructure.

6. Energy Cooperation

South Asia has significant potential for electricity trade because of differences in energy resources, seasonal demand and generation capacity.

  • Hydropower potential in Nepal and Bhutan.
  • Electricity transmission and cross-border grid arrangements.
  • Regional cooperation on renewable energy and energy storage.
  • Gas and petroleum infrastructure where economically and politically feasible.
  • Cooperation on energy efficiency and climate-resilient infrastructure.

Energy trade can create mutual economic benefits, but it requires reliable contracts, transparent pricing, investment security and agreement on environmental and social safeguards.

7. Shared Rivers and Water Diplomacy

South Asia contains major transboundary river systems, including the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra basins. Their management affects agriculture, hydropower, drinking water, navigation, ecosystems and disaster preparedness.

  • Indus Waters Treaty, 1960: Governs the allocation and use of the waters of the Indus river system between India and Pakistan, subject to its terms and dispute-resolution mechanisms.
  • Ganga Water Treaty, 1996: Provides a framework for sharing Ganga waters at Farakka between India and Bangladesh.
  • Mahakali Treaty, 1996: Provides for integrated development of the Mahakali River, including the Pancheshwar Multipurpose Project framework between India and Nepal.
  • India-Bangladesh Joint Rivers Commission: Provides an institutional mechanism for discussion and cooperation on shared river issues.

Water cooperation faces challenges arising from competing development priorities, seasonal variability, climate change, data gaps, upstream-downstream concerns and domestic political sensitivities.

Cooperative water management requires reliable hydrological data, joint monitoring, flood forecasting, environmental safeguards and equitable benefit-sharing.

8. Security Cooperation in South Asia

Regional security is affected by both conventional military concerns and non-traditional threats.

  • Cross-border terrorism and extremist networks.
  • Arms, narcotics and human trafficking.
  • Organised crime and illicit financial flows.
  • Cyber threats and disinformation.
  • Maritime security, piracy and illegal fishing.
  • Border disputes and military tensions.
  • Political instability and humanitarian displacement.
  • Disaster risks, pandemics and climate-related insecurity.

Regional security cooperation requires intelligence sharing, law-enforcement coordination, effective border management, confidence-building measures and political dialogue.

9. Climate Change and Disaster Management

South Asia is highly exposed to climate-related risks, including floods, cyclones, heatwaves, droughts, glacial hazards, coastal erosion and water stress. Many risks cross national borders and require regional preparedness.

  • Develop shared early-warning and disaster-information systems.
  • Coordinate river-basin flood forecasting and emergency response.
  • Strengthen coastal resilience and cyclone preparedness.
  • Promote climate-resilient agriculture and water management.
  • Share technical expertise and disaster-response resources.
  • Improve regional coordination on public health emergencies.

India's disaster response capabilities and geographic position create opportunities for regional humanitarian assistance, but effective cooperation requires trust, rapid communication and clear operational arrangements.

10. China and External Power Competition in South Asia

China's growing economic, infrastructure, diplomatic and security engagement in South Asia has changed the regional strategic environment.

  • Infrastructure investment and connectivity projects.
  • Port, transport and energy development.
  • Defence cooperation and arms transfers.
  • Trade, finance and technology partnerships.
  • Greater diplomatic engagement with smaller South Asian countries.

India's concerns include strategic access, debt sustainability, military implications and the possibility of external influence in its immediate neighbourhood. At the same time, regional countries have their own development priorities and seek diversified partnerships.

India's response should be based on credible development cooperation, timely project delivery, respect for sovereignty and constructive engagement rather than a purely competitive approach.

11. India as a Regional Power and Development Partner

India's regional role is shaped by its economic size, geographic position, military capabilities, institutional capacity and historical links with neighbouring countries.

Positive contributions

  • Development assistance, grants and concessional credit.
  • Disaster relief and humanitarian support.
  • Medical assistance and public health cooperation.
  • Training, scholarships and capacity building.
  • Trade facilitation and connectivity infrastructure.
  • Energy trade and regional electricity cooperation.
  • Maritime security and humanitarian assistance in the Indian Ocean.

Expectations and concerns

  • Smaller neighbours expect sensitivity to sovereignty and domestic political concerns.
  • Project delays can weaken India's credibility as a development partner.
  • Regional perceptions of asymmetry can create mistrust.
  • India must balance security priorities with economic and humanitarian engagement.

12. Challenges to Regional Cooperation

  • Political tensions and unresolved bilateral disputes.
  • Unanimity requirements and institutional limitations in SAARC.
  • Weak trade integration and high transaction costs.
  • Inadequate regional transport and energy infrastructure.
  • Unequal economic capacities and concerns about asymmetry.
  • External power competition and strategic mistrust.
  • Cross-border terrorism and security restrictions.
  • Climate vulnerability and competing water demands.
  • Domestic political changes affecting continuity of regional agreements.
  • Limited institutional capacity to implement and monitor commitments.

13. Reform Priorities for South Asian Cooperation

  1. Revitalise functional cooperation: Prioritise areas such as health, climate, education, disaster response and trade facilitation where common interests are clear.
  2. Strengthen subregional mechanisms: Use BIMSTEC, BBIN and SASEC to pursue practical connectivity and development outcomes.
  3. Improve regional trade: Reduce non-tariff barriers, streamline customs and expand trade-related infrastructure.
  4. Enhance energy integration: Develop transparent cross-border electricity markets and reliable transmission systems.
  5. Institutionalise water cooperation: Strengthen joint data collection, flood forecasting and river-basin dialogue.
  6. Promote people-to-people ties: Expand academic exchanges, tourism, cultural cooperation and cross-border community links.
  7. Build mutual trust: Respect sovereignty, avoid coercive diplomacy and maintain regular political communication.
  8. Improve project execution: Ensure timely completion, transparency, local participation and sustainable financing.
  9. Strengthen regional disaster response: Build shared preparedness and emergency coordination mechanisms.
  10. Support inclusive regionalism: Ensure that smaller States and less-developed regions share the benefits of cooperation.

14. Prelims Knowledge and Mains Analysis

Prelims focus

  • SAARC membership, founding year, charter principles and decision-making.
  • SAFTA and its broad objectives.
  • Difference between SAARC, BIMSTEC, BBIN, SASEC and IORA.
  • Major transboundary river treaties and agreements.
  • Regional connectivity and energy cooperation initiatives.
  • Relevant regional institutions and their mandates.

Mains focus

Explain why South Asia has substantial potential for cooperation but remains weakly integrated. Analyse political mistrust, security disputes, external power competition and institutional limitations alongside opportunities in trade, energy, water, climate and connectivity. India should be assessed as both a major regional power and a partner whose credibility depends on mutual benefit and timely delivery.
