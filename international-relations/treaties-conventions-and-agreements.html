1. Meaning, Nature and Importance of International Treaties

International treaties are formal agreements concluded between States or other entities possessing treaty-making capacity and governed by international law. They establish rights and obligations, regulate cooperation, provide mechanisms for dispute settlement and help develop common international standards.

The title of an instrument—treaty, convention, covenant, charter, protocol, agreement or statute—does not by itself determine its legal character. The intention of the parties and the rules governing the instrument are more important.

  • Bilateral treaties: Agreements between two parties, such as a bilateral trade or defence agreement.
  • Multilateral treaties: Agreements involving several States, often establishing common international rules.
  • Regional agreements: Instruments negotiated within a particular geographical or political grouping.
  • Framework conventions: Establish broad principles and institutions, with later protocols or decisions providing more detailed obligations.
  • Technical agreements: Cover specialised areas such as aviation, postal services, taxation, maritime safety and telecommunications.

Treaties promote predictability, peaceful relations, trade, investment, human rights, environmental protection and collective security. They also influence domestic legislation, institutions and public policy.

2. Sources of International Law

Article 38(1) of the Statute of the International Court of Justice identifies important sources considered by the Court:

  • International conventions: Treaties establishing rules expressly recognised by the States concerned.
  • International custom: General State practice accepted as law, requiring State practice and opinio juris—the belief that the practice is legally obligatory.
  • General principles of law: Principles recognised across legal systems and applied to international disputes where appropriate.
  • Judicial decisions and scholarly writings: Subsidiary means for determining rules of law.

Resolutions of international organisations, unilateral declarations, decisions of international tribunals and other instruments may also have legal or interpretative significance depending on their context and the applicable rules.

Important distinction: Not every resolution or declaration is a binding treaty. Some instruments are politically significant but do not create the same legal obligations as a treaty in force.

3. Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, 1969

The Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT) codifies important rules governing treaties between States. It addresses conclusion, entry into force, reservations, interpretation, amendment, invalidity, termination and suspension.

Key concepts

  • Signature: May authenticate the text and, depending on the treaty and circumstances, may express consent to be bound. In many treaties, signature is subject to ratification.
  • Ratification: The formal international act by which a State establishes its consent to be bound where ratification is required.
  • Accession: A State joins a treaty under its provisions, usually after the initial signing stage.
  • Acceptance or approval: Alternative formal methods of expressing consent to be bound.
  • Entry into force: The treaty becomes legally operative according to its specified conditions.
  • Reservation: A unilateral statement seeking to exclude or modify the legal effect of particular provisions in their application to the reserving State, subject to applicable limits.
  • Amendment: A formal change to treaty provisions by agreement or through the treaty's prescribed procedure.
  • Withdrawal or denunciation: A State exits a treaty in accordance with the treaty's provisions or applicable international law.

Interpretation and performance

Article 26 embodies pacta sunt servanda: every treaty in force is binding upon its parties and must be performed in good faith.

Article 27 generally prevents a State from invoking its internal law as justification for failure to perform a treaty. Article 31 provides the general rule of interpretation: good-faith interpretation according to the ordinary meaning of the terms, in their context and in light of the treaty's object and purpose. Article 32 permits supplementary means, including preparatory work and circumstances of conclusion, in specified situations.

Material breach, impossibility of performance, fundamental change of circumstances and conflict with a peremptory norm of general international law may have consequences under the Convention's conditions. A State cannot simply disregard a treaty because its domestic policy preferences have changed.

4. Treaty-Making in India: Constitutional Position

The Constitution does not contain a single comprehensive treaty-making provision. Treaty-making and implementation involve the Union's executive power and the distribution of legislative powers.

  • Article 73: The executive power of the Union extends to matters on which Parliament has power to make laws, subject to constitutional limitations.
  • Union List, Entry 14: Covers entering into treaties and agreements with foreign countries and implementing treaties, agreements and conventions with foreign countries.
  • Article 253: Parliament may make laws for implementing treaties, agreements and conventions with other countries and decisions made at international conferences, associations or other bodies.
  • Article 51(c): Directs the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organised peoples with one another.
  • Article 246 and the Seventh Schedule: Govern legislative distribution and must be read with Article 253.
  • Article 253 and State subjects: Parliament's treaty-implementation power can extend to matters otherwise within the State List, subject to the constitutional framework.

India follows a broadly dualist approach: a treaty does not automatically become enforceable domestic law merely because India has accepted it internationally. Where implementation requires changes to domestic law or creates enforceable rights and obligations, appropriate legislation may be necessary.

The executive generally conducts negotiations and concludes international agreements. There is no general constitutional rule requiring parliamentary ratification of every treaty. However, legislation is required where domestic legal changes are necessary, and Parliament exercises oversight through questions, debates, committees and scrutiny of implementing legislation.

5. Important Judicial Principles in India

  • Maganbhai Ishwarbhai Patel v. Union of India (1969): Distinguished between implementing an international agreement through executive action and situations requiring legislation, particularly where domestic law or individual rights must be altered.
  • Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin (1980): Recognised that an international covenant does not by itself override domestic law, although courts may consider international commitments when interpreting domestic provisions.
  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Used international conventions, including CEDAW, to develop guidelines on workplace sexual harassment in the absence of adequate domestic legislation, so far as consistent with constitutional guarantees and domestic law.
  • Gramophone Company of India v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey (1984): Recognised the relevance of international law in domestic adjudication, subject to consistency with municipal law.

These decisions demonstrate that international obligations can influence interpretation and policy, but their domestic operation remains governed by the Constitution, legislation and judicial principles.

6. Major International Treaties and Conventions Relevant to India

Human rights

  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), 1966: Covers civil and political rights, including liberty, fair trial, expression and participation in public affairs.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), 1966: Addresses rights relating to work, health, education, social security and an adequate standard of living.
  • CEDAW, 1979: Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), 1989: Establishes standards for children's civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), 2006: Promotes equality, accessibility, inclusion and participation of persons with disabilities.
  • Convention against Torture (CAT), 1984: Establishes obligations concerning prevention and prohibition of torture. India has signed but not ratified it.

Environment and climate

  • UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), 1992: Framework for international climate cooperation.
  • Kyoto Protocol, 1997: Established quantified emission-reduction commitments for specified developed-country parties.
  • Paris Agreement, 2015: Framework of nationally determined contributions, transparency and global stocktakes.
  • Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992: Conservation, sustainable use and fair and equitable benefit-sharing from genetic resources.
  • CITES, 1973: Regulates international trade in listed endangered species.
  • Ramsar Convention, 1971: Conservation and wise use of wetlands.
  • Montreal Protocol, 1987: Controls production and consumption of ozone-depleting substances.
  • UN Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD), 1994: Addresses desertification, land degradation and drought.

Security and disarmament

  • United Nations Charter, 1945: Establishes the UN framework, including rules on use of force, collective security and peaceful dispute settlement.
  • Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), 1968: Framework concerning nuclear weapons, disarmament and peaceful uses of nuclear energy. India is not a party.
  • Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBT), 1996: Seeks to prohibit all nuclear explosions. It has not entered into force; India is not a signatory.
  • Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC), 1993: Prohibits chemical weapons and establishes verification arrangements.
  • Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), 1972: Prohibits development, production, acquisition and stockpiling of biological and toxin weapons.

Trade and maritime affairs

  • Marrakesh Agreement, 1994: Established the World Trade Organization.
  • General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT): Foundational multilateral rules for trade in goods.
  • General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS): Multilateral framework for trade in services.
  • Agreement on Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS): Establishes minimum standards for intellectual property protection in trade.
  • Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA): Seeks to simplify, modernise and harmonise customs procedures.
  • United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), 1982: Legal framework for maritime zones, navigation, marine resources and dispute settlement.
  • MARPOL, 1973/1978: Rules to prevent pollution from ships.

7. Reservations, Declarations and Treaty Flexibility

Multilateral treaties accommodate different legal systems and national circumstances through reservations, interpretative declarations, optional protocols, transition periods and differentiated obligations.

  • Reservations may enable wider participation but can weaken uniform application.
  • Some treaties prohibit reservations or restrict them to specified provisions.
  • A reservation incompatible with the object and purpose of a treaty may be impermissible under the VCLT framework.
  • Interpretative declarations clarify a State's understanding but do not necessarily alter treaty obligations.
  • Optional protocols may create additional obligations for States that separately accept them.
  • Special and differential treatment in trade agreements may provide developing countries with longer transition periods or flexibility.

India's approach often seeks to balance acceptance of international standards with constitutional requirements, developmental priorities, sovereignty and policy flexibility.

8. Treaty Compliance, Monitoring and Enforcement

International law generally lacks a central enforcement authority equivalent to a domestic government. Compliance is encouraged through a combination of legal, diplomatic, institutional and reputational mechanisms.

  • Reporting and review: States submit periodic reports to treaty bodies or review mechanisms.
  • Monitoring and verification: Technical bodies inspect, assess or verify compliance under specific treaties.
  • Dispute settlement: Negotiation, mediation, arbitration and judicial settlement may be available.
  • Countermeasures and sanctions: May arise under applicable international law or institutional decisions, subject to legal conditions.
  • Domestic implementation: Legislation, regulations, administrative institutions and judicial remedies translate obligations into practice.
  • Peer and public scrutiny: International review and civil society monitoring can influence State behaviour.

Trade agreements often contain dedicated dispute settlement chapters. These may provide consultations, panel proceedings, compliance review and, in some cases, authorised trade remedies.

9. Challenges for India in International Treaty-Making

  • Developmental priorities: International environmental and economic commitments must be reconciled with poverty reduction, energy access and industrial development.
  • Policy autonomy: Treaty obligations can constrain future policy choices, making careful negotiation essential.
  • Implementation capacity: Domestic institutions may lack adequate resources, coordination or technical expertise.
  • Unequal bargaining power: Developing countries may face pressure to accept rules shaped predominantly by powerful States.
  • Changing technology: Existing treaties may not adequately address cyber operations, artificial intelligence, data flows and emerging technologies.
  • Fragmented governance: Overlapping treaties and institutions can create inconsistent obligations and compliance burdens.
  • Enforcement asymmetry: Powerful States may be better placed to resist pressure or influence the interpretation of rules.

10. India's Approach and Way Forward

  1. Negotiate treaties through careful assessment of legal, economic, security and constitutional implications.
  2. Strengthen coordination between the Ministry of External Affairs, line ministries, States and specialised agencies.
  3. Ensure timely domestic legislation and administrative rules where implementation requires them.
  4. Build legal and technical expertise in emerging fields such as cyber security, digital trade and climate governance.
  5. Promote equitable rules that recognise differentiated capacities and responsibilities where appropriate.
  6. Use multilateral forums to shape standards rather than merely respond to rules developed elsewhere.
  7. Improve transparency and parliamentary scrutiny of major international commitments while respecting legitimate confidentiality in negotiations.
  8. Maintain consistency between international commitments, constitutional values and domestic public policy.

  • India-EFTA TEPA: Signed on 10 March 2024 and in force from 1 October 2025.
  • India-UK CETA: Signed on 24 July 2025 and in force from 15 July 2026.
  • India-Oman CEPA: Signed on 18 December 2025 and in force from 1 June 2026.
  • India-New Zealand FTA: Signed on 27 April 2026 and in force from 20 October 2026.
  • India-EU FTA: Negotiations concluded on 27 January 2026. Conclusion of negotiations is not the same as signature, ratification or entry into force.

13. Multilateral Trade Framework: World Trade Organization

The World Trade Organization (WTO), established on 1 January 1995 through the Marrakesh Agreement, provides the central institutional framework for multilateral trade rules. India was a founding member of the WTO and had been a GATT contracting party since 1948.

Core principles

  • Most-Favoured-Nation (MFN): A trade advantage granted to one WTO member should generally be extended to all members, subject to permitted exceptions.
  • National Treatment: Imported products should generally receive treatment no less favourable than like domestic products after entry into the market, subject to the relevant agreement.
  • Transparency: Members publish and notify relevant trade measures and policies.
  • Predictability: Bound tariff commitments and rules reduce arbitrary changes in market access.
  • Special and Differential Treatment: Certain provisions provide developing and least-developed countries with flexibility and assistance.

Major WTO agreements

  • GATT 1994: Trade in goods, tariffs and non-discrimination.
  • GATS: Trade in services and four modes of supply.
  • TRIPS: Minimum standards for intellectual property rights, including patents, copyright and trademarks.
  • Agreement on Agriculture: Domestic support, market access and export competition disciplines.
  • Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures: Disciplines on subsidies and remedies against certain subsidised imports.
  • Anti-Dumping Agreement: Conditions for imposing anti-dumping duties where dumped imports cause material injury.
  • Sanitary and Phytosanitary Measures Agreement: Food safety and animal and plant health measures.
  • Technical Barriers to Trade Agreement: Technical regulations, standards and conformity assessment procedures.
  • Trade Facilitation Agreement: Customs transparency, release and clearance of goods and border cooperation.
  • Dispute Settlement Understanding: Rules for resolving disputes between WTO members.
  • Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies: Addresses certain harmful fisheries subsidies and entered into force in September 2025.

WTO institutions

  • Ministerial Conference: The highest decision-making body, meeting at least once every two years.
  • General Council: Performs the functions of the Ministerial Conference between its meetings.
  • Dispute Settlement Body: Administers the dispute settlement system.
  • Trade Policy Review Body: Conducts reviews of members' trade policies.
  • Specialised councils and committees: Oversee implementation of individual agreements.

14. India's Key Issues and Negotiating Positions at the WTO

Food security and public stockholding

India argues that public procurement and food stockholding are essential for food security, farmer support and the functioning of the public distribution system. The WTO Agreement on Agriculture disciplines domestic support, creating disputes over the calculation of support and the treatment of administered prices.

  • The peace clause adopted at the Bali Ministerial Conference in 2013 provides specified protection from certain legal challenges relating to public stockholding programmes for food security, subject to conditions.
  • India seeks a permanent solution that reflects the needs of developing countries and contemporary food-security challenges.
  • Relevant domestic programmes include minimum support price procurement and public distribution arrangements.

Special and Differential Treatment

India supports preserving development policy space and flexibility for developing countries. It has opposed approaches that disregard differences in income, institutional capacity and development needs.

Fisheries subsidies

India has emphasised the need to protect the livelihoods of small-scale and traditional fishers while addressing subsidies that contribute to overcapacity, overfishing and illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing.

Public health and TRIPS

India has supported the use of TRIPS flexibilities, including compulsory licensing under appropriate conditions, to protect public health and access to medicines. The Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health is an important reference point.

Dispute settlement reform

The WTO Appellate Body has been non-functional since December 2019 because of vacancies. This has weakened the enforceability and predictability of the multilateral dispute settlement system. Some members use interim arrangements, including the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA), while broader reform remains under discussion.

15. Regional Trade Agreements and Economic Integration

Regional Trade Agreements (RTAs) are arrangements between two or more economies that provide preferential trade treatment. They may be bilateral, regional or cross-regional. The WTO permits certain exceptions to MFN treatment under specified conditions.

Different levels of economic integration

  • Preferential Trade Agreement: Members reduce tariffs on selected products while retaining their own external tariffs.
  • Free Trade Area: Members remove or substantially reduce internal trade barriers but maintain independent external tariffs.
  • Customs Union: Members combine internal free trade with a common external tariff.
  • Common Market: A customs union with wider movement of factors of production, such as labour and capital.
  • Economic Union: Deeper integration involving coordination or harmonisation of economic policies and, in some cases, common institutions or currency arrangements.

Important examples

  • SAFTA: South Asian Free Trade Area under SAARC.
  • ASEAN Free Trade Area (AFTA): Regional tariff liberalisation and economic integration among ASEAN members.
  • RCEP: Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, involving ASEAN and five partner economies. India withdrew from negotiations in 2019 and is not a member.
  • CPTPP: Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, a high-standard trade agreement across the Asia-Pacific.
  • AfCFTA: African Continental Free Trade Area, seeking to create a continent-wide market.
  • European Union: A deeply integrated political and economic union, with a customs union and single market and a common currency used by many, but not all, members.
  • EAEU: Eurasian Economic Union, a regional economic integration arrangement.

Benefits and limitations

  • Trade creation can shift demand towards more efficient producers within an agreement.
  • Trade diversion can shift imports away from more efficient non-members towards less efficient member suppliers because of preferential tariffs.
  • Regional agreements can attract investment, strengthen supply chains and support regional production networks.
  • Overlapping agreements can create a complex network of rules of origin and compliance requirements.

16. Trade in Goods: Tariffs, Rules of Origin and Non-Tariff Barriers

Trade in goods involves customs duties, quotas, product standards, licensing, customs procedures and other measures that affect cross-border movement.

  • Tariff: A customs duty imposed on imported or exported goods.
  • Bound tariff: The maximum tariff commitment a WTO member has agreed not to exceed, subject to applicable rules.
  • Applied tariff: The rate actually imposed at the border.
  • Tariff-rate quota: A specified quantity may enter at a lower tariff, with a higher tariff applying beyond the quota.
  • Rules of origin: Criteria used to determine the country of origin of a product for tariff preferences, trade remedies or other purposes.
  • Non-tariff measures: Measures such as standards, testing, licensing, sanitary requirements and technical regulations.
  • Trade remedies: Anti-dumping duties, countervailing duties and safeguards, subject to legal conditions.

Rules of origin are particularly important in FTAs. Without effective rules and verification, goods from a non-member could be routed through a member country to obtain preferential access without meeting the required origin criteria.

17. Trade in Services, Investment and Digital Trade

GATS and the four modes of supply

  1. Mode 1 — Cross-border supply: Services supplied from one country to another, such as online consultancy.
  2. Mode 2 — Consumption abroad: A consumer travels abroad to receive a service, such as tourism or education.
  3. Mode 3 — Commercial presence: A foreign service supplier establishes a business presence in another country.
  4. Mode 4 — Presence of natural persons: Individuals travel temporarily to supply services abroad, subject to relevant commitments and immigration rules.

Investment agreements

Investment treaties seek to provide protections and predictable rules for cross-border investment. They may address fair and equitable treatment, expropriation, transfers, national treatment and dispute settlement. Their scope depends on the treaty text.

Investor-State Dispute Settlement (ISDS) may allow qualifying foreign investors to bring claims against host States before an arbitral tribunal under the relevant treaty. It raises questions about investor protection, regulatory autonomy, transparency, public interest and the cost of arbitration.

India adopted a Model Bilateral Investment Treaty in 2015 and has sought to recalibrate investment protection and dispute settlement provisions. India's investment treaty policy must be studied separately from its goods and services FTAs.

Digital trade

  • Cross-border data flows and data localisation.
  • Privacy, personal data protection and cyber security.
  • Electronic authentication, digital signatures and paperless trade.
  • Consumer protection and online dispute resolution.
  • Digital taxation and treatment of electronic transmissions.
  • Artificial intelligence, digital platforms and competition concerns.

India seeks to balance digital trade opportunities with data protection, digital inclusion, domestic regulatory space and the development of its digital economy.

18. Trade, Global Value Chains and Supply-Chain Resilience

Global Value Chains (GVCs) divide production across multiple countries. A product may be designed in one country, use components manufactured in several others, be assembled elsewhere and sold globally.

Why GVCs matter to India

  • Integration can increase exports, investment, employment and technology transfer.
  • Participation in intermediate goods trade can help domestic firms enter international production networks.
  • Access to reliable logistics, ports, energy, skilled workers and predictable regulation is essential.
  • Excessive dependence on a single country or supplier can create strategic vulnerabilities.

Supply-chain initiatives

  • Supply Chain Resilience Initiative (SCRI): Cooperation among India, Japan and Australia to improve supply-chain resilience and diversification.
  • Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF): A framework for cooperation on trade, supply chains, clean economy and fair economy. It is not a conventional FTA.
  • Minerals Security Partnership (MSP): Cooperation to support diversified and responsible critical-mineral supply chains.
  • India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC): A proposed connectivity and economic corridor linking India, the Middle East and Europe through transport, energy and digital infrastructure cooperation.
  • International North-South Transport Corridor (INSTC): A multimodal connectivity initiative linking India with Iran, Central Asia, Russia and wider Eurasian markets.

Supply-chain resilience does not necessarily mean complete self-sufficiency. It involves diversification, strategic reserves where appropriate, trusted partnerships, domestic capacity and reliable connectivity.

19. Trade and Development: India's Policy Choices

Trade policy must balance export competitiveness, consumer welfare, employment, industrial development, food security, strategic autonomy and environmental sustainability.

  • Export promotion: Improve logistics, quality, standards compliance, market intelligence and access to finance.
  • Import management: Address unfair trade practices and strategic dependencies while avoiding inefficient protectionism.
  • MSME integration: Help smaller firms understand rules of origin, certification, customs procedures and foreign market requirements.
  • Services exports: Expand access for IT, professional services, education, health, finance and other competitive sectors.
  • Agricultural trade: Balance export opportunities with farmer livelihoods, food security and domestic price stability.
  • Green trade: Respond to climate-related standards and carbon measures while seeking fair treatment for developing economies.
  • Technology access: Build capabilities in semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, critical minerals, clean energy and advanced manufacturing.

Trade agreements alone cannot guarantee export success. Domestic productivity, infrastructure, skills, regulatory quality and firm-level competitiveness determine whether market access becomes actual economic opportunity.

20. India's Negotiating Priorities in Future Agreements

  1. Undertake sector-wise assessments before and after concluding agreements.
  2. Consult State governments, industry, farmers, workers, MSMEs and other affected stakeholders.
  3. Secure meaningful access for Indian services and skilled professionals.
  4. Ensure rules of origin prevent simple trade diversion and misuse of preferences.
  5. Protect legitimate public-policy space in areas such as health, food security and environmental regulation.
  6. Strengthen domestic standards, testing laboratories, logistics and customs capacity.
  7. Monitor utilisation rates and actual export and investment outcomes rather than relying only on headline tariff concessions.
  8. Negotiate transparent and balanced investment protection and dispute settlement provisions.
  9. Build resilience in critical supply chains without undermining beneficial international economic integration.
  10. Improve parliamentary and public understanding of major commitments and their likely domestic effects.

23. Mains Analytical Framework

Possible theme: “India's growing network of trade agreements reflects a shift towards strategic economic diplomacy. Examine the opportunities and challenges.”

Dimensions for analysis

  • Economic: Export diversification, investment, employment, productivity and consumer welfare.
  • Strategic: Diversification of partners, resilience and reduced dependence on concentrated supply chains.
  • Federal: Effects on agriculture, industry, services and State-level economic interests.
  • Social: Impact on workers, farmers, MSMEs, consumers and vulnerable sectors.
  • Regulatory: Standards, data governance, intellectual property, competition and dispute settlement.
  • Developmental: Balancing openness with industrial capacity, technological upgrading and policy space.

Balanced conclusion: Trade agreements are instruments of economic and strategic policy, not ends in themselves. Their success depends on negotiated terms, domestic competitiveness, institutional coordination, fair adjustment support and effective implementation.
