International Financial and Trade Institutions

1. Evolution of the International Economic Order

The contemporary international economic order developed in response to the instability of the inter-war period, the Great Depression and the economic disruptions of the Second World War. The Bretton Woods Conference of 1944 sought to establish institutions that could promote monetary stability, reconstruction and international economic cooperation.

  • International Monetary Fund (IMF): Established to promote monetary cooperation, exchange stability and balance-of-payments support.
  • International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD): Initially focused on post-war reconstruction and subsequently became a major development-finance institution.
  • General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), 1947: Established rules and negotiations for reducing barriers to trade in goods.
  • World Trade Organization (WTO), 1995: Created a permanent institutional framework for multilateral trade rules, negotiations and dispute settlement.

Changing character of the global economy

  • Expansion of cross-border trade, investment and financial flows.
  • Growth of multinational corporations and global value chains.
  • Increasing importance of services, digital commerce, data and intellectual property.
  • Greater interaction between trade, climate policy, technology and national security.
  • Emergence of new development-finance institutions and regional financial arrangements.

The international economic order is not governed by one institution. It consists of overlapping institutions, agreements, standards and national policies.

2. International Monetary Fund (IMF)

The IMF was conceived at Bretton Woods in 1944 and began operations in 1945. It is a specialised agency of the United Nations system, but has its own Articles of Agreement, membership and governance arrangements.

Principal objectives

  • Promote international monetary cooperation.
  • Facilitate the expansion and balanced growth of international trade.
  • Promote exchange-rate stability and orderly exchange arrangements.
  • Support a multilateral system of international payments.
  • Provide temporary financial assistance to members facing balance-of-payments difficulties.

Core functions

  • Surveillance: Monitors national, regional and global economic developments and advises governments on macroeconomic policies.
  • Lending: Provides financial assistance to members experiencing actual or potential balance-of-payments problems.
  • Capacity development: Provides technical assistance and training in taxation, public financial management, monetary policy, financial regulation and statistics.
  • Reserve support: Allocates Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), which supplement members' official reserve assets.

Governance structure

  • Board of Governors: Highest decision-making body, with one governor and alternate from each member country.
  • International Monetary and Financial Committee (IMFC): Advises the Board of Governors on international monetary and financial issues.
  • Executive Board: Conducts the day-to-day business of the institution.
  • Managing Director: Heads the IMF staff and chairs the Executive Board.

Quotas and voting power

Each member has a quota broadly reflecting its position in the world economy. Quotas influence:

  • The financial resources a member contributes to the IMF.
  • Its voting power, together with basic votes.
  • Its access to certain forms of IMF financing.
  • Its share in general SDR allocations.

Quota reviews are therefore central to debates about representation. Developing economies argue that voting shares should better reflect changes in global economic weight.

Sixteenth General Review of Quotas

The IMF Board of Governors concluded the Sixteenth General Review of Quotas in December 2023, approving a 50 per cent equiproportional increase in quotas. The increase is intended to strengthen the quota-based resources of the institution. Its effectiveness depends on the required member consents and related institutional conditions. The central analytical issue is that an equiproportional increase in itself does not redistribute quota shares among members.

Special Drawing Rights (SDRs)

  • SDRs are an international reserve asset created by the IMF; they are not a currency.
  • They are not a claim on the IMF but represent a potential claim on freely usable currencies of IMF members.
  • Their value is based on a basket of major international currencies.
  • SDRs can supplement members' official reserves and may be exchanged for freely usable currencies through prescribed arrangements.
  • General allocations are distributed broadly in proportion to members' quotas, creating debate over whether poorer countries receive adequate practical benefit.

IMF lending facilities

  • Stand-By Arrangement: Supports members facing short-term balance-of-payments difficulties.
  • Extended Fund Facility: Supports countries facing longer-term structural balance-of-payments problems.
  • Rapid Financing Instrument: Provides rapid assistance for urgent balance-of-payments needs.
  • Rapid Credit Facility: Provides concessional rapid assistance to eligible low-income countries.
  • Resilience and Sustainability Facility: Supports longer-term resilience measures, including certain climate-related and pandemic-preparedness reforms.

Criticism and reform issues

  • Quota and voting arrangements may under-represent emerging and developing economies.
  • Loan conditionalities can involve difficult fiscal consolidation and structural adjustment.
  • Uniform policy prescriptions may not adequately account for national circumstances.
  • Capital-flow volatility and private external debt complicate traditional balance-of-payments management.
  • Climate vulnerability and repeated external shocks increase financing requirements.

India and the IMF

India is a founding member. Its engagement includes participation in quota and governance reform discussions, macroeconomic surveillance, international monetary cooperation and debates on the global financial safety net. India supports a more representative IMF while emphasising the importance of financial stability and the development priorities of emerging economies.

3. World Bank Group

The World Bank Group is a group of institutions working to reduce poverty, promote shared prosperity and support development. It provides finance, technical assistance, research and policy advice.

Five constituent institutions

  • International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD): Provides loans, guarantees and advisory services primarily to middle-income and creditworthy lower-income countries.
  • International Development Association (IDA): Provides grants and highly concessional financing to eligible low-income countries.
  • International Finance Corporation (IFC): Supports private-sector development through investments, mobilisation of private capital and advisory services.
  • Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA): Provides political risk insurance and credit enhancement to encourage cross-border investment.
  • International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID): Provides facilities for conciliation and arbitration of investment disputes between states and foreign investors, subject to consent and jurisdictional requirements.

World Bank and World Bank Group: distinction

The term World Bank generally refers to IBRD and IDA together. The World Bank Group includes all five institutions. This distinction is important for factual questions.

Areas of intervention

  • Infrastructure, transport and energy.
  • Education, healthcare and human-capital development.
  • Agriculture, rural livelihoods and food systems.
  • Urban development, water and sanitation.
  • Climate adaptation, mitigation and disaster resilience.
  • Governance, public financial management and institutional capacity.
  • Social protection and poverty reduction.

Major financing instruments

  • Investment Project Financing: Supports specific development projects and programmes.
  • Development Policy Financing: Supports policy and institutional reforms through budget support, subject to agreed conditions.
  • Program-for-Results: Links disbursement to achievement of specified programme results.
  • Guarantees and risk-sharing: Help mobilise private investment by reducing particular risks.

India and the World Bank Group

India has engaged with the World Bank Group in infrastructure, health, education, water, sanitation, urban development, rural livelihoods, energy transition and institutional reform. The effectiveness of such assistance depends on project design, implementation capacity, environmental and social safeguards and measurable outcomes.

Criticism and challenges

  • Governance and voting arrangements require greater representation of developing economies.
  • Project conditionalities and external policy advice may raise questions of national ownership.
  • Large infrastructure projects can create environmental, displacement and livelihood concerns.
  • Development finance must balance investment needs with debt sustainability.
  • Climate and global public-good challenges require greater lending capacity and private-capital mobilisation.

4. World Trade Organization (WTO)

The WTO was established on 1 January 1995 following the Uruguay Round of negotiations. It provides the principal multilateral institutional framework for international trade rules.

Principal functions

  • Administers multilateral trade agreements.
  • Provides a forum for negotiations among members.
  • Monitors national trade policies through the Trade Policy Review Mechanism.
  • Administers the dispute settlement system.
  • Provides technical assistance and capacity-building support.
  • Cooperates with other international economic institutions.

Institutional structure

  • Ministerial Conference: Highest decision-making body; ordinarily meets at least once every two years.
  • General Council: Conducts the WTO's work between Ministerial Conferences.
  • Dispute Settlement Body: General Council meeting to administer dispute settlement procedures.
  • Trade Policy Review Body: General Council meeting to review members' trade policies.
  • Goods, Services and TRIPS Councils: Oversee the principal areas of WTO agreements.

Foundational principles

  • Most-Favoured-Nation treatment: A trade advantage granted to one WTO member generally must be extended to all members, subject to recognised exceptions.
  • National Treatment: Imported products should not be treated less favourably than like domestic products after entering the market, subject to the relevant agreement.
  • Transparency: Members are expected to publish and notify relevant trade measures.
  • Predictability: Tariff bindings and negotiated commitments create greater certainty for traders.
  • Fair competition: WTO agreements discipline certain forms of dumping and subsidies.
  • Special and Differential Treatment: Various agreements provide developing and least-developed countries with flexibilities, transition periods and assistance.

Decision-making

The WTO generally works through consensus. Consensus means that no member present formally objects to a proposed decision. The system gives all members a formal voice but can make negotiations difficult when interests diverge sharply.

5. Major WTO Agreements and Their Significance

General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)

GATT governs trade in goods and seeks to reduce tariffs and other barriers while promoting non-discrimination. The 1994 GATT is part of the WTO legal framework. The original GATT dates to 1947.

General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS)

GATS provides the multilateral framework for trade in services. It recognises four modes of supply:

  1. Mode 1 – Cross-border supply: Service supplied from one country to another, such as an online consultancy.
  2. Mode 2 – Consumption abroad: Consumer travels abroad to obtain a service, such as tourism or education.
  3. Mode 3 – Commercial presence: A foreign service supplier establishes a business presence in another country.
  4. Mode 4 – Presence of natural persons: Individuals travel temporarily to another country to supply a service.

India has an interest in services trade because of its strengths in information technology, business services, professional services and skilled human resources.

Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)

TRIPS establishes minimum standards for intellectual property protection and enforcement in WTO members. It covers patents, copyright, trademarks, geographical indications, industrial designs and other intellectual property rights.

  • Members must provide minimum standards of protection.
  • Patent protection is generally required for inventions in all fields of technology, subject to permitted exclusions and conditions.
  • Compulsory licensing is permitted under specified conditions.
  • The Doha Declaration on TRIPS and Public Health, 2001, affirmed members' ability to use TRIPS flexibilities to protect public health and promote access to medicines.

Agreement on Agriculture (AoA)

The Agreement on Agriculture addresses three broad pillars:

  • Market access: Tariffication and disciplines on import restrictions.
  • Domestic support: Rules governing agricultural subsidies and support measures.
  • Export competition: Disciplines on export subsidies and related measures.

India's concerns include food security, public stockholding, livelihood protection, the treatment of developing-country support and the use of outdated external reference prices in subsidy calculations.

Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures (SCM)

The SCM Agreement disciplines certain subsidies that distort trade. It distinguishes prohibited subsidies from actionable subsidies and provides rules for countervailing duties where subsidised imports cause injury to domestic industry.

Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA)

The TFA seeks to simplify and modernise customs procedures, improve transparency, reduce delays and promote cooperation among border agencies. It is especially relevant for developing countries seeking to reduce logistics costs.

Technical Barriers to Trade (TBT) Agreement

The TBT Agreement disciplines technical regulations, standards and conformity-assessment procedures to ensure they do not create unnecessary obstacles to trade. It does not prevent governments from pursuing legitimate objectives such as safety, health and environmental protection.

Sanitary and Phytosanitary (SPS) Agreement

The SPS Agreement governs measures intended to protect human, animal and plant life or health from risks such as pests, diseases, contaminants and food-safety hazards. It emphasises scientific principles, risk assessment and consistency while recognising members' right to choose an appropriate level of protection.

Agreement on Trade-Related Investment Measures (TRIMs)

TRIMs addresses investment measures related to trade in goods that are inconsistent with GATT obligations, particularly national treatment and quantitative restrictions. Certain local-content and trade-balancing requirements may be inconsistent with its rules.

Government Procurement Agreement (GPA)

The GPA is a plurilateral agreement that opens specified government procurement markets among its parties. WTO membership does not automatically make a country a party to the GPA.

Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies

Adopted at MC12 in 2022, the Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies entered into force on 15 September 2025 after the required acceptance threshold was reached. It disciplines certain harmful subsidies linked to illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing, overfished stocks and fishing in areas beyond national jurisdiction without appropriate regulation. India deposited its instrument of acceptance on 20 July 2026. The agreement is important for ocean sustainability, but also raises questions about the development needs and livelihoods of small-scale fishers.

6. WTO Dispute Settlement System

The Dispute Settlement Understanding (DSU) provides a rules-based process for resolving disputes between WTO members. It is a state-to-state mechanism; private companies do not directly bring WTO disputes.

Broad stages

  1. Consultations: Members attempt to resolve the dispute through dialogue.
  2. Panel establishment: A panel examines the matter if consultations do not resolve it.
  3. Panel proceedings: The panel examines arguments and evidence and circulates its report.
  4. Appeal: The original system provides for appeals on questions of law and legal interpretation.
  5. Adoption: Reports are adopted under DSU procedures.
  6. Implementation: The losing member is expected to bring its measure into conformity.
  7. Compliance proceedings or authorised countermeasures: These may follow where implementation is disputed or not achieved.

Appellate Body crisis

The WTO Appellate Body has been unable to hear appeals because vacancies have not been filled. The last sitting member's term expired in November 2020. This has weakened the intended two-tier dispute settlement system. In some disputes, an appeal without a functioning appellate mechanism can prevent a panel report from becoming finally enforceable under the ordinary process.

Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement (MPIA)

The MPIA is an interim arrangement among participating WTO members. It uses arbitration under Article 25 of the DSU to provide an appeal-like review in disputes between participating members. It does not bind all WTO members and does not replace the Appellate Body for the entire membership.

WTO reform

WTO members have been discussing reforms relating to dispute settlement, decision-making, transparency, development and the organisation's negotiating function. Following MC14 in March 2026, reform discussions moved towards more substantive issue-based work. The challenge is to restore confidence in multilateral rules while ensuring that reforms are acceptable to members at different levels of development.

7. India's Major Positions in the WTO

Public stockholding and food security

India operates public procurement and food distribution programmes to support farmers and provide subsidised food grains to eligible households. WTO debates concern the calculation of domestic support, the use of historical external reference prices and the treatment of public stockholding programmes under agricultural subsidy rules.

  • India argues that food-security programmes must be protected from rules that do not adequately reflect inflation and changing economic conditions.
  • Developing countries seek a permanent solution for public stockholding for food-security purposes.
  • The issue involves the balance between food security, farmer support and international trade disciplines.

Special and Differential Treatment

Developing countries argue that different levels of development require differentiated obligations, transition periods and policy flexibility. India has emphasised that development status and the capacity to implement complex rules must be taken into account in negotiations.

Fisheries subsidies

India's concerns include the livelihoods of small-scale and traditional fishers, the developmental needs of coastal communities and the principle that countries with greater historical contributions to overfishing should bear appropriate responsibility. India's acceptance of the first Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies should be distinguished from its negotiating positions on additional disciplines.

TRIPS and access to medicines

India has supported the use of TRIPS flexibilities to protect public health and promote access to affordable medicines. Its pharmaceutical industry and generic-medicine capacity make intellectual property rules an important trade and development issue.

Digital trade and e-commerce

Key concerns include data governance, privacy, cybersecurity, consumer protection, taxation, digital industrial policy and the ability to regulate emerging technologies. India seeks to balance participation in digital trade with domestic regulatory autonomy.

Services and movement of professionals

India seeks greater opportunities for services exports and temporary movement of professionals under Mode 4 of GATS. Visa rules, recognition of qualifications and domestic regulatory requirements influence the practical ability of Indian professionals to access foreign markets.

Trade remedies and policy space

India uses trade-remedy instruments under domestic law and WTO rules, while also facing trade-remedy actions by other countries. The challenge is to protect legitimate domestic interests without creating arbitrary or discriminatory restrictions.

8. International Trade Economics: Essential Concepts

Trade institutions operate within an economic system shaped by comparative advantage, production costs, market access, exchange rates, supply chains and domestic policy choices.

Absolute and comparative advantage

  • Absolute advantage: A country can produce a good using fewer resources than another country.
  • Comparative advantage: A country can produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.

Comparative advantage explains why specialisation and trade can benefit countries even when one country is more productive in every sector. However, the distribution of gains within a country may be unequal, requiring adjustment policies and social protection.

Tariffs and non-tariff measures

  • Tariff: A customs duty imposed on imported or exported goods.
  • Ad valorem tariff: Calculated as a percentage of the value of the product.
  • Specific tariff: Charged as a fixed amount per unit or quantity.
  • Bound tariff: Maximum tariff rate committed by a WTO member for a product.
  • Applied tariff: Rate actually imposed by a country.
  • Non-tariff measure: A policy measure other than an ordinary customs tariff that can affect trade, including technical regulations, quotas and licensing requirements.

Not every non-tariff measure is protectionist. Food-safety standards, environmental requirements and product-safety rules may pursue legitimate public objectives, although discriminatory or unnecessarily restrictive implementation can obstruct trade.

Dumping and anti-dumping duties

Dumping generally refers to exporting a product at less than its normal value. Under WTO rules, an anti-dumping duty may be imposed only after the prescribed investigation establishes dumping, material injury or threat of injury, and a causal link. Anti-dumping duties are not automatic penalties for low-priced imports.

Countervailing duties

Countervailing duties may be imposed to offset certain subsidies provided by an exporting country where subsidised imports cause injury to the domestic industry, following the required investigation and legal procedures.

Safeguard measures

Safeguards are temporary restrictions or duties that may be imposed when increased imports cause or threaten serious injury to a domestic industry. Unlike anti-dumping and countervailing measures, safeguards do not require a finding of unfair trade practices.

Rules of origin

Rules of origin determine the country in which a product is considered to have originated. They are important for tariffs, preferential trade agreements, trade remedies, quotas and trade statistics.

  • Wholly obtained: Goods entirely produced or obtained in one country.
  • Substantial transformation: Goods undergo a qualifying manufacturing or processing operation.
  • Change in tariff classification: Origin is determined by a specified change in customs classification.
  • Regional value content: A prescribed proportion of the product's value must originate within the qualifying region.

Rules of origin and trade agreement misuse

Strict rules of origin can prevent trade deflection, where goods from a non-member country are routed through a member country to obtain preferential tariffs. At the same time, excessively complex rules can increase compliance costs and reduce the utilisation of trade agreements.

Trade creation and trade diversion

  • Trade creation: A trade agreement shifts consumption from higher-cost domestic production to lower-cost imports from a partner.
  • Trade diversion: A trade agreement shifts imports from a more efficient non-member to a less efficient member because of preferential tariff treatment.

Global value chains (GVCs)

GVCs divide production across countries, with different stages such as design, components, assembly, logistics and marketing taking place in different locations. Participation can create jobs, technology transfer and export opportunities, but can also expose economies to supply-chain disruptions and dependence on concentrated sources.

Balance of payments and exchange rates

  • Current account: Records trade in goods and services, primary income and secondary income.
  • Capital account: Records capital transfers and transactions in non-produced, non-financial assets under the balance-of-payments framework.
  • Financial account: Records cross-border investment and financial transactions, including direct investment, portfolio investment and other investment.
  • Foreign-exchange reserves: External assets held by monetary authorities to support confidence, meet external payment needs and manage volatility.

A current-account deficit is not automatically a crisis. Its sustainability depends on the nature of financing, export capacity, external debt, reserves and the productive use of capital inflows.

Trade balance and current account: distinction

The trade balance generally refers to exports minus imports of goods. The current account is broader because it also includes services, primary income and secondary income.

9. Trade Agreements and India's Trade Policy

Trade agreements establish negotiated rules governing market access and economic cooperation. They may cover tariffs, services, investment, standards, intellectual property, digital trade, customs procedures and dispute settlement.

Types of trade agreements

  • Free Trade Agreement (FTA): Reduces or eliminates tariffs and other barriers among members, while each member generally retains its own external tariff policy.
  • Preferential Trade Agreement (PTA): Provides preferential tariff treatment for selected products.
  • Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement (CEPA): Usually covers a broader range of goods, services, investment and economic cooperation.
  • Comprehensive Economic Cooperation Agreement (CECA): A broad cooperation arrangement that may cover goods, services and other economic areas.
  • Customs union: Combines internal free trade with a common external tariff.
  • Common market: Adds freer movement of factors of production to the features of a customs union.

India's trade-policy considerations

  • Access to major export markets for goods and services.
  • Integration into global manufacturing and services value chains.
  • Protection of sensitive sectors and vulnerable producers.
  • Reliable access to energy, minerals, semiconductors and critical inputs.
  • Investment, technology transfer and employment generation.
  • Compatibility with domestic industrial, environmental and social policies.

Potential benefits of trade agreements

  • Lower tariffs and improved market access.
  • Greater export competitiveness and economies of scale.
  • More predictable investment and regulatory conditions.
  • Access to technology, intermediate goods and services.
  • Greater diversification of markets and supply chains.

Potential risks

  • Import competition may affect less competitive domestic industries.
  • Rules of origin may be difficult for small businesses to comply with.
  • Trade deficits may widen in particular sectors.
  • Standards and regulatory obligations can impose compliance costs.
  • Benefits may be concentrated in sectors and regions with greater capacity.

Effective use of trade agreements

Signing an agreement does not automatically generate exports. Benefits depend on utilisation rates, logistics, standards compliance, information for businesses, rules-of-origin documentation, trade finance and domestic productive capacity.

Trade agreements should therefore be assessed through actual export performance, investment, job creation, productivity, import dependence and distributional effects rather than headline tariff reductions alone.

10. World Bank, IMF and WTO: Comparative Understanding

Institution Primary focus Major instruments Key distinction
IMF Monetary cooperation and external stability Surveillance, lending, SDRs, technical assistance Focuses on macroeconomic and balance-of-payments stability
World Bank Group Development and poverty reduction Loans, grants, guarantees, private-sector investment, expertise Focuses on long-term development and poverty reduction
WTO Multilateral trade rules Agreements, negotiations, monitoring, dispute settlement Creates and administers rules governing international trade

The IMF and World Bank are Bretton Woods institutions. The WTO emerged later from the GATT process and is not a Bretton Woods institution.

11. Regional and New Development Finance Institutions

Asian Development Bank (ADB)

Established in 1966 and headquartered in Manila, the ADB supports economic and social development in Asia and the Pacific through loans, grants, technical assistance and policy dialogue.

  • Infrastructure and regional connectivity.
  • Energy transition and climate resilience.
  • Urban development and public services.
  • Regional economic cooperation and integration.

Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)

The AIIB began operations in 2016 and is headquartered in Beijing. It finances sustainable infrastructure and other productive sectors in Asia and beyond.

  • Transport, energy and logistics.
  • Water, sanitation and urban services.
  • Digital connectivity and sustainable infrastructure.
  • Private-capital mobilisation and co-financing.

New Development Bank (NDB)

The NDB was established by the BRICS countries under the 2014 Fortaleza Declaration and began operations in 2015. Its mandate is to mobilise resources for infrastructure and sustainable development projects in emerging markets and developing countries.

European Bank for Reconstruction and Development (EBRD)

The EBRD was established in 1991. It supports transition towards market-oriented economies and private-sector development, initially in Central and Eastern Europe and subsequently across a wider geographical area.

Inter-American Development Bank (IDB)

The IDB supports development in Latin America and the Caribbean through finance, technical assistance and institutional support.

African Development Bank (AfDB)

The AfDB promotes economic development and social progress across African countries, including infrastructure, agriculture, regional integration and private-sector development.

Why multiple development banks matter

  • Diversify sources of development finance.
  • Expand financing for infrastructure and climate-related projects.
  • Provide regional knowledge and specialised expertise.
  • Increase competition and choice among financing institutions.
  • Mobilise private capital through guarantees and co-financing.

Concerns

  • Fragmentation and overlap among institutions.
  • Differences in lending standards and safeguards.
  • Debt sustainability and repayment risks.
  • Unequal access to finance for smaller and poorer economies.

12. Bank for International Settlements and Global Financial Regulation

Bank for International Settlements (BIS)

The BIS was established in 1930 and is headquartered in Basel, Switzerland. It supports cooperation among central banks and provides banking services to them.

  • Promotes cooperation among central banks.
  • Conducts research on monetary and financial stability.
  • Provides a forum for discussion of financial regulation.
  • Hosts committees that develop international standards and supervisory guidance.

Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS)

The Basel Committee, hosted by the BIS, develops international standards and guidance for banking supervision. Its standards are not treaties; national authorities decide how to implement them.

  • Basel I: Introduced a framework centred on minimum capital requirements and credit risk.
  • Basel II: Developed a more risk-sensitive framework based on minimum capital requirements, supervisory review and market discipline.
  • Basel III: Strengthened capital quality and quantity, introduced capital buffers, leverage requirements and liquidity standards in response to the global financial crisis.

Financial Stability Board (FSB)

The FSB coordinates international work on financial stability and monitors implementation of agreed regulatory and supervisory policies. It brings together national authorities, international financial institutions and standard-setting bodies.

Basel standards and national sovereignty

International standards help reduce regulatory arbitrage and strengthen resilience, but their implementation must account for national financial structures, banking-sector conditions and development needs.

13. Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

FATF is an intergovernmental body established in 1989 to develop standards and promote effective implementation of measures against money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing.

Principal functions

  • Develops international recommendations on anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing.
  • Promotes implementation of standards through national systems.
  • Conducts mutual evaluations and follow-up assessments.
  • Identifies jurisdictions with strategic deficiencies through public monitoring statements.
  • Promotes international cooperation and information sharing.

Grey list and black list

  • Jurisdictions under increased monitoring: Often referred to as the grey list; these jurisdictions have strategic deficiencies and are working with FATF to address them.
  • High-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action: Often referred to as the black list; these jurisdictions have serious strategic deficiencies and require enhanced due diligence and, in the most serious cases, countermeasures.

These lists are FATF monitoring instruments, not criminal convictions or automatic United Nations sanctions.

Importance for India

  • Strengthens domestic action against illicit financial flows.
  • Improves cooperation among financial intelligence and law-enforcement agencies.
  • Supports action against money laundering and terrorist financing.
  • Promotes integrity and credibility of the financial system.

Related Indian institutions and framework

  • Financial Intelligence Unit – India (FIU-IND): Receives, processes, analyses and disseminates financial intelligence relating to suspicious transactions.
  • Enforcement Directorate: Investigates offences under laws including the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002.
  • Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002: Provides the domestic legal framework for prevention of money laundering and attachment and confiscation of proceeds of crime, subject to law.
  • Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act, 1967: Provides a framework addressing unlawful and terrorist activities, including relevant financing offences.

14. Sovereign Debt and International Debt Architecture

Sovereign debt refers to borrowing by national governments. Governments borrow domestically and externally to finance public expenditure, infrastructure, social programmes and fiscal deficits.

Types of sovereign debt

  • Domestic debt: Borrowing raised within the domestic financial system, usually in domestic currency.
  • External debt: Debt owed to non-residents, which may be denominated in domestic or foreign currency.
  • Bilateral debt: Debt owed to another government or its agencies.
  • Multilateral debt: Debt owed to institutions such as the World Bank or regional development banks.
  • Commercial debt: Borrowing from private lenders or through international bond markets.

Debt sustainability

Debt sustainability concerns a government's ability to meet current and future debt obligations without extraordinary financial assistance, default or severe disruption to economic activity.

Important indicators include:

  • Debt-to-GDP ratio.
  • Interest payments as a share of government revenue.
  • External debt service relative to exports.
  • Foreign-exchange reserve adequacy.
  • Currency composition and maturity profile of debt.
  • Growth rate, fiscal balance and borrowing costs.

Causes of debt distress

  • Excessive borrowing and weak revenue mobilisation.
  • Depreciation of the domestic currency where debt is foreign-currency denominated.
  • High global interest rates and refinancing costs.
  • Commodity-price shocks and export concentration.
  • Conflict, natural disasters and climate-related shocks.
  • Weak project selection and poor public investment management.

Debt restructuring

Debt restructuring changes the terms of existing debt to restore sustainability. It may involve maturity extensions, interest-rate reductions, principal reductions or other negotiated changes.

Paris Club

The Paris Club is an informal group of official bilateral creditors that negotiates coordinated solutions to payment difficulties of debtor countries. It is not a formal international organisation established by treaty.

G20 Common Framework for Debt Treatments

The Common Framework was developed to coordinate debt treatments for eligible low-income countries. It seeks to bring traditional official bilateral creditors and newer creditors into a coordinated process, with comparability of treatment as an important principle. Implementation has faced concerns about delays, creditor coordination and transparency.

India and sovereign debt issues

India participates in discussions on debt sustainability, development finance and international financial reform. Its regional economic engagement, including financial assistance and development partnerships, also makes debt sustainability in neighbouring countries relevant to India's economic and strategic interests.

15. Global Financial Safety Net

The Global Financial Safety Net (GFSN) refers to the institutions and arrangements that provide financial support to countries facing external financing pressures and financial crises.

Main components

  • Foreign-exchange reserves: First line of defence against external payment pressures.
  • Bilateral swap arrangements: Agreements between central banks to exchange currencies for liquidity support.
  • Regional financing arrangements: Regional mechanisms that provide financial assistance to members.
  • IMF resources: Multilateral financing facilities and reserve support.
  • Multilateral development banks: Provide development and crisis-related financing within their mandates.

Challenges

  • Unequal access to liquidity across countries.
  • Fragmentation among regional and global arrangements.
  • Stigma associated with seeking IMF assistance.
  • Insufficient support for countries facing repeated external shocks.
  • Need for better coordination between emergency liquidity and long-term development finance.

India's foreign-exchange reserves, financial regulation and macroeconomic management are important elements of its external resilience. Regional cooperation and international financial institutions also contribute to stability in the wider neighbourhood.

16. Trade, Climate and Environmental Governance

Trade and environmental policy increasingly interact. Governments use environmental standards, carbon-related measures, subsidies and technology policies that can affect international competitiveness and market access.

Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)

A carbon border adjustment mechanism seeks to address differences in carbon costs between domestic producers and imports. The European Union's CBAM is a prominent example. It raises questions about climate ambition, carbon leakage, measurement of embedded emissions and the treatment of developing-country exporters.

Key concerns for India

  • Compliance costs for carbon-intensive exports such as steel, aluminium, cement and fertilisers.
  • Availability of reliable emissions measurement and verification systems.
  • Access to affordable clean technologies and climate finance.
  • Risk that unilateral environmental measures may operate as disguised trade restrictions.
  • Need to reconcile climate objectives with development priorities and historical responsibility.

Trade and industrial subsidies

Countries increasingly use subsidies to support clean energy, semiconductors, electric vehicles, batteries and strategic manufacturing. Such measures may promote innovation and resilience but can also distort competition, trigger subsidy disputes and encourage protectionist responses.

Policy balance

India needs to improve domestic industrial competitiveness, adopt credible environmental standards, expand clean-energy capacity and engage in international negotiations so that climate-related trade measures are transparent, non-discriminatory and sensitive to different levels of development.

17. Technology, Digital Trade and Financial Innovation

Digital trade

Digital trade includes digitally ordered or digitally delivered goods and services, cross-border data flows and digital platforms. It raises issues of taxation, privacy, cybersecurity, consumer protection, competition and regulatory sovereignty.

Electronic commerce discussions

WTO members have discussed electronic commerce and digital trade, including the treatment of electronic transmissions and the continuation or scope of the moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions. Members differ over the economic and fiscal implications of a permanent arrangement.

Cross-border data governance

  • Data localisation and cross-border transfer rules.
  • Privacy and personal-data protection.
  • Cybersecurity and national security.
  • Competition and market concentration in digital platforms.
  • Taxation of digital services and digital products.

Fintech and international payments

Digital payments, payment-system interoperability, central bank digital currencies and financial technology can reduce transaction costs and improve financial inclusion. However, they also raise regulatory challenges relating to cyber risk, consumer protection, money laundering and cross-border supervision.

India's interests

India seeks to expand digital services exports, strengthen digital public infrastructure, promote secure cross-border payments and preserve the ability to regulate data and digital markets in the public interest.

18. India's Interests in the International Economic Order

Development priorities

  • Maintain policy space for poverty reduction, food security and livelihood protection.
  • Promote industrialisation, employment and domestic manufacturing capacity.
  • Improve market access for goods, services and skilled professionals.
  • Secure reliable access to energy, minerals, technology and critical inputs.
  • Attract investment and participate more effectively in global value chains.

Institutional priorities

  • Reform IMF and World Bank governance to improve developing-country representation.
  • Strengthen multilateral trade rules and dispute settlement.
  • Preserve meaningful special and differential treatment.
  • Improve access to affordable development and climate finance.
  • Promote transparency and fairness in international financial regulation.

Economic security

Recent disruptions have demonstrated the importance of resilient supply chains. India must balance economic efficiency with strategic diversification in sectors such as semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, energy, telecommunications, food systems and critical minerals.

Strategic approach

India's approach combines participation in multilateral institutions, bilateral and regional trade agreements, South-South cooperation, development partnerships and domestic economic reforms. The objective is not isolation from global markets but integration on terms compatible with national development and strategic autonomy.

19. Major Challenges in the International Economic System

  • Representation deficit: Developing countries seek greater voice in institutions whose governance structures reflect earlier distributions of economic power.
  • Protectionism: Tariffs, export controls, subsidies and unilateral restrictions can fragment international markets.
  • Debt vulnerability: High borrowing costs and external shocks constrain development expenditure.
  • Unequal gains from trade: Trade benefits may be concentrated in particular sectors, regions and skill groups.
  • Dispute settlement weakness: The Appellate Body crisis reduces certainty in the multilateral trading system.
  • Climate-related trade tensions: Environmental measures may create new market-access barriers.
  • Digital fragmentation: Divergent rules on data, technology and digital services can obstruct cross-border trade.
  • Supply-chain concentration: Dependence on a limited number of suppliers creates vulnerabilities during crises.
  • Financing gaps: Developing countries face difficulties mobilising long-term and affordable finance for infrastructure and climate transition.
  • Institutional fragmentation: Overlapping mandates can reduce coordination and increase administrative costs.

20. Reform Agenda and Way Forward

  1. Make IMF and World Bank governance more representative of contemporary economic realities.
  2. Strengthen the global financial safety net and improve access to emergency liquidity.
  3. Improve sovereign debt transparency, restructuring procedures and creditor coordination.
  4. Restore a predictable, effective and accessible WTO dispute settlement system.
  5. Make trade rules responsive to food security, public health and development concerns.
  6. Improve transparency and discipline in the use of subsidies and trade restrictions.
  7. Expand affordable climate finance and technology transfer for developing countries.
  8. Strengthen domestic competitiveness through infrastructure, skills, innovation and productivity.
  9. Improve the participation of small businesses in export markets and trade agreements.
  10. Promote resilient and diversified global supply chains without excessive economic fragmentation.
  11. Build domestic capacity for trade negotiations, standards compliance and economic diplomacy.

21. Prelims Factual Reference Points

  • Bretton Woods Conference: 1944.
  • IMF and IBRD: Created through the Bretton Woods arrangements.
  • GATT: 1947; WTO replaced the GATT institutional framework in 1995.
  • IMF: Headquarters in Washington, D.C.; quota-based institution.
  • SDR: International reserve asset, not a currency.
  • World Bank: IBRD and IDA together.
  • World Bank Group: IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA and ICSID.
  • WTO: Established on 1 January 1995; headquartered in Geneva.
  • GATS: Four modes of services supply.
  • TRIPS: Intellectual property standards under the WTO framework.
  • AoA: Market access, domestic support and export competition.
  • TFA: Entered into force in February 2017.
  • Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies: Entered into force on 15 September 2025.
  • WTO dispute settlement: The Appellate Body remains unable to review appeals because of vacancies.
  • MPIA: Interim appeal arbitration arrangement among participating WTO members.
  • ADB: Established in 1966; headquarters in Manila.
  • AIIB: Began operations in 2016; headquarters in Beijing.
  • NDB: Established by BRICS countries in 2014; began operations in 2015.
  • BIS: Established in 1930; headquarters in Basel.
  • FATF: Established in 1989; develops standards against money laundering and terrorist financing.
  • Basel Committee: Hosted by the BIS; its standards are not treaties.
  • Paris Club: Informal group of official bilateral creditors.

22. Important Distinctions for Objective Questions

Concepts Distinction
IMF and World Bank Macroeconomic and external stability versus long-term development finance.
World Bank and World Bank Group IBRD plus IDA versus all five constituent institutions.
Tariff and non-tariff measure Customs duty versus other measures affecting trade.
Anti-dumping and countervailing duty Response to dumping versus response to actionable subsidisation.
Safeguard and anti-dumping measure Temporary response to import surges causing serious injury versus response to dumping causing material injury.
Trade balance and current account Trade in goods versus goods, services, primary income and secondary income.
FTA and customs union Members retain separate external tariffs in an FTA; customs union has a common external tariff.
Panel and Appellate Body Panel examines the dispute; Appellate Body was designed to review legal issues on appeal.
Paris Club and Common Framework Informal official bilateral creditor group versus a G20-supported framework for eligible low-income country debt treatments.
SDR and currency International reserve asset versus legal tender issued by a monetary authority.

23. Mains Analytical Framework

How to analyse an international economic institution

  1. Mandate: What problem was the institution created to address?
  2. Institutional design: How are decisions made and how are members represented?
  3. Effectiveness: Has it delivered stability, development or predictable rules?
  4. Equity: Do its policies account for differences in development levels and capacities?
  5. India's interests: What are the implications for food security, industrialisation, services, technology and strategic autonomy?
  6. Reform: What changes would improve legitimacy, effectiveness and inclusiveness?

Possible analytical themes

  • Reforming Bretton Woods institutions in a changing global economy.
  • WTO multilateralism and the rise of unilateral trade measures.
  • Food security and agricultural subsidy negotiations.
  • Trade liberalisation and domestic livelihood protection.
  • Global debt distress and the development-finance gap.
  • Climate measures and their implications for developing-country exports.
  • Digital trade, data sovereignty and India's policy choices.
  • Global supply-chain resilience and India's manufacturing ambitions.

Balanced conclusion

India's objective should be to strengthen a rules-based and representative international economic order while retaining legitimate policy space for inclusive development. A more effective multilateral system must combine predictable rules with fair representation, sustainable finance and sensitivity to the differing capacities of developed and developing countries.
