1. Evolution of the International Economic Order

The contemporary international financial and trade system emerged largely from the economic arrangements negotiated towards the end of the Second World War. The Bretton Woods Conference of 1944 established the foundations of the International Monetary Fund and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, which became the core of the World Bank Group.

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, concluded in 1947, provided a framework for reducing trade barriers. The World Trade Organization replaced the GATT institutional framework in 1995.

Core objectives

  • Promote international monetary and financial stability.
  • Support reconstruction, development and poverty reduction.
  • Facilitate international trade and investment.
  • Provide financial assistance during economic crises.
  • Establish rules and mechanisms for resolving economic disputes.

2. International Monetary Fund (IMF)

The IMF was established in 1944 and began operations in 1945. Its principal mandate is to promote international monetary cooperation, exchange-rate stability and balanced growth of international trade, while supporting global financial stability.

Major functions

  • Surveillance: Monitors global and national economic developments and provides policy advice.
  • Lending: Provides financial assistance to member countries facing balance-of-payments problems.
  • Capacity development: Offers technical assistance and training in areas such as taxation, public finance, monetary policy and statistics.
  • Special Drawing Rights: Issues and allocates SDRs, an international reserve asset supplementing members' official reserves.

Governance

  • All member countries are represented through the Board of Governors.
  • Day-to-day institutional work is overseen by the Executive Board.
  • Voting power is weighted broadly according to quotas.
  • Quotas influence members' financial contributions, voting power and access to certain forms of financing.

Special Drawing Rights

SDRs are not a currency and are not a claim on the IMF. They represent a potential claim on freely usable currencies of IMF members. Their value is based on a basket of major international currencies.

India and the IMF

India is a founding member of the IMF. Its engagement includes economic surveillance, financial cooperation, quota and governance discussions and participation in international monetary policy debates.

Criticism and challenges

  • Quota and voting arrangements are criticised for under-representing emerging economies.
  • Conditional lending can require difficult fiscal and structural adjustments.
  • Uniform policy prescriptions may not sufficiently account for national circumstances.
  • Financial crises increasingly involve capital flows, debt vulnerabilities and global shocks beyond traditional monetary concerns.

3. World Bank Group

The World Bank Group is a group of institutions that provides development finance, knowledge and technical assistance. Its broad objectives include poverty reduction and shared prosperity.

Constituent institutions

  • International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD): Provides loans and other financial support to middle-income and creditworthy lower-income countries.
  • International Development Association (IDA): Provides concessional financing and grants to the poorest countries.
  • International Finance Corporation (IFC): Supports private-sector development through investment and advisory services.
  • Multilateral Investment Guarantee Agency (MIGA): Provides political risk insurance and credit enhancement to encourage investment.
  • International Centre for Settlement of Investment Disputes (ICSID): Provides facilities for conciliation and arbitration of investment disputes between States and foreign investors, subject to jurisdictional requirements.

Areas of work

  • Infrastructure and transport.
  • Education and healthcare.
  • Agriculture and rural development.
  • Climate resilience and clean energy.
  • Governance and institutional capacity.
  • Social protection and poverty reduction.

India and the World Bank

India has received World Bank financing and technical assistance in areas such as infrastructure, health, education, urban development, rural livelihoods and environmental sustainability.

Challenges

  • Need for greater representation of developing countries in governance.
  • Balancing development finance with debt sustainability.
  • Ensuring that projects are socially inclusive and environmentally responsible.
  • Improving the speed and effectiveness of project implementation.

4. World Trade Organization (WTO)

The WTO was established on 1 January 1995 following the Uruguay Round of trade negotiations. It provides the principal multilateral framework for international trade rules and negotiations.

Core functions

  • Administer multilateral trade agreements.
  • Provide a forum for trade negotiations.
  • Monitor national trade policies.
  • Facilitate dispute settlement under WTO agreements.
  • Provide technical assistance and capacity building for developing countries.

Foundational principles

  • Most-Favoured-Nation treatment: A trade advantage granted to one member generally must be extended to all WTO members, subject to recognised exceptions.
  • National Treatment: Imported products should not be treated less favourably than like domestic products after entering the market, subject to the relevant agreement.
  • Transparency: Members are expected to publish and notify relevant trade measures.
  • Predictability: Tariff bindings and other commitments provide greater certainty for traders.
  • Fair competition: WTO agreements address practices such as dumping and certain subsidies.

5. Major WTO Agreements

General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT)

GATT governs trade in goods and seeks to reduce tariffs and other barriers while promoting non-discrimination. The 1994 GATT is part of the WTO legal framework.

General Agreement on Trade in Services (GATS)

GATS establishes rules for international trade in services. It recognises four modes of supply:

  • Mode 1: Cross-border supply.
  • Mode 2: Consumption abroad.
  • Mode 3: Commercial presence.
  • Mode 4: Presence of natural persons.

Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights (TRIPS)

TRIPS establishes minimum standards for intellectual property protection and enforcement in WTO members. It includes provisions on patents, copyright, trademarks and other intellectual property rights.

Agreement on Agriculture (AoA)

The Agreement on Agriculture addresses market access, domestic support and export competition. Its rules are important for agricultural trade and food security debates.

Trade Facilitation Agreement (TFA)

The TFA seeks to simplify and modernise customs procedures, improve transparency and reduce delays at borders.

Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures

This agreement disciplines certain subsidies and establishes conditions for countervailing measures against subsidised imports that cause injury.

6. WTO Dispute Settlement Mechanism

The WTO dispute settlement system provides a rules-based process for resolving trade disputes among member governments.

Basic stages

  1. Consultations between the disputing members.
  2. Establishment of a panel where consultations do not resolve the dispute.
  3. Panel examination and circulation of findings.
  4. Appeal on questions of law under the intended appellate framework, subject to the current institutional situation.
  5. Adoption and implementation of rulings in accordance with WTO procedures.

The Appellate Body has been unable to function since December 2019 because vacancies have not been filled. This has weakened the two-tier dispute settlement system and contributed to concerns about enforceability and predictability.

Some WTO members have used alternative arrangements, including the Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement, but participation is voluntary and it does not replace the Appellate Body for all members.

7. India's Position on Major WTO Issues

Agricultural subsidies and public stockholding

India maintains that public stockholding for food security is important for supporting vulnerable populations, farmers and domestic food distribution. WTO discussions have focused on subsidy limits, historical reference prices and the need for a permanent solution for public stockholding programmes.

Special and Differential Treatment

Developing countries argue that different levels of development require policy flexibility and differentiated obligations. India has defended the importance of special and differential treatment in multilateral trade rules.

Fisheries subsidies

WTO negotiations on fisheries subsidies seek to curb harmful support that contributes to overcapacity, overfishing and illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing. India has emphasised the development needs of small-scale fishers and the importance of appropriate transition periods and policy space.

Public health and TRIPS

India has supported measures to improve access to affordable medicines and has participated in debates over intellectual property rules and public health. The TRIPS Agreement contains flexibilities, including compulsory licensing under specified conditions.

Digital trade and e-commerce

India has sought to preserve policy space on digital trade, data governance and customs treatment of electronic transmissions. Discussions involve balancing trade facilitation, innovation, consumer protection and domestic regulatory autonomy.

8. Regional Development Banks

Asian Development Bank (ADB)

The ADB was established in 1966 and is headquartered in Manila. It supports economic and social development in Asia and the Pacific through loans, grants, technical assistance and policy dialogue.

  • Infrastructure and connectivity.
  • Energy and climate resilience.
  • Urban development and public services.
  • Regional economic cooperation.

Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB)

The AIIB began operations in 2016 and is headquartered in Beijing. It finances sustainable infrastructure and other productive sectors in Asia and beyond.

  • Transport and logistics.
  • Energy and power infrastructure.
  • Water, sanitation and urban services.
  • Digital and cross-border connectivity.

New Development Bank (NDB)

The NDB was established by BRICS countries in 2014 and began operations in 2015. Its mandate is to mobilise resources for infrastructure and sustainable development projects in emerging markets and developing countries.

Relevance for India

  • Diversifies sources of development finance.
  • Supports infrastructure investment and sustainable development.
  • Expands institutional cooperation with emerging economies.
  • Creates opportunities for financing in local currencies and innovative instruments, subject to institutional rules.

9. Bank for International Settlements (BIS)

The BIS is an international financial institution that supports cooperation among central banks and provides banking services to them. It is headquartered in Basel, Switzerland.

Functions

  • Promotes cooperation among central banks.
  • Conducts research on monetary and financial stability.
  • Provides a forum for discussion of financial regulation.
  • Hosts committees that develop international standards and supervisory guidance.

The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, hosted by the BIS, develops international standards for bank capital, liquidity and risk management. These standards are not treaties; their implementation depends on national regulatory authorities.

10. Financial Action Task Force (FATF)

FATF is an intergovernmental body established in 1989 to develop standards and promote effective implementation of measures against money laundering, terrorist financing and proliferation financing.

Major functions

  • Develops recommendations on anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing.
  • Conducts mutual evaluations of member and other assessed jurisdictions.
  • Identifies jurisdictions with strategic deficiencies through its public monitoring processes.
  • Promotes international cooperation and information sharing.

Importance for India

  • Strengthens domestic anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing systems.
  • Supports action against illicit financial flows.
  • Improves cooperation among financial intelligence and law-enforcement agencies.
  • Supports integrity of the international financial system.

FATF's public statements and monitoring lists should be distinguished from criminal convictions or automatic legal sanctions. Their implications depend on the applicable national and international frameworks.

11. International Financial Architecture and Developing Countries

The international financial architecture includes institutions, rules and arrangements governing monetary stability, development finance, sovereign debt and cross-border capital flows.

Major concerns

  • Under-representation of developing economies in institutional decision-making.
  • High borrowing costs and limited access to long-term development finance.
  • Debt distress and restructuring delays.
  • Volatility in international capital flows.
  • Inadequate climate finance for vulnerable countries.
  • Unequal access to technology and financial resources.

Reform priorities

  • Increase the voice and representation of emerging economies.
  • Improve the speed and fairness of sovereign debt restructuring.
  • Expand concessional finance for low-income and climate-vulnerable countries.
  • Strengthen multilateral development bank lending capacity.
  • Promote transparent and responsible lending practices.
  • Improve coordination among creditors and international institutions.

12. Trade, Development and India's Economic Interests

India's participation in international financial and trade institutions must balance the benefits of integration with the need to protect development priorities and domestic policy space.

Major interests

  • Predictable access to global markets.
  • Protection of food and livelihood security.
  • Affordable access to medicines and essential technologies.
  • Fair rules for services, digital trade and intellectual property.
  • Access to infrastructure and climate finance.
  • Greater representation in international financial decision-making.

India's policy choices require balancing competitiveness, consumer interests, domestic industry, social protection and international obligations.

13. Challenges in the Global Trade System

  • Growing protectionism and trade restrictions.
  • Disputes over agricultural subsidies and food security.
  • Unequal capacity to participate in complex trade negotiations.
  • Trade-distorting subsidies and non-tariff barriers.
  • Fragmentation of global supply chains.
  • Digital trade, data localisation and technology regulation disputes.
  • Weakness in the WTO dispute settlement system.
  • Increasing use of unilateral trade measures and sanctions.

14. Way Forward

  1. Support meaningful reform of the IMF and World Bank governance structures.
  2. Restore a predictable and effective WTO dispute settlement system.
  3. Protect the legitimate development and food-security interests of developing countries.
  4. Strengthen domestic competitiveness through infrastructure, skills and innovation.
  5. Diversify export markets and improve participation in global value chains.
  6. Promote responsible borrowing and sustainable public debt management.
  7. Improve access to affordable climate and development finance.
  8. Strengthen financial regulation and action against illicit financial flows.
  9. Build negotiating capacity through research, data and inter-ministerial coordination.
