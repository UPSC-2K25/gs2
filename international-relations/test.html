1. Meaning and Importance of Global Groupings

Global groupings are formal or informal platforms through which countries coordinate policies, discuss common challenges and influence international economic and political arrangements. They may be organised around economic weight, regional identity, strategic interests or shared development concerns.

India participates in several global groupings to advance strategic autonomy, economic interests, development priorities, international institutional reform and the voice of the Global South.

Functions

  • Coordinate positions on global economic and political issues.
  • Promote trade, investment and financial cooperation.
  • Discuss climate change, energy and sustainable development.
  • Improve representation of emerging and developing economies.
  • Facilitate cooperation on health, technology and security.
  • Provide diplomatic platforms beyond traditional power-centred institutions.

2. Group of Twenty (G20)

The G20 is a major forum for international economic cooperation. It was established in 1999 in the aftermath of financial crises in the late 1990s. It was elevated to a leaders' summit in 2008 during the global financial crisis.

Composition

The G20 comprises 19 countries, the European Union and the African Union. It represents major advanced and emerging economies and accounts for a substantial share of global economic activity.

Objectives

  • Promote international economic and financial stability.
  • Coordinate responses to global economic crises.
  • Strengthen sustainable, inclusive and balanced growth.
  • Discuss international financial architecture and development finance.
  • Promote cooperation on climate, energy, health and digital transformation.

Institutional features

  • The G20 does not have a permanent secretariat.
  • The presidency rotates annually among members.
  • Its work is organised through the Sherpa Track and Finance Track, supported by working groups and engagement groups.
  • Decisions are generally based on consensus and political commitment rather than binding treaty obligations.

India's G20 Presidency, 2023

India's presidency placed emphasis on inclusive development, digital public infrastructure, sustainable growth, climate finance and the concerns of developing countries.

Important outcomes included:

  • New Delhi Leaders' Declaration: A consensus declaration addressing economic, development, climate, technology and geopolitical issues.
  • African Union membership: The African Union was admitted as a permanent member of the G20.
  • Digital Public Infrastructure: Recognition of the role of inclusive and interoperable digital systems in development.
  • Global Biofuels Alliance: Launched to promote international cooperation on sustainable biofuels.
  • Connectivity: Announcement of the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor initiative.

Significance for India

  • Enhanced India's diplomatic visibility and agenda-setting role.
  • Strengthened representation of African and developing-country concerns.
  • Provided an opportunity to promote digital public infrastructure and development priorities.
  • Expanded India's engagement with major economies.

Limitations

  • Geopolitical divisions can prevent consensus on contentious issues.
  • Implementation depends largely on national governments.
  • Developing countries may have limited capacity to shape technical negotiations.
  • Climate finance and development commitments often face delivery gaps.

3. BRICS

BRICS is a forum of major emerging economies. The grouping initially brought together Brazil, Russia, India and China; South Africa joined in 2010, and the organisation subsequently expanded its membership.

Membership and partner-country arrangements have evolved. For examination purposes, distinguish founding members, subsequent full members and partner countries, and verify the current official list.

Objectives

  • Promote cooperation among emerging economies.
  • Support reform of international financial and political institutions.
  • Increase the voice of developing countries in global governance.
  • Expand trade, investment, technology and development cooperation.
  • Encourage a more representative and multipolar international order.

Major institutions

  • New Development Bank: Provides financing for infrastructure and sustainable development projects.
  • Contingent Reserve Arrangement: A framework intended to provide liquidity support in response to short-term balance-of-payments pressures, subject to its rules.

Importance for India

  • Provides a platform for South-South cooperation.
  • Supports calls for reform of global financial governance.
  • Enables engagement with emerging economies.
  • Offers opportunities in development finance and infrastructure.

Challenges

  • Strategic differences among members, particularly India and China.
  • Different economic structures and geopolitical priorities.
  • Difficulty in coordinating common positions on international conflicts.
  • Institutional expansion may make consensus more difficult.

4. India-Brazil-South Africa Dialogue Forum (IBSA)

IBSA was established in 2003 through the Brasilia Declaration. It brings together three large democracies from Asia, Africa and Latin America.

Objectives

  • Promote South-South cooperation.
  • Strengthen democratic and multilateral dialogue.
  • Support reform of international institutions.
  • Cooperate on poverty reduction, health, education and development.
  • Promote trade, technology and people-to-people links.

IBSA Facility for Poverty and Hunger Alleviation

The IBSA Fund supports development projects in partner countries through cooperation with the United Nations system. It reflects practical South-South development cooperation.

Significance for India

  • Provides a platform linking three major developing democracies.
  • Strengthens India's engagement with Africa and Latin America.
  • Supports development diplomacy and multilateral reform.

IBSA and BRICS have overlapping membership, but they are different forums with distinct institutional identities and political orientations.

5. Group of Seven (G7)

The G7 is an informal grouping of advanced industrial democracies. Its members are Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States. The European Union participates in G7 work but is not counted as one of the seven member countries.

Areas of discussion

  • Global economic governance and financial stability.
  • International security and geopolitical developments.
  • Climate change, energy and environmental policy.
  • Technology, supply-chain resilience and economic security.
  • Development assistance and global health.

India's engagement

India has participated in G7 outreach sessions and discussions on issues such as global development, climate, digital technology, food security and international security.

Significance and limitations

  • Provides access to major advanced economies and technology partners.
  • Creates opportunities to communicate developing-country priorities.
  • Its membership is not representative of the full diversity of the global economy.
  • Its political commitments do not automatically bind non-member countries.

6. Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)

The OECD is an intergovernmental organisation established in 1961. It promotes policies intended to improve economic and social well-being through research, standards, policy dialogue and comparative analysis.

Major areas of work

  • Tax policy and international tax cooperation.
  • Trade, investment and economic regulation.
  • Education, employment and skills.
  • Governance, public administration and anti-corruption.
  • Climate, development and environmental policy.
  • Digital economy and responsible business conduct.

India is not an OECD member but engages with the organisation through policy dialogue and participation in selected initiatives. OECD standards and data can influence international regulatory practices and affect India's economic interests.

7. Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC)

APEC is a regional economic forum established in 1989. It promotes trade and investment cooperation across the Asia-Pacific region.

Objectives

  • Facilitate trade and investment.
  • Promote regional economic integration.
  • Improve business mobility and customs procedures.
  • Strengthen cooperation on digital trade, supply chains and sustainable growth.

APEC operates through voluntary cooperation and consensus. It is not a conventional free-trade agreement or a binding supranational institution.

India is not a member. Its interest in APEC is linked to its economic engagement with the Asia-Pacific and Indo-Pacific regions. Accession depends on the forum's membership procedures and political decisions.

8. Commonwealth

The Commonwealth is a voluntary association of independent countries, many of which have historical links with the British Empire. It is based on shared principles expressed in the Commonwealth Charter, including democracy, human rights, development and the rule of law.

Areas of cooperation

  • Democratic institutions and electoral processes.
  • Education, youth development and skills.
  • Climate resilience and sustainable development.
  • Small-State concerns and development finance.
  • Legal and institutional cooperation.

India is an important member and has used the Commonwealth as a platform for engagement with countries across Africa, Asia, the Caribbean and the Pacific.

9. Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)

The Non-Aligned Movement emerged during the Cold War as a grouping of countries seeking to avoid formal alignment with competing military blocs. It was formally established at the 1961 Belgrade Summit.

Foundational principles

  • Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity.
  • Non-interference in internal affairs.
  • Peaceful coexistence.
  • Opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination.
  • Independent foreign policy and freedom from bloc politics.

India's role

India was one of the leading countries in the development of non-alignment. It used the movement to support decolonisation, disarmament, development and a more equitable international order.

Contemporary relevance

The Cold War context has ended, but NAM continues to provide a platform for developing countries to discuss strategic autonomy, development, international inequality and global governance reform.

10. Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)

The CIS was established in 1991 following the dissolution of the Soviet Union. It provides a framework for cooperation among several post-Soviet States.

Its activities cover economic coordination, security dialogue, migration, cultural exchange and other areas. Membership and participation have changed over time, so current status should be checked against official records.

For India, the CIS is relevant mainly through its relations with Russia, Central Asia and the wider Eurasian region.

11. Comparative Assessment of Global Groupings

  • G20: Broad economic coordination among major advanced and emerging economies.
  • BRICS: Cooperation among emerging economies and advocacy for global governance reform.
  • IBSA: South-South cooperation among India, Brazil and South Africa.
  • G7: Coordination among major advanced industrial democracies.
  • OECD: Policy research, standards and economic cooperation.
  • APEC: Asia-Pacific trade and investment cooperation.
  • Commonwealth: Voluntary cooperation based on shared principles and historical links.
  • NAM: Strategic autonomy and collective voice of developing countries.
  • CIS: Cooperation among several post-Soviet States.

12. India's Strategic Approach

  1. Use global groupings to strengthen India's voice in international decision-making.
  2. Promote reform of international institutions to reflect contemporary economic and demographic realities.
  3. Build coalitions on climate finance, technology access, food security and development.
  4. Maintain strategic autonomy and avoid exclusive dependence on any grouping.
  5. Strengthen cooperation with developing countries while maintaining partnerships with advanced economies.
  6. Convert summit commitments into measurable domestic and international outcomes.
  7. Improve technical capacity in negotiations, international law and economic diplomacy.

15. Newer Strategic Groupings and International Initiatives

15.1 Evolution of International Groupings

Contemporary international cooperation increasingly takes place through flexible, issue-based and minilateral arrangements in addition to traditional multilateral organisations. These platforms may focus on maritime security, technology, infrastructure, climate, supply chains, development finance or strategic coordination.

They differ in membership, institutional structure and legal character. Some are formal international organisations, while others are informal partnerships, coalitions, initiatives or project-based frameworks.

15.2 Quad

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad) brings together India, Australia, Japan and the United States. It is a diplomatic partnership focused on cooperation in the Indo-Pacific.

Areas of cooperation

  • Maritime security and a free, open and inclusive Indo-Pacific.
  • Critical and emerging technologies.
  • Resilient supply chains and critical minerals.
  • Cyber security and infrastructure security.
  • Health security, disaster response and humanitarian assistance.
  • Climate action and clean-energy cooperation.

Significance for India: The Quad provides a platform to strengthen Indo-Pacific partnerships without requiring India to enter a formal military alliance. It complements India's emphasis on strategic autonomy and a rules-based maritime order.

Limitations: Different threat perceptions, varied national priorities and the absence of a binding collective-defence obligation constrain the extent of coordination.

15.3 I2U2

I2U2 is a grouping of India, Israel, the United Arab Emirates and the United States. It is sometimes described as a West Asian or Middle Eastern economic cooperation platform.

Areas of cooperation

  • Food security and agricultural technology.
  • Renewable energy and clean-energy projects.
  • Technology, innovation and investment.
  • Infrastructure and economic connectivity.

Significance for India: It connects India's economic and technological capabilities with West Asian investment, innovation and strategic partnerships. It also complements India's wider engagement with the Gulf and the eastern Mediterranean.

Limitation: Its project-based character and regional geopolitical tensions can affect implementation and continuity.

15.4 India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)

IMEC was announced during the 2023 G20 Summit in New Delhi through a memorandum of understanding involving India, the United States, Saudi Arabia, the European Union, the United Arab Emirates, France, Germany and Italy.

Proposed components

  • An eastern corridor connecting India with the Arabian Gulf.
  • A northern corridor connecting the Gulf with Europe.
  • Rail and ship-to-rail connectivity.
  • Potential infrastructure for electricity, digital connectivity and clean hydrogen.

Significance for India: It could diversify trade routes, improve logistics connectivity, strengthen India-West Asia-Europe economic relations and create opportunities for infrastructure and energy cooperation.

Challenges: Financing, coordination across multiple jurisdictions, infrastructure gaps, regional instability, security concerns and the need for sustained political commitment.

Prelims distinction: IMEC is a proposed connectivity and economic corridor framework, not a fully operational railway or a conventional free-trade agreement.

15.5 Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA)

The Global Biofuels Alliance was launched during India's G20 Presidency in September 2023. It seeks to promote international cooperation in the development and deployment of sustainable biofuels.

Objectives

  • Promote sustainable biofuel production and use.
  • Facilitate knowledge-sharing, technology cooperation and capacity-building.
  • Develop common standards and certification approaches where appropriate.
  • Encourage investment and innovation in biofuel value chains.
  • Support energy diversification and lower-emission development pathways.

Significance for India: It supports energy security, agricultural value addition, rural livelihoods and international cooperation on cleaner fuels.

Challenges: Feedstock availability, land and water pressures, food-versus-fuel concerns, lifecycle emissions and the need to ensure genuine environmental sustainability.

15.6 Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI)

CDRI was launched by India in 2019 as a global partnership to promote the resilience of infrastructure systems to natural hazards and climate-related risks. It brings together national governments, international organisations, institutions and other stakeholders.

Major objectives

  • Promote disaster-resilient infrastructure planning and investment.
  • Develop standards, tools and knowledge for resilient infrastructure.
  • Strengthen technical capacity and institutional preparedness.
  • Improve cooperation on infrastructure resilience, including in developing countries and small island developing States.

Significance for India: CDRI strengthens India's development diplomacy and supports the integration of disaster-risk reduction into infrastructure planning.

Prelims distinction: CDRI is a partnership and coalition, not a treaty-based military alliance or a conventional financial institution.

15.7 International Solar Alliance (ISA)

The International Solar Alliance was launched by India and France in 2015. It is a treaty-based international organisation that seeks to promote solar energy deployment, particularly among countries with abundant solar resources.

Areas of work

  • Mobilisation of finance for solar projects.
  • Capacity-building and technical cooperation.
  • Aggregation of demand and reduction of project costs.
  • Support for solar applications in agriculture, health, public infrastructure and remote communities.
  • Promotion of international cooperation on solar energy deployment.

Significance for India: ISA showcases India's leadership in climate diplomacy and supports affordable clean-energy access in developing countries.

15.8 Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF)

IPEF was launched in 2022 by the United States and partner economies in the Indo-Pacific. It is a framework for economic cooperation rather than a conventional free-trade agreement.

Four pillars

  1. Trade: Cooperation on trade-related standards and economic practices.
  2. Supply chains: Resilience, diversification and crisis coordination.
  3. Clean economy: Clean energy, decarbonisation and infrastructure cooperation.
  4. Fair economy: Tax transparency, anti-corruption and related governance cooperation.

India participates in the supply-chain, clean-economy and fair-economy pillars. It did not join the trade pillar negotiations.

Significance: IPEF provides an additional platform for supply-chain resilience and economic cooperation in the Indo-Pacific, while preserving flexibility in India's trade-policy commitments.

15.9 Supply Chain Resilience Initiative (SCRI)

The Supply Chain Resilience Initiative was launched in 2021 by India, Japan and Australia to promote diversification and resilience of supply chains in the Indo-Pacific.

Core objectives

  • Reduce excessive dependence on concentrated sources of critical supplies.
  • Promote investment and diversification of production networks.
  • Improve supply-chain risk assessment and information-sharing.
  • Strengthen regional economic resilience.

Significance for India: The initiative complements domestic manufacturing, logistics improvement and efforts to diversify critical imports.

15.10 Minerals Security Partnership (MSP)

The Minerals Security Partnership was launched in 2022 by a group of countries and the European Union to strengthen resilient and responsible critical-mineral supply chains.

India joined the partnership in 2023.

Areas of cooperation

  • Responsible extraction and processing of critical minerals.
  • Investment in diversified supply chains.
  • Environmental, social and governance standards.
  • Recycling, processing technology and supply-chain transparency.

Significance for India: Critical minerals are important for electric vehicles, batteries, renewable energy, electronics and defence technologies. Cooperation can reduce strategic dependence and improve resource security.

15.12 Strategic Significance for India

  • Strategic autonomy: Flexible groupings allow issue-based cooperation without requiring exclusive alignment.
  • Economic security: Supply-chain initiatives reduce vulnerability to external disruptions.
  • Connectivity: Infrastructure partnerships can improve India's access to markets and strategic regions.
  • Technology: Minilateral cooperation can improve access to research, innovation and emerging technologies.
  • Climate diplomacy: Solar, biofuel and resilient-infrastructure initiatives strengthen India's development-oriented climate engagement.
  • Global South: Development partnerships create opportunities to share India's experience in digital systems, capacity-building and affordable technology.

  • Quad has four members: India, Australia, Japan and the United States.
  • I2U2 has four participants: India, Israel, the UAE and the United States.
  • IMEC was announced during India's 2023 G20 Presidency; it is a corridor framework, not an operational free-trade agreement.
  • CDRI was launched by India in 2019 and focuses on disaster-resilient infrastructure.
  • ISA was launched by India and France in 2015 and is treaty-based.
  • IPEF has four pillars; India did not join its trade pillar.
  • SCRI was launched by India, Japan and Australia in 2021.
  • India joined the Minerals Security Partnership in 2023.
  • GBA was launched during India's G20 Presidency in 2023.
  • Not every grouping is a treaty-based organisation. Distinguish alliances, coalitions, partnerships, frameworks and proposed corridors.

15.14 Mains Perspective

Newer groupings reflect a shift towards flexible, issue-based international cooperation. They allow India to pursue specific interests in maritime security, technology, energy, infrastructure and supply-chain resilience while preserving strategic autonomy. Their effectiveness, however, depends on sustained political commitment, financing, institutional coordination and measurable implementation.

16. Additional Global Groupings and Trade-Related Economic Frameworks

16.1 Group of 77 (G77)

The Group of 77 is a coalition of developing countries established in 1964 through the Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries at the first session of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in Geneva.

Objectives

  • Promote the collective economic interests of developing countries.
  • Strengthen their negotiating capacity within the United Nations system.
  • Promote a more equitable international economic order.
  • Advance cooperation on development finance, trade, technology transfer and climate justice.
  • Support reforms in international financial and economic institutions.

Working mechanism

The G77 functions through coordination among developing countries in UN bodies and international conferences. It has chapters in different international institutional settings, including Geneva, New York, Paris, Rome, Vienna and Nairobi. China participates in the coalition's work, although the formal name remains G77 and China.

Importance for India

  • Provides a platform to articulate the concerns of developing countries.
  • Strengthens India's position on development finance, climate finance and technology access.
  • Supports demands for fairer international trade rules and greater representation in global institutions.
  • Provides diplomatic support for the concerns of the Global South.

Limitations

  • Members have different levels of development and economic interests.
  • Consensus can be difficult on trade, climate and geopolitical issues.
  • Collective declarations do not automatically translate into binding commitments or implementation.

16.2 African Union (AU)

The African Union is a continental organisation established in 2002, succeeding the Organisation of African Unity (OAU), which was founded in 1963. Its headquarters are in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

Objectives

  • Promote political and economic integration across Africa.
  • Strengthen peace, security and conflict resolution.
  • Advance democratic governance and constitutional order.
  • Promote sustainable development and regional infrastructure.
  • Strengthen Africa's collective voice in global governance.

Important institutions

  • Assembly of the Union: Comprises Heads of State and Government and is the AU's supreme policy and decision-making organ.
  • Executive Council: Comprises foreign ministers or designated ministers and coordinates policy areas.
  • AU Commission: The secretariat-like executive and administrative body.
  • Peace and Security Council: A standing decision-making organ for conflict prevention, management and resolution.
  • Pan-African Parliament: Provides a platform for representation and deliberation on continental issues.
  • African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights: A judicial institution under the African human-rights framework.

African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

The AfCFTA is a major AU-backed initiative to create a single continental market for goods and services, facilitate investment and promote intra-African trade. Its framework seeks to reduce tariff and non-tariff barriers and improve regional value chains.

India-Africa relations

  • Trade, investment and access to emerging African markets.
  • Energy security, critical minerals and agricultural cooperation.
  • Development partnerships, lines of credit and capacity-building.
  • Cooperation in health, pharmaceuticals, education and digital public infrastructure.
  • Support for a stronger African voice in the United Nations and international financial institutions.

G20 significance

The African Union became a permanent member of the G20 in 2023 during India's G20 Presidency. This expanded the representation of African countries in a major forum for global economic coordination.

Challenges: Conflict, political instability, infrastructure deficits, debt vulnerability, uneven development and difficulties in implementing continental integration.

16.3 AUKUS

AUKUS is a trilateral security partnership announced in September 2021 by Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. It is intended to strengthen security and technological cooperation in the Indo-Pacific.

Two major pillars

  • Pillar I: Cooperation to support Australia's acquisition of conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines, alongside associated industrial, technological and workforce arrangements.
  • Pillar II: Advanced capability cooperation in areas such as artificial intelligence, quantum technologies, cyber capabilities, undersea systems, electronic warfare and hypersonic-related technologies.

Strategic significance

  • Strengthens defence and technological cooperation among three close security partners.
  • Reflects the growing strategic importance of the Indo-Pacific.
  • May influence regional military balances and maritime security calculations.
  • Creates opportunities for advanced defence-industrial and technology cooperation.

Implications for India

  • Contributes to the broader strategic environment in which India pursues its Indo-Pacific interests.
  • May complement cooperation among several partners, including countries participating in the Quad, without being institutionally linked to the Quad.
  • Raises questions about regional militarisation, strategic stability and the nuclear non-proliferation framework.

Important distinction: AUKUS is a security and advanced-technology partnership. It is not a collective-defence treaty identical to NATO, and India is not a member.

16.4 Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP)

RCEP is a regional free trade agreement signed on 15 November 2020. Negotiations were launched in 2012 under ASEAN's leadership, bringing together ASEAN members and their principal free trade agreement partners.

Membership

RCEP has 15 participating countries: the ten ASEAN members and Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand and the Republic of Korea.

Major areas covered

  • Trade in goods and tariff reduction.
  • Rules of origin and regional cumulation.
  • Trade in services and investment.
  • Customs procedures and trade facilitation.
  • Electronic commerce and intellectual property.
  • Competition, economic and technical cooperation.
  • Small and medium-sized enterprises.

Economic significance

RCEP seeks to deepen regional production networks by enabling firms to source inputs from participating economies while meeting agreed rules of origin. It can facilitate cross-border supply chains in electronics, machinery, textiles, automotive components and other sectors.

Why India did not join

India withdrew from RCEP negotiations in November 2019 after raising concerns about the likely effects of the agreement on domestic industry, agriculture, dairy producers and trade balances.

  • Import competition: Concerns about increased imports from highly competitive manufacturing economies.
  • Trade imbalance: Concerns about the expansion of bilateral trade deficits, particularly with China.
  • Rules of origin: Concerns about goods entering through one member economy after undergoing limited processing elsewhere.
  • Domestic industry: Concerns about the adjustment capacity of small manufacturers and vulnerable sectors.
  • Policy space: Concerns about the ability to respond to import surges and protect sensitive sectors.

Implications for India

  • India remains outside the regional trade framework covering major East Asian and Pacific economies.
  • Indian exporters may face relative disadvantages where competing countries receive preferential market access.
  • India must improve domestic competitiveness, logistics, standards and trade facilitation.
  • India retains the option of considering future participation under the agreement's accession framework.

Prelims fact: RCEP is not the same as ASEAN. ASEAN is a regional organisation; RCEP is a free trade agreement involving ASEAN and five other participating countries.

16.5 Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP)

The CPTPP is a high-standard regional free trade agreement signed in March 2018. It evolved from the Trans-Pacific Partnership after the United States withdrew from the original TPP framework.

Membership

As of 15 November 2026, its 12 members are Australia, Brunei Darussalam, Canada, Chile, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, New Zealand, Peru, Singapore, the United Kingdom and Vietnam.

Major areas covered

  • Tariff reduction and market access in goods.
  • Services trade and investment.
  • Digital trade and electronic commerce.
  • Intellectual property protection.
  • Government procurement.
  • Competition policy and state-owned enterprises.
  • Labour and environmental commitments.
  • Rules governing investment and dispute settlement.

Importance for the international economy

The CPTPP represents an ambitious model of trade integration that extends beyond conventional tariff concessions. It seeks to establish common rules for trade, investment, digital commerce and regulatory practices.

India's position

India is not a member. CPTPP membership would require assessment of its rules on market access, intellectual property, investment, state-owned enterprises, digital trade and domestic regulatory policy.

RCEP and CPTPP: distinction

  • RCEP is ASEAN-led and includes China; India is not a member.
  • CPTPP is a separate high-standard trade agreement across the Asia-Pacific and other regions; India is not a member.
  • Both aim to deepen trade and investment integration but differ in membership, rules and regulatory ambition.

16.6 OPEC and OPEC+

The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) is an intergovernmental organisation established in 1960. It coordinates petroleum policies among its member countries with the stated objective of stabilising oil markets and securing regular supplies to consumers and stable income for producers.

OPEC's functions

  • Coordinate petroleum policies among participating members.
  • Monitor oil-market developments and production conditions.
  • Discuss production adjustments and market stability.
  • Represent the interests of oil-producing member countries.

OPEC+

OPEC+ refers to the wider cooperation framework between OPEC members and participating non-OPEC oil-producing countries. It is associated with the Declaration of Cooperation initiated in 2016.

OPEC+ is not simply another name for OPEC. It brings together OPEC and participating non-OPEC producers for consultations and coordinated production decisions.

Importance for India

  • India is a major importer of crude oil and is exposed to changes in international oil prices.
  • Production decisions can influence global supply, prices, inflation and India's import bill.
  • Higher oil prices can worsen the current account balance and raise fiscal pressures through fuel-related costs.
  • Lower prices may benefit consumers and importing economies but can also affect producer-country finances and investment.
  • India must balance energy security, affordability, strategic reserves and diversification of suppliers.

Important distinction: India is not an OPEC member. OPEC is an organisation of petroleum-exporting countries; OPEC+ is a broader production-cooperation arrangement.

16.7 Pacific Islands Forum (PIF)

The Pacific Islands Forum is a regional intergovernmental organisation established in 1971. It brings together Pacific island countries and regional partners to discuss shared political, economic, environmental and security concerns.

Areas of cooperation

  • Climate change, sea-level rise and ocean governance.
  • Regional security and disaster resilience.
  • Trade, economic development and connectivity.
  • Fisheries and sustainable use of marine resources.
  • Regional diplomacy and collective representation.

Importance for India

  • Supports India's engagement with Pacific Island countries through the Forum for India-Pacific Islands Cooperation (FIPIC).
  • Strengthens cooperation on climate resilience, health, capacity-building and development.
  • Provides a platform for engagement with the wider Indo-Pacific island region.
  • Reinforces India's position as a development partner and supporter of maritime cooperation.

16.9 How These Groupings Affect India's Trade and Economic Interests

  1. Market access: Trade agreements influence tariffs, standards, rules of origin and the competitiveness of Indian exports.
  2. Supply-chain integration: Regional frameworks shape where firms source components, manufacture goods and locate investment.
  3. Energy security: OPEC and OPEC+ production decisions affect India's import costs, inflation and external balance.
  4. Technology access: Strategic partnerships influence access to advanced technologies, research, investment and industrial capabilities.
  5. Development finance: Cooperation among developing countries and regional institutions can create alternatives and additional sources of finance.
  6. Global rule-making: G77, BRICS and the African Union provide platforms for developing countries to shape international economic discussions.
  7. Strategic diversification: India must balance partnerships with advanced economies, emerging powers and developing countries.

16.10 Prelims and Mains Revision

Prelims focus

  • G77 was established in 1964; it is a coalition of developing countries.

1. Understanding Global Groupings and International Economic Frameworks

Global groupings are formal or informal arrangements through which countries coordinate policies, negotiate common positions, pursue economic interests and respond to international challenges. They may be organised around economic weight, geographical proximity, shared political interests, security concerns or development priorities.

They have become increasingly important because economic interdependence, technological change, climate risks, financial instability, supply-chain disruptions and geopolitical competition cannot be addressed effectively by individual countries acting alone.

1.1 Major Types of Groupings

  • Global economic forums: G20, G7 and OECD.
  • Emerging-economy and Global South platforms: BRICS, IBSA and G77.
  • Regional economic forums: APEC, ASEAN and the African Union.
  • Security and strategic groupings: Quad, AUKUS and SCO.
  • Trade agreements and economic partnerships: RCEP, CPTPP, IPEF and regional free trade arrangements.
  • Issue-based coalitions: International Solar Alliance, Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure and Global Biofuels Alliance.
  • Energy producer arrangements: OPEC and OPEC+.
  • Historical political platforms: Non-Aligned Movement and Commonwealth.

1.2 Distinguishing Different Institutional Forms

Not every international grouping is an international organisation or a treaty-based alliance. Their legal status, membership rules and obligations differ substantially.

  • International organisation: Usually has a constituent instrument, defined membership and continuing institutional structures. Examples include the United Nations, African Union and ASEAN.
  • Informal forum: Provides a platform for dialogue and coordination without necessarily having a founding treaty or permanent secretariat. Examples include the G20 and G7.
  • Strategic partnership: Facilitates cooperation among selected countries in areas such as defence, technology or economic security. Examples include AUKUS and I2U2.
  • Free trade agreement: Creates negotiated trade obligations among participating parties. Examples include RCEP and CPTPP.
  • Economic cooperation framework: Establishes common initiatives or standards without necessarily creating a conventional free trade agreement. Example: IPEF.
  • Issue-based initiative: Coordinates voluntary cooperation around a particular development or policy objective. Examples include ISA, CDRI and GBA.

Examination relevance: Questions may deliberately mix organisations, forums, alliances, initiatives and agreements. Their nature must be identified correctly before discussing membership or obligations.

2. Group of Twenty (G20)

2.1 Origin and Evolution

The G20 is a major forum for international economic cooperation. It was established in 1999 in response to financial instability in the late 1990s, particularly the Asian financial crisis.

Initially, it operated at the level of Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors. The global financial crisis of 2008 demonstrated the need for coordination at the highest political level. The first G20 Leaders' Summit was held in Washington, D.C., in November 2008.

Its evolution reflects the growing importance of emerging economies in global economic governance and the need to coordinate responses to financial crises, development challenges and cross-border economic risks.

2.2 Membership and Representation

The G20 comprises 19 countries, the European Union and the African Union. Its members collectively represent a substantial share of global GDP, trade, population and financial activity.

The 19 country members are Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, India, Indonesia, Italy, Japan, Mexico, the Republic of Korea, Russia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Türkiye, the United Kingdom and the United States.

The European Union and African Union participate as regional organisations. The African Union became a permanent member at the New Delhi Summit in September 2023, strengthening the representation of African countries in global economic discussions.

Important distinction: The G20 is not a body consisting of the world's 20 largest national economies. Its membership includes 19 countries and two regional organisations.

2.3 Institutional Structure

The G20 is an informal forum, not an international organisation established by a constituent treaty. It does not have a permanent secretariat, headquarters or independent enforcement authority.

  • Presidency: Rotates annually among members. The presidency sets priorities, organises meetings and facilitates negotiations.
  • Troika: The previous, current and incoming presidencies coordinate continuity across successive presidencies.
  • Leaders' Summit: The highest-level meeting, attended by Heads of State or Government, provides political direction.
  • Ministerial meetings: Finance Ministers, Foreign Ministers and other sectoral ministers deliberate on issues within their respective areas.
  • Working groups: Officials and experts negotiate technical proposals on development, employment, agriculture, education, health, energy, environment, digital economy and anti-corruption.
  • International organisations: Institutions such as the IMF, World Bank, WTO and UN contribute expertise and participate in discussions.

2.4 Two Principal Negotiating Tracks

A. Finance Track

Led by Finance Ministers and Central Bank Governors, this track addresses macroeconomic and financial issues.

  • Global economic outlook, growth and financial stability.
  • International financial architecture and IMF-related matters.
  • Reform and financing capacity of Multilateral Development Banks.
  • Debt vulnerabilities and sustainable financing.
  • International taxation and illicit financial flows.
  • Financial regulation, financial inclusion and digital finance.
  • Infrastructure financing and sustainable investment.

B. Sherpa Track

Led by Sherpas appointed by member governments, this track coordinates the broader political, developmental and sectoral agenda.

  • Development and Sustainable Development Goals.
  • Climate change, energy transition and environmental sustainability.
  • Health, education, employment and food security.
  • Trade, investment and supply-chain resilience.
  • Digital transformation and emerging technologies.
  • Anti-corruption, gender equality and social inclusion.

2.5 Decision-Making and Commitments

  • Major declarations are generally negotiated through consensus.
  • Outcomes usually represent political commitments rather than legally binding treaty obligations.
  • Members implement agreed policies through their domestic laws, budgets and institutions.
  • Follow-up, monitoring and mutual assessment can encourage implementation.
  • The Troika and successive presidencies help carry forward unfinished work.

The absence of binding enforcement gives members flexibility, but implementation depends substantially on political will and national capacity.

2.6 Major Areas of Work

  • Macroeconomic coordination: Economic slowdowns, inflation, financial instability, exchange-rate spillovers and global debt risks.
  • International financial architecture: IMF governance, Multilateral Development Bank reform, representation and financing capacity.
  • Debt and development finance: Debt transparency, creditor coordination, debt treatment and financing for vulnerable economies.
  • Trade and supply chains: Trade restrictions, logistics, investment, supply-chain resilience and production networks.
  • Taxation: International tax cooperation, profit shifting, tax transparency and digitalisation of the economy.
  • Climate and energy: Energy security, clean-energy investment, climate finance and sustainable development.
  • Digital economy: Digital public infrastructure, digital payments, data governance, artificial intelligence and cybersecurity.

2.7 Important Initiatives and Mechanisms

  • Financial Stability Board (FSB): Established in 2009 following the global financial crisis to coordinate financial authorities and promote financial stability. It is a distinct institution associated with the G20 process.
  • Common Framework for Debt Treatments: A framework for coordinated debt treatment for eligible countries facing debt distress, involving official bilateral creditors and seeking coordination with private creditors.
  • OECD/G20 BEPS process: Addresses tax avoidance strategies that exploit gaps and mismatches in tax rules to shift profits away from jurisdictions where economic activity occurs.
  • Global Partnership for Financial Inclusion: Promotes financial inclusion through policy coordination, research and sharing of good practices.
  • Quality Infrastructure Investment: Promotes economic efficiency, environmental and social considerations, resilience, governance and transparency in infrastructure.

2.8 India's G20 Presidency, 2023

India held the G20 Presidency from 1 December 2022 to 30 November 2023 under the theme Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam — One Earth, One Family, One Future.

India sought to make the G20 more responsive to the concerns of developing countries and connect global economic discussions with inclusive development.

  • New Delhi Leaders' Declaration: Adopted by consensus in September 2023, addressing sustainable development, climate action, financial architecture, digital transformation, food security, gender equality and geopolitical challenges.
  • African Union membership: The African Union was admitted as a permanent G20 member.
  • Digital Public Infrastructure: Recognition of inclusive digital public infrastructure as an instrument of development.
  • Global Biofuels Alliance: Launched to promote international cooperation on sustainable biofuels.
  • India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor: Announced as a connectivity and economic cooperation initiative involving participating partners.
  • Multilateral Development Bank reform: Leaders supported efforts to make these institutions better, bigger and more effective.

2.9 India's Interests and Negotiating Priorities

  • Strengthen the representation of developing countries in global decision-making.
  • Promote reform of the IMF and Multilateral Development Banks.
  • Advance climate finance, adaptation and technology transfer.
  • Protect food and energy security amid international price volatility.
  • Promote affordable and interoperable digital public infrastructure.
  • Ensure that tax and trade rules recognise the development needs of emerging economies.
  • Support predictable and fair debt-resolution mechanisms.
  • Maintain strategic autonomy while engaging with competing geopolitical powers.

2.10 Limitations and Way Forward

  • Representation deficit: Many small, low-income and vulnerable countries are not members.
  • Implementation gap: Political declarations may not translate into timely domestic action.
  • Geopolitical divisions: Conflicts and strategic rivalry can complicate consensus.
  • Unequal bargaining capacity: Major economies often have greater technical and diplomatic resources.
  • Debt-resolution challenges: Coordination among creditors can be slow and complex.
  • Climate-finance shortfalls: Commitments and actual financial flows may diverge.

India can promote wider consultation, transparent implementation monitoring, affordable development finance, fair debt treatment, open supply chains and stronger continuity across presidencies.

2.11 Prelims and Mains Focus

  • Established in 1999; first Leaders' Summit held in 2008.
  • Comprises 19 countries, the European Union and the African Union.
  • African Union became a permanent member in 2023.
  • No permanent secretariat or founding treaty.
  • Two principal negotiating tracks: Finance Track and Sherpa Track.
  • New Delhi Leaders' Declaration adopted by consensus in September 2023.

Mains perspective: The G20 illustrates both the potential and limitations of informal multilateralism. Its effectiveness depends on consensus, national implementation and the willingness of powerful members to accommodate developing-country concerns.

3. BRICS

3.1 Origin and Evolution

The term BRIC was initially used in 2001 to describe the growing economic importance of Brazil, Russia, India and China. The four countries developed political coordination, and the first BRIC summit was held in 2009. South Africa joined in 2010, and the first BRICS summit with five members was held in 2011.

BRICS has evolved from an economic grouping into a wider platform for political coordination, development cooperation and advocacy for reform of global governance.

3.2 Membership and Expansion

Following the expansion process initiated at the 2023 Johannesburg Summit, BRICS has eleven full members: Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the United Arab Emirates, Indonesia and Saudi Arabia.

Saudi Arabia's participation has been described differently in some official and diplomatic communications. For examination purposes, consult the latest official BRICS membership list rather than relying on older five-member or ten-member descriptions.

The partner-country category was created at the 2024 Kazan Summit. Partner countries participate under a different arrangement from full members and do not automatically enjoy the same decision-making status.

Countries announced as partners during the 2025 Brazilian presidency included Belarus, Bolivia, Cuba, Kazakhstan, Malaysia, Nigeria, Thailand, Uganda, Uzbekistan and Vietnam.

Important distinction: Full membership, partner-country status, outreach participation and BRICS Plus participation are different forms of engagement.

3.3 Objectives and Principles

  • Strengthen economic, political and social cooperation among members.
  • Increase the representation of emerging economies and developing countries in global governance.
  • Promote reform of the United Nations, IMF, World Bank and other international institutions.
  • Expand trade, investment, infrastructure and development cooperation.
  • Strengthen South-South cooperation and development partnerships.
  • Promote a multipolar international order and greater policy autonomy.
  • Cooperate on climate change, health, technology, agriculture and sustainable development.

BRICS generally emphasises sovereign equality, mutual respect, consensus, non-interference and cooperation among countries with different political and economic systems.

3.4 Three Broad Pillars of Cooperation

  • Political and security cooperation: Dialogue on international peace, security, counter-terrorism, multilateral reform and regional developments.
  • Economic and financial cooperation: Trade, investment, development finance, financial stability, infrastructure and local-currency settlement discussions.
  • People-to-people cooperation: Education, culture, academic exchange, youth, science, technology and civil society engagement.

3.5 Institutional Structure and Decision-Making

BRICS is an informal intergovernmental coordination forum. It does not have a constituent treaty, a permanent secretariat or a common supranational executive.

  • Rotating presidency: The chairing member organises the annual summit and coordinates the year's agenda.
  • Leaders' Summit: Provides political direction and adopts declarations and decisions.
  • Ministerial meetings: Foreign Ministers, Finance Ministers and other ministers coordinate sectoral cooperation.
  • Senior officials: Conduct preparatory consultations and negotiations.
  • Working groups: Address specific areas such as trade, agriculture, health, science and technology.
  • Consensus: Major decisions are generally based on agreement among participating full members.

Expansion increases the group's representativeness but also creates challenges of coordination, agenda-setting and consensus-building.

3.6 New Development Bank (NDB)

The New Development Bank was established by the BRICS countries through the 2014 Fortaleza Agreement and began operations in 2015. Its headquarters are in Shanghai, China.

Objectives

  • Mobilise resources for infrastructure and sustainable development projects.
  • Support clean energy, transport, water, urban development and environmental sustainability.
  • Complement existing multilateral and regional development banks.
  • Expand development financing for emerging and developing economies.

Institutional features

  • It is a multilateral development bank with its own legal personality and governance structure.
  • Its membership is not limited to BRICS countries; additional countries may join subject to the Bank's rules.
  • It raises resources through capital contributions and borrowing in financial markets.
  • It provides loans and other forms of financing for approved projects.

Importance for India

  • Provides an additional source of infrastructure and sustainable-development finance.
  • Can support transport, renewable energy, water and urban infrastructure.
  • Offers a platform for greater participation by emerging economies in development finance.

Limitation: The NDB's effectiveness depends on financial sustainability, project quality, creditworthiness, environmental safeguards and the ability to mobilise affordable capital.

3.7 Contingent Reserve Arrangement (CRA)

The BRICS Contingent Reserve Arrangement was established through an agreement signed in 2014. It is intended to provide liquidity support to members facing short-term balance-of-payments pressures.

  • It is a financial safety-net arrangement among participating members.
  • It is distinct from the New Development Bank.
  • It is not a development project-financing institution.
  • Its use is governed by agreed access limits and procedural conditions.

3.8 Economic and Trade Cooperation

  • Promoting trade and investment among member economies.
  • Facilitating business-to-business and financial-sector cooperation.
  • Exploring payment-system interoperability and reduced transaction costs.
  • Discussing local-currency settlement and reducing excessive dependence on a single international currency.
  • Supporting infrastructure and sustainable-development finance.
  • Encouraging cooperation in agriculture, energy, manufacturing and digital technologies.

Discussions on local-currency trade do not mean that BRICS has established a common currency. There is no operational BRICS-wide common currency replacing national currencies.

3.9 Importance for India

  • Global governance reform: Supports India's demand for more representative international institutions.
  • Global South: Provides a platform to articulate developing-country concerns.
  • Development finance: The NDB offers additional financing opportunities.
  • Economic diversification: Enables engagement with major emerging markets and resource-rich countries.
  • Energy security: Expanded membership includes several important energy producers.
  • Strategic autonomy: Provides a forum for engagement with different geopolitical actors.
  • Multilateral diplomacy: Strengthens India's participation in debates on international financial architecture and development.

3.10 Challenges and Internal Contradictions

  • India-China tensions: Strategic rivalry and the unresolved boundary question complicate political and economic cooperation.
  • Diverse geopolitical interests: Members may have conflicting positions on international conflicts and security issues.
  • Economic asymmetry: The economic weight and global influence of members are highly unequal.
  • Expansion: More members increase diversity but make consensus and institutional coordination more complex.
  • Limited institutionalisation: Informal arrangements can constrain continuity and implementation.
  • Sanctions and financial risks: Different international sanctions environments may complicate payments, finance and investment.
  • Currency coordination: Differences in monetary systems, capital controls and financial markets make a common currency impractical in the near term.

3.11 Way Forward

  • Strengthen institutional coordination while preserving flexibility.
  • Ensure that expansion does not weaken the group's capacity to deliver practical outcomes.
  • Promote trade facilitation and investment based on transparent rules.
  • Strengthen the NDB's lending capacity and project safeguards.
  • Improve financial connectivity without creating unrealistic expectations about a common currency.
  • Maintain dialogue on areas of convergence despite geopolitical differences.
  • Focus on measurable development outcomes for the Global South.

3.12 Prelims and Mains Focus

  • BRIC originated as an economic term; the political forum developed through government initiatives.
  • South Africa joined the original four in 2010; its first participation as BRICS was in 2011.
  • NDB headquarters: Shanghai, China.
  • CRA is a liquidity-support arrangement, not a development bank.
  • BRICS has full members and a separate partner-country category.
  • BRICS does not have a common currency or permanent secretariat.

Mains perspective: BRICS represents an effort to diversify global governance and increase the influence of emerging economies. Its potential is significant, but internal asymmetries and geopolitical differences limit its ability to act as a unified strategic bloc.

4. India-Brazil-South Africa Dialogue Forum (IBSA)

IBSA was established in 2003 through the Brasilia Declaration. It brings together India, Brazil and South Africa, three large, diverse democracies from Asia, South America and Africa.

4.1 Objectives

  • Promote South-South cooperation.
  • Strengthen democratic and multilateral dialogue.
  • Support reform of international institutions.
  • Cooperate on poverty reduction, health, education and development.
  • Promote trade, technology and people-to-people links.
  • Coordinate positions on issues affecting developing countries.

4.2 IBSA Fund

The IBSA Facility for Poverty and Hunger Alleviation supports development projects in partner countries through cooperation with the United Nations system. It focuses on practical development cooperation, including poverty reduction, food security, health, education and capacity-building.

The Fund is an important example of South-South development cooperation in which developing countries act as development partners rather than only recipients of assistance.

4.3 Significance for India

  • Strengthens India's engagement with Africa and Latin America.
  • Provides a platform linking three major developing democracies.
  • Supports reform of global institutions and multilateralism.
  • Encourages development diplomacy and cooperation on social-sector issues.

4.4 IBSA and BRICS: Difference

IBSA and BRICS have overlapping membership because India, Brazil and South Africa participate in both. However, IBSA is a trilateral forum of three democracies, whereas BRICS is a larger grouping of emerging economies with a different membership and institutional agenda.

IBSA should not be treated as a subsidiary of BRICS. Its separate identity remains relevant for democratic dialogue and South-South cooperation.

5. Group of Seven (G7)

5.1 Origin and Membership

The G7 is an informal grouping of advanced industrial democracies. It emerged from economic coordination discussions in the 1970s. Its members are Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States.

The European Union participates in G7 work but is not counted as one of the seven member countries. Russia participated in the expanded G8 arrangement for a period, but its participation was suspended in 2014 following the annexation of Crimea.

5.2 Areas of Discussion

  • Global economic governance and financial stability.
  • International security and geopolitical developments.
  • Climate change, energy and environmental policy.
  • Technology, supply-chain resilience and economic security.
  • Development assistance and global health.
  • Sanctions, export controls and strategic economic coordination.

5.3 Working Mechanism

The G7 operates through an annual leaders' summit, ministerial meetings, senior officials and expert-level working groups. Its presidency rotates among members and coordinates the annual agenda.

It is not a treaty-based international organisation and does not have a permanent secretariat. Its declarations are political commitments rather than binding international law.

5.4 Economic Influence

  • Coordinates positions on global financial stability and economic policy.
  • Influences discussions on development finance and international sanctions.
  • Shapes debates on technology standards, critical minerals and supply-chain resilience.
  • Coordinates policies among several major advanced economies.
  • Can influence international capital flows, trade restrictions and investment decisions through national policies.

5.5 India's Engagement

India has participated in G7 outreach sessions and discussions on global development, climate, digital technology, food security, critical minerals and international security.

Engagement allows India to communicate developing-country priorities to major advanced economies and build cooperation in technology, investment, energy and strategic issues.

5.6 Significance and Limitations

  • Provides access to major advanced economies and technology partners.
  • Creates opportunities to communicate developing-country priorities.
  • Its membership does not represent the full diversity of the global economy.
  • Its political commitments do not automatically bind non-member countries.
  • Differences among members can limit common action.

6. Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD)

6.1 Origin and Nature

The OECD is an intergovernmental organisation established in 1961, succeeding the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation. Its headquarters are in Paris, France.

It promotes policies intended to improve economic and social well-being through research, standards, policy dialogue, data collection and comparative analysis.

6.2 Major Areas of Work

  • Tax policy and international tax cooperation.
  • Trade, investment and economic regulation.
  • Education, employment and skills.
  • Governance, public administration and anti-corruption.
  • Climate, development and environmental policy.
  • Digital economy and responsible business conduct.
  • Competition policy and corporate governance.
  • Economic statistics and comparative policy analysis.

6.3 Important OECD Initiatives

  • Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA): Assesses the application of knowledge and skills by 15-year-old students in participating education systems.
  • BEPS project: Addresses base erosion and profit shifting by multinational enterprises.
  • Global Forum on Transparency and Exchange of Information for Tax Purposes: Promotes international cooperation on tax transparency.
  • OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct: Provide recommendations for responsible business conduct.
  • International tax standards: Contribute to discussions on cross-border taxation, information exchange and digitalisation.

6.4 India's Engagement

India is not an OECD member but engages with the organisation through policy dialogue, technical cooperation and participation in selected initiatives. OECD standards and data can influence international regulatory practices and affect India's economic interests.

6.5 Significance and Concerns

  • Provides comparative data and policy research.
  • Influences global standards in taxation, governance and education.
  • Creates opportunities for India to participate in technical negotiations.
  • Some standards may reflect the priorities of advanced economies and require careful adaptation to developing-country conditions.

7. Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC)

7.1 Origin and Membership

APEC was established in 1989 to promote economic cooperation across the Asia-Pacific region. It currently has 21 member economies.

APEC uses the term member economies because its work focuses primarily on trade and economic cooperation. Its participants include sovereign States as well as separately represented economic entities such as Hong Kong, China and Chinese Taipei.

7.2 Objectives

  • Facilitate free and open trade and investment.
  • Promote regional economic integration.
  • Improve customs procedures and trade facilitation.
  • Strengthen business mobility and regulatory cooperation.
  • Promote digital trade and supply-chain connectivity.
  • Support sustainable and inclusive economic growth.

7.3 Institutional Features

  • APEC operates through consensus and voluntary cooperation.
  • Its members make non-binding commitments rather than treaty obligations.
  • Economic Leaders' Meetings provide high-level political direction.
  • Ministerial meetings, senior officials and working groups implement the agenda.
  • The APEC Secretariat is located in Singapore.

7.4 Major Economic Initiatives

  • Bogor Goals: Adopted in 1994 to promote free and open trade and investment in the Asia-Pacific region.
  • Osaka Action Agenda: Provided a framework for implementing the Bogor Goals.
  • Putrajaya Vision 2040: Sets out the long-term vision of an open, dynamic, resilient and peaceful Asia-Pacific community.
  • Trade facilitation: Focuses on reducing transaction costs and improving customs, logistics and regulatory processes.
  • Digital economy cooperation: Addresses digital connectivity, innovation and cross-border economic activity.

7.5 India's Position

India is not a member of APEC. Its interest is linked to economic engagement with the Asia-Pacific and Indo-Pacific regions, supply-chain integration, trade and investment cooperation.

India's participation in other Indo-Pacific arrangements does not automatically provide APEC membership. Accession depends on the forum's membership procedures and political decisions.

7.6 APEC and WTO: Difference

The WTO is a treaty-based international organisation with binding agreements and a formal dispute settlement framework. APEC is a voluntary economic cooperation forum that relies on consensus and non-binding commitments.

8. Group of 77 (G77)

8.1 Origin and Objectives

The Group of 77 was established in 1964 through the Joint Declaration of the Seventy-Seven Developing Countries at the first session of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in Geneva.

It is a coalition of developing countries seeking to promote their collective economic interests and strengthen their negotiating capacity within the United Nations system. China participates in its work, and the coalition is often referred to as G77 and China.

8.2 Major Areas of Cooperation

  • International trade and development.
  • Development finance and debt sustainability.
  • Climate finance and environmental justice.
  • Technology transfer and capacity-building.
  • Reform of international economic institutions.
  • Food security, industrialisation and sustainable development.

8.3 Working Mechanism

The G77 coordinates positions among developing countries in UN bodies and international conferences. It has chapters in several institutional settings, including New York, Geneva, Paris, Rome, Vienna and Nairobi.

It does not operate as a conventional treaty-based international organisation with a permanent central secretariat and binding common policies.

8.4 Importance for India

  • Provides a platform to articulate the concerns of developing countries.
  • Strengthens India's position on development finance, climate finance and technology access.
  • Supports demands for fairer international trade rules.
  • Creates opportunities for coalition-building in multilateral negotiations.

8.5 Limitations

  • Members have different levels of development and economic interests.
  • Consensus can be difficult on trade, climate and geopolitical issues.
  • Collective declarations do not automatically translate into binding commitments.

9. African Union (AU)

9.1 Origin and Objectives

The African Union is a continental organisation established in 2002, succeeding the Organisation of African Unity (OAU), which was founded in 1963. Its headquarters are in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

  • Promote political and economic integration across Africa.
  • Strengthen peace, security and conflict resolution.
  • Advance democratic governance and constitutional order.
  • Promote sustainable development and regional infrastructure.
  • Strengthen Africa's collective voice in global governance.

9.2 Important Institutions

  • Assembly of the Union: Comprises Heads of State and Government and is the AU's supreme policy and decision-making organ.
  • Executive Council: Comprises foreign ministers or designated ministers and coordinates policy areas.
  • AU Commission: The executive and administrative body.
  • Peace and Security Council: A standing decision-making organ for conflict prevention, management and resolution.
  • Pan-African Parliament: Provides a platform for representation and deliberation on continental issues.
  • African Court on Human and Peoples' Rights: A judicial institution under the African human-rights framework.

9.3 African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA)

The AfCFTA is an AU-backed initiative to create a single continental market for goods and services, facilitate investment and promote intra-African trade.

  • Seeks to reduce tariff and non-tariff barriers.
  • Promotes regional value chains and industrialisation.
  • Supports trade facilitation and customs cooperation.
  • Creates opportunities for investment and cross-border production.

9.4 India-Africa Relations

  • Trade, investment and access to emerging African markets.
  • Energy security and access to critical minerals.
  • Development partnerships, lines of credit and capacity-building.
  • Health, pharmaceuticals, education and digital public infrastructure.
  • Cooperation on reform of the United Nations and international financial institutions.

9.5 G20 Significance

The African Union became a permanent member of the G20 in 2023 during India's G20 Presidency. This expanded the representation of African countries in a major forum for global economic coordination.

9.6 Challenges

Conflict, political instability, infrastructure deficits, debt vulnerability, uneven development and difficulties in implementing continental integration remain major challenges.

10. Commonwealth

The Commonwealth is a voluntary association of independent countries, many of which have historical links with the British Empire. It is based on shared principles expressed in the Commonwealth Charter, including democracy, human rights, development and the rule of law.

10.1 Areas of Cooperation

  • Democratic institutions and electoral processes.
  • Education, youth development and skills.
  • Climate resilience and sustainable development.
  • Small-State concerns and development finance.
  • Legal, institutional and public-administration cooperation.
  • Trade, investment and professional networks.

10.2 Institutional Features

  • The Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting provides political direction.
  • The Commonwealth Secretariat coordinates programmes and cooperation.
  • The Commonwealth Ministerial Action Group addresses serious or persistent violations of Commonwealth principles.
  • The Commonwealth Foundation supports civil society participation.

10.3 Importance for India

India is an important member and uses the Commonwealth as a platform for engagement with countries across Africa, Asia, the Caribbean and the Pacific. It provides opportunities for cooperation in education, development, climate resilience and institutional capacity-building.

11. Non-Aligned Movement (NAM)

11.1 Origin and Historical Context

The Non-Aligned Movement emerged during the Cold War as a grouping of countries seeking to avoid formal alignment with competing military blocs. It was formally established at the 1961 Belgrade Summit.

Its leading founding figures included Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Josip Broz Tito of Yugoslavia, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Sukarno of Indonesia and Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana.

11.2 Foundational Principles

  • Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity.
  • Non-interference in internal affairs.
  • Peaceful coexistence.
  • Opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination.
  • Independent foreign policy and freedom from bloc politics.
  • Support for disarmament and international peace.

11.3 India's Role

India was one of the leading countries in the development of non-alignment. It used the movement to support decolonisation, disarmament, development and a more equitable international order.

Non-alignment was not intended to mean isolation or neutrality on every international issue. It emphasised independent judgement and the freedom to decide foreign-policy positions according to national interests.

11.4 Contemporary Relevance

  • Provides a platform for developing countries to discuss strategic autonomy.
  • Supports debates on international inequality and global governance reform.
  • Offers a diplomatic forum for countries seeking to avoid exclusive geopolitical alignment.
  • Continues to address development, disarmament and multilateral cooperation.

11.5 Limitations

The movement has faced difficulties in maintaining unity because members have different political systems, security interests and economic priorities. Its ability to influence international outcomes has also been constrained by limited institutional and enforcement mechanisms.

12. Commonwealth of Independent States (CIS)

The CIS was established in 1991 following the dissolution of the Soviet Union. It provides a framework for cooperation among several post-Soviet States.

12.1 Areas of Cooperation

  • Economic coordination and trade.
  • Migration and labour mobility.
  • Security dialogue and cooperation.
  • Transport and communication links.
  • Cultural, educational and people-to-people exchange.

12.2 Importance for India

The CIS is relevant mainly through India's relations with Russia, Central Asia and the wider Eurasian region. India seeks access to energy resources, markets, transport routes and strategic partnerships in the region.

Important distinction: The CIS should not be confused with the Eurasian Economic Union (EAEU), which is a separate economic integration organisation with a different membership and institutional framework.

13. Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO)

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation evolved from the Shanghai Five mechanism and was established in 2001. It provides a platform for cooperation on regional security, counter-terrorism, economic engagement and political dialogue across Eurasia.

13.1 Objectives

  • Strengthen mutual trust and good-neighbourly relations.
  • Cooperate against terrorism, separatism and extremism within the organisation's framework.
  • Promote regional security and stability.
  • Expand economic, transport and connectivity cooperation.
  • Encourage cultural, educational and people-to-people exchanges.

13.2 Institutional Features

  • Council of Heads of State: The highest decision-making body.
  • Council of Heads of Government: Focuses on economic and other practical cooperation.
  • Secretariat: Located in Beijing, China.
  • Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS): Based in Tashkent, Uzbekistan, and coordinates cooperation against terrorism, separatism and extremism.

13.3 India's Participation

India became a full member in 2017. Its participation provides access to a major Eurasian forum involving Central Asian States, Russia, China and other members.

13.4 Importance for India

  • Engagement with Central Asia and Eurasia.
  • Regional counter-terrorism dialogue.
  • Opportunities for energy and connectivity cooperation.
  • Participation in a forum that includes major continental powers.

13.5 Challenges

  • Strategic differences with China.
  • Different approaches to connectivity and regional security.
  • Concerns about the political use of security terminology.
  • Need to balance engagement with India's wider partnerships.

For detailed treatment of India's Eurasian and regional security engagement, see the chapter on regional organisations.

14. Quad

14.1 Origin and Evolution

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad) brings together India, Australia, Japan and the United States. Its origins lie in cooperation following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. It was revived in 2017 amid growing interest in a free, open and inclusive Indo-Pacific.

14.2 Nature and Objectives

The Quad is a diplomatic and strategic partnership rather than a formal military alliance. It does not have a mutual-defence treaty or a permanent integrated military command.

  • Promote a free, open, inclusive and rules-based Indo-Pacific.
  • Strengthen maritime security and regional stability.
  • Improve supply-chain resilience and economic security.
  • Promote critical and emerging technology cooperation.
  • Strengthen infrastructure, connectivity and quality investment.
  • Cooperate on climate change, health and disaster response.

14.3 Important Areas of Cooperation

  • Maritime domain awareness: Information-sharing and capacity-building to improve awareness of maritime activity.
  • Supply-chain resilience: Diversification and cooperation to reduce vulnerabilities in critical supply chains.
  • Critical technologies: Cooperation in telecommunications, semiconductors, artificial intelligence and emerging technologies.
  • Infrastructure: Support for quality, sustainable and transparent infrastructure.
  • Health: Cooperation on health security and access to medical goods.
  • Climate: Clean energy, resilience and environmental cooperation.

14.4 Significance for India

  • Strengthens India's role in Indo-Pacific diplomacy.
  • Supports maritime security and freedom of navigation.
  • Provides opportunities for advanced technology and supply-chain cooperation.
  • Allows India to work with major partners without entering a formal military alliance.
  • Complements India's Act East Policy and broader Indo-Pacific engagement.

14.5 Challenges

  • Different strategic priorities among members.
  • Need to avoid being perceived as an exclusive anti-China military bloc.
  • Limited institutionalisation and reliance on national implementation.
  • Balancing maritime security with economic interdependence in the region.

Important distinction: The Quad is not NATO and does not impose a collective-defence obligation on its members.

15. AUKUS

15.1 Origin and Membership

AUKUS is a trilateral security partnership announced in September 2021 by Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States. It seeks to strengthen security and technological cooperation in the Indo-Pacific.

15.2 Two Pillars

  • Pillar I: Cooperation to support Australia's acquisition of conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines, together with associated industrial, technological and workforce arrangements.
  • Pillar II: Advanced capability cooperation in artificial intelligence, quantum technologies, cyber capabilities, undersea systems, electronic warfare and hypersonic-related technologies.

15.3 Strategic Significance

  • Strengthens defence and technological cooperation among three security partners.
  • Reflects the growing strategic importance of the Indo-Pacific.
  • May influence regional military balances and maritime security calculations.
  • Creates opportunities for advanced defence-industrial and technology cooperation.

15.4 Implications for India

  • Contributes to the broader strategic environment in which India pursues its Indo-Pacific interests.
  • May complement cooperation among several partners, including countries participating in the Quad, without being institutionally linked to the Quad.
  • Raises questions about regional militarisation, strategic stability and the nuclear non-proliferation framework.

Important distinction: AUKUS is a security and advanced-technology partnership. It is not a collective-defence treaty identical to NATO, and India is not a member.

16. I2U2 Grouping

16.1 Membership and Origin

I2U2 brings together India, Israel, the United Arab Emirates and the United States. The grouping emerged from earlier diplomatic cooperation and was formalised at the leaders' level in 2022.

It is sometimes described as the West Asian Quad, but it is institutionally distinct from the Indo-Pacific Quad.

16.2 Areas of Cooperation

  • Food security and agricultural innovation.
  • Renewable energy and clean technology.
  • Transport and logistics infrastructure.
  • Water management and technology.
  • Investment and private-sector cooperation.
  • Health, space and other emerging sectors.

16.3 Significance for India

  • Connects India's economic interests with the Gulf, Israel and the United States.
  • Can support investment in food parks, renewable energy and logistics.
  • Creates opportunities for agricultural technology and water-management cooperation.
  • Strengthens India's engagement with West Asia beyond traditional energy and diaspora issues.

16.4 Limitations

Implementation depends on commercially viable projects, political stability, investment conditions and cooperation among participating governments and private-sector entities.

17. Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEF)

17.1 Origin and Nature

IPEF was launched in May 2022 by the United States and regional partners. It is an economic cooperation framework for the Indo-Pacific, not a conventional free trade agreement.

India participates in the framework but has not joined its Trade Pillar.

17.2 Four Pillars

  • Trade: Cooperation on trade-related issues, including digital trade, labour, environment and regulatory practices. India has not joined this pillar.
  • Supply Chains: Cooperation to strengthen supply-chain resilience, identify vulnerabilities and improve crisis response.
  • Clean Economy: Cooperation on clean energy, climate technologies, emissions reduction and sustainable investment.
  • Fair Economy: Cooperation on anti-corruption, tax transparency and improving the business environment.

17.3 Economic Significance

  • Supports diversification of regional supply chains.
  • Encourages investment in clean energy and emerging technologies.
  • Promotes cooperation on economic security and transparency.
  • Creates opportunities for India to engage with Indo-Pacific economies.

17.4 India's Position

India has participated in the Supply Chains, Clean Economy and Fair Economy pillars while remaining outside the Trade Pillar. This reflects India's interest in functional cooperation alongside caution about certain trade-related commitments and policy implications.

Important distinction: IPEF does not provide the same tariff-reduction and market-access structure as a conventional free trade agreement.

18. India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor (IMEC)

18.1 Origin and Purpose

IMEC was announced through a memorandum of understanding on the sidelines of the G20 New Delhi Summit in September 2023. It is intended to improve connectivity between India, the Middle East and Europe.

18.2 Proposed Components

  • Eastern corridor: Intended to connect India with the Arabian Gulf region through maritime routes.
  • Northern corridor: Intended to connect the Gulf region with Europe through rail and other transport links.
  • Logistics infrastructure: Ports, railways, shipping and multimodal connectivity.
  • Digital connectivity: Potential digital infrastructure links.
  • Energy connectivity: Proposed cooperation involving electricity and clean-energy infrastructure, including potential hydrogen-related links.

18.3 Economic Significance for India

  • Potentially reduces transport time and logistics costs.
  • Improves India's connectivity with Gulf and European markets.
  • Creates opportunities for trade, investment and industrial cooperation.
  • Can strengthen supply-chain diversification and regional economic integration.
  • Supports India's role as a connectivity partner across the Indian Ocean and West Asia.

18.4 Challenges

  • Complex coordination among multiple countries and institutions.
  • Political instability and regional conflicts.
  • Financing, construction and regulatory harmonisation.
  • Interoperability of rail, port, customs and logistics systems.
  • Need for commercially viable cargo flows and sustained political commitment.

Important distinction: IMEC is a proposed connectivity and economic corridor initiative, not an operational free trade agreement.

19. Supply Chain Resilience Initiative (SCRI)

The Supply Chain Resilience Initiative was launched in 2021 by India, Japan and Australia to promote resilient and diversified supply chains in the Indo-Pacific.

19.1 Objectives

  • Reduce excessive dependence on concentrated sources of critical supplies.
  • Promote investment diversification and production networks.
  • Strengthen business linkages and information-sharing.
  • Improve resilience against natural disasters, geopolitical disruptions and economic shocks.
  • Encourage regional cooperation in manufacturing and logistics.

19.2 Importance for India

  • Supports manufacturing diversification and domestic industrial capacity.
  • Creates opportunities to attract investment from Japan and Australia.
  • Strengthens cooperation in electronics, pharmaceuticals and other critical sectors.
  • Complements India's efforts to improve logistics and manufacturing competitiveness.

The initiative is distinct from a free trade agreement and relies on cooperation, investment and business participation.

20. Minerals Security Partnership (MSP)

The Minerals Security Partnership was launched in 2022 by a group of countries and the European Union to strengthen secure, sustainable and responsible supply chains for critical minerals.

20.1 Objectives

  • Promote responsible investment in critical mineral supply chains.
  • Diversify sources of minerals required for clean energy and advanced technologies.
  • Improve environmental, social and governance standards.
  • Support processing, refining and recycling capacity.
  • Reduce vulnerabilities arising from excessive supply concentration.

20.2 Importance for India

  • Supports access to lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite and rare earth elements.
  • Strengthens the foundation for electric vehicles, batteries and renewable energy.
  • Can support India's semiconductor, electronics and defence-industrial ambitions.
  • Creates opportunities for overseas mineral partnerships and domestic processing capacity.

20.3 Challenges

Critical-mineral projects involve long investment cycles, environmental risks, complex local political conditions, processing concentration and competition among major economies. Responsible investment and transparent benefit-sharing are essential.

21. International Solar Alliance (ISA)

21.1 Origin and Objectives

The International Solar Alliance was launched by India and France at the 2015 Paris Climate Conference. Its headquarters are in India. It seeks to promote solar energy deployment and cooperation among solar-resource-rich countries and other participating members.

  • Mobilise investment for solar energy.
  • Reduce the cost of solar technologies and financing.
  • Promote solar applications in agriculture, public services and rural areas.
  • Strengthen capacity-building and technology cooperation.
  • Support universal energy access and climate action.

21.2 Importance for India

  • Strengthens India's international climate leadership.
  • Promotes solar deployment and affordable clean energy.
  • Supports development partnerships with Africa, Asia and island countries.
  • Creates opportunities for Indian solar technology, finance and project expertise.

22. Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI)

22.1 Origin and Objectives

CDRI was launched by India at the 2019 UN Climate Action Summit. It is a global partnership of national governments, international organisations and other stakeholders seeking to strengthen the resilience of infrastructure systems against disasters and climate risks.

  • Promote disaster-resilient infrastructure.
  • Support knowledge exchange and technical cooperation.
  • Improve risk assessment and resilient infrastructure planning.
  • Mobilise investment and capacity-building.
  • Strengthen the resilience of transport, energy, communication and urban infrastructure.

22.2 Importance for India

  • Builds on India's experience in disaster risk reduction.
  • Strengthens international cooperation in resilient infrastructure.
  • Supports the interests of vulnerable developing countries and island States.
  • Promotes integration of climate and disaster risks into infrastructure planning.

23. Global Biofuels Alliance (GBA)

The Global Biofuels Alliance was launched during India's G20 Presidency in September 2023. It seeks to promote international cooperation in sustainable biofuels.

23.1 Objectives

  • Promote the use of sustainable biofuels.
  • Facilitate technology cooperation and knowledge-sharing.
  • Develop common standards and certification approaches.
  • Encourage investment and capacity-building.
  • Support energy diversification and emissions reduction.

23.2 Importance for India

  • Supports ethanol blending and domestic biofuel development.
  • Can reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels.
  • Creates opportunities for agricultural waste utilisation and rural income generation.
  • Strengthens India's role in clean-energy diplomacy.

Challenge: Biofuel development must account for food security, water use, land use, biodiversity and the sustainability of feedstocks.

24. OPEC and OPEC+

24.1 Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)

OPEC is an intergovernmental organisation established in 1960. Its founding members were Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. Its headquarters are in Vienna, Austria.

OPEC seeks to coordinate petroleum policies among member countries and promote stability in international oil markets.

Major functions

  • Coordinate petroleum policies among participating members.
  • Monitor global oil-market developments.
  • Discuss production policies and market stability.
  • Represent the interests of oil-producing member countries.

24.2 OPEC+

OPEC+ refers to the wider cooperation framework between OPEC members and participating non-OPEC oil-producing countries. It is associated with the Declaration of Cooperation initiated in 2016.

OPEC+ is not simply another name for OPEC. It brings together OPEC and participating non-OPEC producers for consultations and coordinated production decisions.

24.3 Importance for India

  • India is a major importer of crude oil and is exposed to changes in international oil prices.
  • Production decisions can influence global supply, prices, inflation and India's import bill.
  • Higher oil prices can worsen the current account balance and raise fiscal pressures.
  • Lower prices may benefit consumers and importing economies but can affect producer-country finances and investment.
  • India must balance energy security, affordability, strategic reserves and diversification of suppliers.

Important distinction: India is not an OPEC member. OPEC is an organisation of petroleum-exporting countries; OPEC+ is a broader production-cooperation arrangement.

25. Regional Trade Agreements and Economic Frameworks

25.1 Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP)

RCEP is a regional free trade agreement signed on 15 November 2020. Negotiations were launched in 2012 under ASEAN's leadership. It entered into force in 2022 and has 15 participating countries: the ten ASEAN members and Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand and the Republic of Korea.

Major Areas Covered

  • Trade in goods and tariff reduction.
  • Rules of origin and regional cumulation.
  • Trade in services and investment.
  • Customs procedures and trade facilitation.
  • Electronic commerce and intellectual property.
  • Competition, economic and technical cooperation.
  • Small and medium-sized enterprises.

Economic Significance

RCEP seeks to deepen regional production networks by enabling firms to source inputs from participating economies while meeting agreed rules of origin. It can facilitate cross-border supply chains in electronics, machinery, textiles, automotive components and other sectors.

Why India Did Not Join

India withdrew from RCEP negotiations in November 2019 after raising concerns about the likely effects of the agreement on domestic industry, agriculture, dairy producers and trade balances.

  • Import competition: Concerns about increased imports from highly competitive manufacturing economies.
  • Trade imbalance: Concerns about expansion of trade deficits, particularly with China.
  • Rules of origin: Concerns about goods entering through one member economy after undergoing limited processing elsewhere.
  • Domestic industry: Concerns about adjustment capacity of small manufacturers and vulnerable sectors.
  • Policy space: Concerns about the ability to respond to import surges and protect sensitive sectors.

Implications for India

  • India remains outside a major regional trade framework covering East Asian and Pacific economies.
  • Indian exporters may face relative disadvantages where competitors receive preferential market access.
  • India must improve domestic competitiveness, logistics, standards and trade facilitation.
  • India can continue to pursue bilateral and alternative regional economic partnerships.

Prelims fact: RCEP is not the same as ASEAN. ASEAN is a regional organisation; RCEP is a free trade agreement involving ASEAN and five other participating countries.

25.2 Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP)

The CPTPP is a high-standard regional free trade agreement signed in March 2018. It evolved from the Trans-Pacific Partnership after the United States withdrew from the original TPP framework.

Major Areas Covered

  • Tariff reduction and market access in goods.
  • Services trade and investment.
  • Digital trade and electronic commerce.
  • Intellectual property protection.
  • Government procurement.
  • Competition policy and state-owned enterprises.
  • Labour and environmental commitments.
  • Rules governing investment and dispute settlement.

Economic Significance

The CPTPP represents an ambitious model of trade integration that extends beyond conventional tariff concessions. It seeks to establish common rules for trade, investment, digital commerce and regulatory practices.

India's Position

India is not a member. CPTPP membership would require assessment of its rules on market access, intellectual property, investment, state-owned enterprises, digital trade and domestic regulatory policy.

25.3 RCEP and CPTPP: Comparison

  • RCEP is ASEAN-led and includes China; India is not a member.
  • CPTPP is a separate high-standard trade agreement across the Asia-Pacific and other regions; India is not a member.
  • Both aim to deepen trade and investment integration but differ in membership, rules and regulatory ambition.
  • Neither should be confused with APEC, which is a voluntary economic cooperation forum rather than a free trade agreement.

26. Trade and Economic Issues Connecting Global Groupings

26.1 Global Value Chains

Global value chains involve the distribution of different stages of production across countries. A product may be designed in one country, use components from several others, be assembled elsewhere and sold globally.

Groupings and trade agreements influence the location of production through tariffs, rules of origin, standards, investment conditions and logistics connectivity.

For India, deeper participation in global value chains requires competitive manufacturing, reliable infrastructure, skilled labour, predictable regulation and access to markets.

26.2 Supply-Chain Resilience

Concentration of production in a limited number of countries can create vulnerabilities during pandemics, wars, natural disasters, trade restrictions or geopolitical crises.

  • Diversification of suppliers and production locations.
  • Strategic reserves for critical goods.
  • Domestic capacity in selected strategic sectors.
  • Improved logistics and trade facilitation.
  • Cooperation on critical minerals, semiconductors and pharmaceuticals.

26.3 Tariff and Non-Tariff Barriers

Tariffs are customs duties imposed on imports or exports. Non-tariff barriers include technical standards, licensing requirements, quotas, sanitary and phytosanitary measures, local-content requirements and complex customs procedures.

Such measures may pursue legitimate public objectives, including health, safety and environmental protection, but can also restrict market access when designed or applied in a discriminatory manner.

26.4 Rules of Origin

Rules of origin determine the economic nationality of a product for purposes such as preferential tariff treatment, trade remedies and trade statistics.

  • They prevent goods from non-member countries from receiving preferences through simple trans-shipment.
  • They may require a specified level of local or regional value addition.
  • Regional cumulation allows inputs from multiple agreement members to count towards origin requirements, subject to agreement rules.
  • Complex rules can raise compliance costs for small exporters.

26.5 Protectionism and Economic Security

Countries increasingly use tariffs, subsidies, export controls, investment screening and industrial policies to protect strategic sectors. These measures can strengthen resilience but may also create trade tensions and fragment global markets.

India must balance strategic industrial capacity with openness, competitiveness, predictable trade policy and its obligations under international agreements.

26.6 Critical Minerals and Energy Transition

Clean-energy technologies depend on minerals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, copper, graphite and rare earth elements. Their extraction and processing are geographically concentrated and often associated with environmental and social concerns.

Partnerships such as the Minerals Security Partnership and cooperation through the G20, Quad and other frameworks can help diversify sources, improve responsible investment and develop recycling capacity.

26.7 India's Trade Policy Priorities

  • Diversify export markets and import sources.
  • Improve manufacturing productivity and export competitiveness.
  • Strengthen logistics, ports, customs and trade facilitation.
  • Expand participation in global value chains.
  • Negotiate trade agreements that balance market access with domestic policy priorities.
  • Improve standards, certification and quality infrastructure.
  • Develop domestic capacity in critical minerals, semiconductors, pharmaceuticals and clean technologies.
  • Ensure that trade and investment policy supports employment, sustainability and inclusive growth.

28. India's Strategic Approach to Global Groupings

28.1 Strategic Autonomy and Multi-Alignment

India participates in multiple groupings with different memberships and purposes. This enables it to cooperate with different powers on specific issues without becoming permanently aligned with a single geopolitical bloc.

28.2 Global South Leadership

India seeks to articulate the concerns of developing countries on development finance, debt, food security, climate justice, technology access and international institutional reform.

28.3 Economic Diplomacy

India uses international platforms to attract investment, diversify trade, improve connectivity, strengthen supply chains and gain access to technology.

28.4 Indo-Pacific Engagement

India works through the Quad, ASEAN-led mechanisms, IORA, IPEF and other arrangements to promote maritime security, connectivity, economic cooperation and regional stability.

28.5 Development Partnerships

Initiatives such as ISA, CDRI and GBA support India's development diplomacy and its role in providing international public goods.

28.6 Balancing Competing Interests

India must manage relationships with countries that may cooperate in one forum and compete in another. It should preserve decision-making autonomy while building practical coalitions on issues of common interest.

28.7 Domestic Capacity as the Foundation

International participation is effective only when supported by domestic competitiveness, strong institutions, technological capability, infrastructure, skilled labour and credible implementation.

29. Challenges Facing Global Groupings

  • Geopolitical rivalry: Strategic competition can undermine trust and consensus.
  • Unequal power: Larger economies may dominate agenda-setting and negotiations.
  • Institutional fragmentation: Overlapping groupings can create duplication and competing standards.
  • Implementation deficit: Political declarations may not translate into projects or domestic reforms.
  • Trade protectionism: Tariffs, subsidies and export controls can fragment markets.
  • Debt and financial instability: Vulnerable economies may lack access to affordable finance.
  • Climate inequality: Disagreements persist over historical responsibility, finance and technology transfer.
  • Digital divide: Differences in technological capacity can reinforce global inequality.
  • Supply-chain concentration: Dependence on a limited number of suppliers creates strategic vulnerabilities.
  • Legitimacy and representation: Many informal forums do not adequately represent smaller and vulnerable countries.

30. Way Forward

  1. Strengthen transparency, accountability and follow-up mechanisms in international groupings.
  2. Promote inclusive decision-making and greater representation of developing countries.
  3. Improve coordination among overlapping institutions and initiatives.
  4. Support an open, rules-based and predictable international trading system.
  5. Strengthen resilient and diversified supply chains while avoiding unnecessary protectionism.
  6. Promote affordable climate finance and technology cooperation.
  7. Make debt-resolution mechanisms more predictable and effective.
  8. Encourage responsible investment in critical minerals and clean-energy technologies.
  9. Ensure that connectivity initiatives respect sovereignty, sustainability and local development priorities.
  10. Strengthen domestic institutions and productive capacity so that international commitments translate into national benefits.
