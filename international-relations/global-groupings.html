Global Groupings and India's Strategic Interests

International groupings increasingly shape the rules, institutions and informal arrangements governing global security, trade, finance, technology, energy, climate action and development. For India, they provide platforms to influence international decision-making, build partnerships, diversify economic relationships and protect strategic autonomy.

These groupings are not all the same. Some are political consultation forums, some are regional organisations, some are military or security partnerships, some negotiate trade rules, and others finance infrastructure or coordinate commodity production. Their legal status, membership, decision-making procedures and practical influence differ considerably.

1. Understanding International Groupings

1.1 Why countries form groupings

States form international groupings because many contemporary challenges cannot be managed effectively by individual countries. Financial crises, pandemics, climate change, maritime insecurity, terrorism, supply-chain disruptions and technological competition cross national borders.

Major purposes include:

  • Political coordination: developing common positions on international issues and reforming global institutions.
  • Economic cooperation: facilitating trade, investment, infrastructure, financial stability and development.
  • Security cooperation: addressing terrorism, maritime threats, cyber risks and regional instability.
  • Technology partnerships: cooperating in critical technologies, digital infrastructure, semiconductors and artificial intelligence.
  • Energy security: diversifying energy sources, developing clean energy and managing commodity-market volatility.
  • Collective bargaining: improving the negotiating position of countries that may have limited influence individually.
  • Norm and standard setting: influencing international rules on taxation, trade, data, environmental protection and emerging technologies.

1.2 Different institutional forms

The word "grouping" is often used loosely. It is important to distinguish the institutional character of different arrangements. Membership in a forum does not automatically create legally binding obligations, and participation in a strategic partnership does not necessarily mean membership in a military alliance.

Form Characteristics Examples
Intergovernmental organisation Created through an international agreement or charter, generally with established organs and procedures. United Nations, ASEAN, African Union, SCO
Informal diplomatic forum Facilitates consultation and coordination; outcomes are often political commitments rather than enforceable law. G20, G7, BRICS, IBSA
Security partnership or alliance Provides defence cooperation, strategic coordination or commitments relating to security. AUKUS; formal military alliances have different legal obligations
Free trade agreement Reduces or removes specified trade barriers among participating economies under agreed rules. RCEP, CPTPP, ASEAN-India Trade in Goods Agreement
Economic framework Coordinates economic cooperation without necessarily creating a conventional free trade agreement. IPEF
Development finance institution Provides loans, guarantees, technical assistance or other financial support for development. New Development Bank, AIIB, Asian Development Bank
Infrastructure or cooperation initiative Promotes specific projects or cooperation in selected sectors. IMEC, International Solar Alliance, CDRI
Commodity-producer coordination Coordinates policies among producers of a particular commodity, potentially affecting global prices. OPEC and OPEC+

1.3 How to evaluate a grouping

Its importance should not be judged only by the number of members or the frequency of summits. A useful assessment considers:

  • Whether it has a founding treaty, charter or memorandum of understanding.
  • Whether it has a permanent secretariat, headquarters and dedicated budget.
  • Whether decisions are binding or based on consensus and voluntary implementation.
  • Whether members possess compatible political and economic interests.
  • Whether it has institutions capable of implementing decisions.
  • Whether it has produced measurable outcomes in trade, security, finance or development.
  • Whether it complements or competes with existing multilateral institutions.

2. G20: Global Economic Coordination

2.1 Origin and evolution

The Group of Twenty was established in 1999 in the aftermath of the Asian financial crisis. It initially brought together finance ministers and central bank governors to improve consultation between major advanced and emerging economies.

The global financial crisis of 2008 demonstrated the need for coordination at the highest political level. The G20 leaders' process consequently became central to international economic cooperation. It is now a major forum for discussing global economic stability, development, financial regulation and other cross-border issues.

2.2 Membership and institutional character

The G20 consists of 19 countries, the European Union and the African Union. Its membership brings together advanced economies and major emerging economies. It is not a treaty-based international organisation with a permanent secretariat.

The presidency rotates among members. The presidency works with the previous and succeeding presidencies through the troika arrangement, helping maintain continuity in the agenda.

2.3 Main working processes

  • Finance Track: finance ministers and central bank governors discuss macroeconomic coordination, financial regulation, international taxation, debt, infrastructure finance and financial stability.
  • Sherpa Track: senior representatives coordinate the broader political agenda, development priorities, food and energy security, climate-related issues and institutional reform.
  • Working groups: technical groups examine specific policy areas and develop recommendations.
  • Engagement groups: platforms such as business, civil society, labour, think-tank, women and youth engagement groups provide stakeholder perspectives. Their recommendations do not automatically become binding G20 decisions.

2.4 Major areas of work

  • Global financial stability and macroeconomic coordination.
  • Reform of international financial institutions and multilateral development banks.
  • Debt sustainability and financing for developing countries.
  • International taxation and cooperation against tax avoidance.
  • Food security, agricultural resilience and fertiliser supply.
  • Energy security, clean energy and sustainable development.
  • Digital public infrastructure, digital inclusion and responsible technology.
  • Climate finance and the mobilisation of private capital.
  • Global health cooperation and pandemic preparedness.

2.5 India's G20 Presidency, 2023

India's presidency placed particular emphasis on inclusive, development-oriented and consensus-based global cooperation. The theme was "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam", reflecting the idea of One Earth, One Family, One Future.

Important outcomes and priorities included:

  • Admission of the African Union as a permanent G20 member, strengthening the representation of developing countries.
  • The New Delhi Leaders' Declaration, adopted by consensus.
  • Emphasis on reforming multilateral development banks to increase their lending capacity and improve their ability to address global challenges.
  • Greater attention to debt vulnerabilities and the financing needs of developing economies.
  • Recognition of digital public infrastructure as a tool for financial inclusion and public-service delivery.
  • Support for the development of the Global Biofuels Alliance.
  • Promotion of sustainable lifestyles and the LiFE approach.
  • Greater attention to food security, climate finance, energy transitions and disaster resilience.
  • Announcement of the India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor by participating partners.

2.6 Importance for India

  • Provides a platform to influence global economic governance despite India not being a permanent member of the UN Security Council.
  • Allows India to articulate the concerns of developing economies on debt, climate finance, food security and technology access.
  • Strengthens India's profile as a bridge between advanced and developing economies.
  • Creates opportunities to promote digital public infrastructure, renewable energy and development partnerships.
  • Supports India's demand for more representative and effective multilateral institutions.

2.7 Limitations

  • Its decisions are generally political commitments, not directly enforceable international law.
  • Geopolitical rivalry can prevent consensus on conflicts, sanctions and security questions.
  • Implementation depends on individual members and domestic political priorities.
  • Differences between developed and developing economies persist over climate finance, trade and development responsibilities.

Examination perspective: The G20 illustrates the importance of informal multilateralism. It is influential because of the economic weight of its members, but it cannot replace universal institutions such as the United Nations or WTO.

3. BRICS: Emerging Economies and Global Governance Reform

3.1 Origin and development

The term BRIC originated as an economic grouping of Brazil, Russia, India and China. The countries began formal political coordination in 2006, and their first leaders' summit took place in 2009. South Africa joined in 2010, producing the BRICS name.

BRICS subsequently expanded. Its expanded membership includes Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, the United Arab Emirates, Indonesia and Saudi Arabia. Membership and participation arrangements should be distinguished from invitations, partnership status and expressions of interest.

3.2 Nature and objectives

BRICS is a political and diplomatic coordination forum, not a military alliance or a conventional free trade area. It does not operate as a single economic bloc with a common external tariff.

Its central objectives include:

  • Reforming global financial and political institutions to reflect contemporary economic realities.
  • Increasing the voice and representation of emerging and developing economies.
  • Promoting South-South cooperation.
  • Strengthening economic, financial, scientific and technological cooperation.
  • Encouraging trade and investment in national currencies where mutually feasible.
  • Cooperating on development, health, energy, agriculture and climate-related priorities.

3.3 Three broad pillars

  1. Political and security cooperation: dialogue on international peace, security, global governance and reform of multilateral institutions.
  2. Economic and financial cooperation: trade, investment, development finance, financial cooperation and economic resilience.
  3. People-to-people cooperation: education, research, culture, science, technology and institutional exchanges.

3.4 Institutional mechanisms

  • Leaders' Summit: provides political direction and adopts declarations.
  • Ministerial meetings: facilitate coordination in areas such as foreign affairs, finance, trade and other sectors.
  • New Development Bank: finances infrastructure and sustainable development projects in member countries and other eligible economies.
  • Contingent Reserve Arrangement: provides a framework for liquidity support in response to balance-of-payments pressures, subject to its rules and operational conditions.
  • Business and sectoral mechanisms: encourage cooperation among businesses, financial institutions, researchers and public agencies.

3.5 New Development Bank

The New Development Bank was established by BRICS countries to mobilise resources for infrastructure and sustainable development. Its establishment reflected dissatisfaction with the limited representation of emerging economies in older development-finance institutions.

Its significance includes:

  • Financing infrastructure, clean energy, transport, water and other development priorities.
  • Expanding development-finance options for emerging economies.
  • Encouraging financing in local currencies where feasible.
  • Supporting projects that may not receive sufficient financing from traditional sources.

The NDB is a multilateral development bank, not a trade agreement and not the same institution as the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank.

3.6 Importance for India

  • Provides a platform for advocating reform of the IMF, World Bank and other multilateral institutions.
  • Supports India's demand for greater representation of developing countries in global decision-making.
  • Offers access to development finance through the NDB.
  • Creates opportunities for cooperation in energy, technology, agriculture and infrastructure.
  • Strengthens India's engagement with emerging economies across Asia, Africa and Latin America.

3.7 Challenges and India's balancing strategy

  • Internal differences: members have different political systems, economic structures and foreign-policy priorities.
  • China's economic weight: differences in economic size can create concerns about unequal influence.
  • India-China tensions: border and strategic differences limit the depth of cooperation.
  • Russia-West tensions: geopolitical disagreements can complicate common positions.
  • Expansion: a larger membership can increase representation but make consensus more difficult.
  • Institutional limitations: the grouping does not have the authority or institutional machinery of a supranational organisation.

India should use BRICS to promote multipolarity and development cooperation without allowing it to become an exclusive anti-Western platform. India's participation in BRICS can coexist with its engagement with the United States, Europe, Japan and other partners.

Prelims distinction: BRICS is not a military alliance, does not have a common currency, and is not a conventional free trade area. The NDB and Contingent Reserve Arrangement are separate institutional mechanisms associated with BRICS cooperation.

4. G7 and OECD: Advanced-Economy Coordination and Global Standards

4.1 Group of Seven

The G7 developed from economic coordination among major advanced industrial democracies during the 1970s. Its members are Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States. The European Union participates in G7 work but is not counted as one of the seven member countries.

Its agenda includes:

  • Global economic coordination and financial stability.
  • Energy security and critical mineral supply chains.
  • Sanctions and responses to international conflicts.
  • Climate policy, development assistance and infrastructure.
  • Technology governance, cybersecurity and economic security.
  • Coordination on global health and food security.

The G7 has no supranational authority. Its declarations and political commitments influence global policy because its members possess significant economic, technological and financial power.

4.2 India's relationship with the G7

India is not a G7 member, but it has participated in outreach engagements and discussions with G7 leaders. Its participation provides opportunities to discuss global economic governance, climate finance, technology, supply chains and the concerns of developing economies.

India's engagement is also important because the G7's decisions on export controls, sanctions, energy markets, technology standards and financial regulation can have consequences beyond its membership.

4.3 Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development

The OECD is an intergovernmental organisation that promotes evidence-based public policy, economic cooperation and the development of international standards. It evolved from the post-war European economic cooperation framework and was formally established in 1961.

Its work includes:

  • Economic policy analysis and country reviews.
  • Tax policy, international tax cooperation and action against base erosion and profit shifting.
  • Education indicators and the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA).
  • Investment policy, responsible business conduct and corporate governance.
  • Competition policy, public integrity and regulatory quality.
  • Trade, productivity, employment and development policy.

4.4 OECD and international taxation

The OECD has played an important role in coordinating international discussions on tax avoidance by multinational enterprises. Its Base Erosion and Profit Shifting project addresses strategies that shift profits away from the jurisdictions where economic activity occurs.

The OECD/G20 Inclusive Framework has facilitated discussions on international tax reform, including the two-pillar approach. These debates matter to India because they affect the taxation of multinational enterprises, digital business models, investment flows and the distribution of taxing rights between countries.

4.5 India's engagement and concerns

  • India participates in several OECD-related policy and standard-setting processes, including international tax discussions.
  • OECD data and policy research provide comparative evidence for Indian policymaking.
  • India must ensure that international standards reflect the interests of developing economies.
  • Tax and regulatory standards must balance investment attractiveness with revenue protection and domestic policy autonomy.

Important distinction: The G7 is a political coordination forum of advanced economies. The OECD is a standards-oriented intergovernmental organisation with a broader policy and research role. Neither is a global government.

5. IBSA, G77 and Non-Aligned Movement

5.1 IBSA Dialogue Forum

IBSA brings together India, Brazil and South Africa. It was established in 2003 to strengthen cooperation among three large democratic countries from Asia, South America and Africa.

Its objectives include:

  • Promoting South-South cooperation and development partnerships.
  • Advocating reform of global governance institutions.
  • Cooperating in agriculture, health, education, energy, science and technology.
  • Strengthening democratic and pluralistic approaches to international affairs.

The IBSA Fund supports development projects in partner developing countries, particularly in areas such as poverty reduction, livelihood support, health and capacity building.

IBSA is distinct from BRICS. IBSA has a more specific identity based on cooperation among India, Brazil and South Africa, while BRICS has a broader membership and a more extensive economic and geopolitical agenda.

5.2 Group of 77 and China

The Group of 77 was established in 1964 by developing countries seeking greater influence in international economic negotiations. It operates mainly as a coalition within the United Nations system.

Its priorities include:

  • Fairer international economic relations.
  • Development finance and technology transfer.
  • Reform of international financial and trade arrangements.
  • Climate finance and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities.
  • Greater policy space for developing countries.

China is associated with the coalition's positions and is commonly referred to in the diplomatic formulation "G77 and China", but China's status should not be confused with the membership of the developing-country coalition itself.

The G77 is important because it enables developing countries to coordinate negotiating positions in UN processes, particularly on development, climate and economic issues.

5.3 Non-Aligned Movement

The Non-Aligned Movement emerged during the Cold War as countries sought to avoid formal alignment with either of the two competing power blocs. Its first summit was held in Belgrade in 1961.

India, under Jawaharlal Nehru, played a leading role in shaping the movement alongside leaders from other newly independent countries.

Its historical principles include:

  • Respect for sovereignty and territorial integrity.
  • Non-interference in internal affairs.
  • Peaceful coexistence.
  • Opposition to colonialism and racial discrimination.
  • Independent foreign-policy decision-making.
  • Support for international peace and disarmament.

5.4 Contemporary relevance

The Cold War's bipolar structure has ended, but the central concern of independent decision-making remains relevant. India's contemporary strategic autonomy is not identical to the historical policy of non-alignment, but both emphasise the ability to make decisions according to national interests.

Strategic autonomy does not mean neutrality on every international issue. It means retaining the ability to cooperate with different partners without accepting automatic alignment with one power.

6. Commonwealth and African Union

6.1 Commonwealth of Nations

The Commonwealth is a voluntary association of independent countries, many of which have historical links with the British Empire. It is based on shared commitments expressed in the Commonwealth Charter, including democracy, human rights, development and the rule of law.

Its main institutions and activities include:

  • Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting: provides political direction and a forum for leaders.
  • Commonwealth Secretariat: supports cooperation, technical assistance and institutional work.
  • Commonwealth Ministerial Action Group: addresses serious or persistent violations of Commonwealth values.
  • Technical cooperation: supports public administration, elections, legal reform, education and capacity building.

India is an important member. The Commonwealth is not a customs union, a military alliance or a common market. Its value lies primarily in political dialogue, development cooperation, institutional links and people-to-people relationships.

6.2 African Union

The African Union was launched in 2002, succeeding the Organisation of African Unity. It seeks to promote African integration, peace and security, economic development and a stronger collective voice for the continent.

Important features include:

  • Assembly of the Union: the principal decision-making body of heads of state and government.
  • Executive Council: coordinates policy matters among member states.
  • African Union Commission: performs administrative and coordination functions.
  • Peace and Security Council: deals with conflict prevention, management and resolution.
  • Agenda 2063: the long-term framework for Africa's transformation and integration.
  • African Continental Free Trade Area: seeks to create a more integrated African market.

6.3 African Continental Free Trade Area

The AfCFTA aims to reduce barriers to intra-African trade and improve the movement of goods and services across participating African economies. It is also intended to encourage industrial development, regional value chains and investment.

Its economic importance includes:

  • Reducing fragmented national markets.
  • Creating opportunities for economies of scale.
  • Supporting regional manufacturing and processing.
  • Improving the prospects for intra-African supply chains.
  • Increasing Africa's collective bargaining power in global trade.

6.4 India's engagement with Africa

  • Development partnerships, lines of credit and capacity-building programmes.
  • Cooperation in agriculture, health, education, information technology and infrastructure.
  • Energy and mineral-resource partnerships.
  • Support for African representation in global governance, including the African Union's inclusion in the G20.
  • People-to-people ties, diaspora connections and educational cooperation.

India's approach should combine development partnership with commercial engagement, local capacity building and respect for African priorities rather than treating Africa merely as a source of raw materials.

7. Shanghai Cooperation Organisation

7.1 Origin and evolution

The Shanghai Cooperation Organisation was established in 2001, building on earlier cooperation among China, Russia and Central Asian countries. Its original concerns included border confidence building, regional stability and security cooperation.

The organisation subsequently expanded its membership and developed a broader agenda covering political, economic and security cooperation across Eurasia. India and Pakistan became full members in 2017.

7.2 Main objectives

  • Maintaining regional peace, security and stability.
  • Combating terrorism, separatism and extremism.
  • Promoting economic, trade, transport and energy cooperation.
  • Encouraging cultural, educational and scientific exchanges.
  • Facilitating dialogue among Eurasian states.

7.3 Institutional mechanisms

  • Council of Heads of State: the highest decision-making body.
  • Council of Heads of Government: addresses economic and other cooperation matters.
  • Secretariat: based in Beijing and responsible for administrative support.
  • Regional Anti-Terrorist Structure (RATS): supports cooperation against terrorism, separatism and extremism.

7.4 India's interests

  • Engagement with Central Asian countries and access to Eurasian diplomatic platforms.
  • Counterterrorism cooperation and discussion of regional security.
  • Energy security and potential connectivity opportunities.
  • Dialogue on Afghanistan and regional stability.
  • Maintaining engagement with Russia, China and Central Asian states through a multilateral platform.

7.5 Challenges

  • India-China strategic and border tensions.
  • India-Pakistan disputes and differences over terrorism.
  • Competing regional connectivity projects and security priorities.
  • Concerns about the dominance of particular members.
  • Difficulty translating broad declarations into concrete economic outcomes.

India's participation should be understood as a means of retaining regional diplomatic access and pursuing specific interests, not as an endorsement of every member's policies.

8. ASEAN and ASEAN-Led Regional Frameworks

8.1 ASEAN: origin and membership

The Association of Southeast Asian Nations was established in 1967 through the Bangkok Declaration. Its original purpose was to promote regional cooperation, stability and economic development in Southeast Asia.

ASEAN has eleven members following the admission of Timor-Leste in October 2025. Its membership and institutional development have expanded considerably since its establishment.

8.2 ASEAN Charter and institutional structure

The ASEAN Charter, which entered into force in 2008, gave ASEAN a formal legal and institutional framework.

  • ASEAN Summit: the highest policy-making body, comprising leaders of member states.
  • ASEAN Coordinating Council: coordinates implementation of summit decisions and cross-pillar work.
  • ASEAN Community Councils: oversee the three community pillars.
  • ASEAN Secretariat: based in Jakarta and headed by the Secretary-General.
  • Sectoral ministerial bodies: facilitate cooperation in areas such as trade, defence, transport, education and environment.

8.3 Three pillars of the ASEAN Community

  1. Political-Security Community: promotes regional peace, dialogue, security cooperation and confidence building.
  2. Economic Community: seeks deeper economic integration, trade facilitation, investment and regional production networks.
  3. Socio-Cultural Community: promotes cooperation in education, public health, disaster management, environmental protection and social development.

8.4 ASEAN's operating principles

ASEAN traditionally emphasises consensus, consultation, sovereignty and non-interference. These principles have helped maintain dialogue among states with different political systems and levels of economic development.

However, consensus and non-interference can also make it difficult to respond quickly to internal political crises, human rights concerns and security disputes.

8.5 ASEAN-led forums

ASEAN Plus Three

ASEAN Plus Three brings ASEAN together with China, Japan and South Korea. It developed after the Asian financial crisis and supports cooperation in finance, food security, trade, health and regional economic resilience.

East Asia Summit

The East Asia Summit is a leaders-led forum for strategic dialogue on political, security and economic issues. It includes ASEAN members and major regional partners, including India, China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, Russia and the United States.

It is important to India because it provides a high-level platform for discussing Indo-Pacific security, maritime cooperation, connectivity and regional economic issues.

ASEAN Regional Forum

The ASEAN Regional Forum promotes dialogue and confidence building on security issues in the Asia-Pacific and Indo-Pacific region. It addresses matters such as maritime security, counterterrorism, non-proliferation and disaster relief.

ASEAN Defence Ministers' Meeting-Plus

The ADMM-Plus brings ASEAN defence ministers together with selected external partners to promote practical defence and security cooperation, including maritime security, humanitarian assistance and disaster relief.

8.6 India's relationship with ASEAN

India became a sectoral dialogue partner in 1992, a full dialogue partner in 1995 and a summit-level partner in 2002. The relationship has developed into a central component of India's Act East Policy.

Major areas of cooperation include:

  • Trade and investment, including the ASEAN-India trade agreements.
  • Connectivity through roads, ports, maritime routes and digital links.
  • Maritime security and freedom of navigation.
  • Defence exercises, capacity building and coast guard cooperation.
  • Education, tourism, culture and people-to-people relations.
  • Cooperation in pharmaceuticals, digital technology, agriculture and renewable energy.

8.7 Trade and supply-chain relevance

ASEAN is central to regional manufacturing and production networks. Many goods are produced through cross-border supply chains in which components, services and intermediate goods move between several economies before final assembly.

ASEAN-India economic cooperation can support India's participation in these networks, but challenges include logistics costs, non-tariff barriers, rules-of-origin concerns and trade imbalances.

Important distinction: ASEAN is a regional organisation. ASEAN Plus Three, the East Asia Summit, the ASEAN Regional Forum and ADMM-Plus are separate ASEAN-led cooperation frameworks, not additional ASEAN member organisations.

9. BIMSTEC, SAARC and Indian Ocean Rim Association

9.1 BIMSTEC

The Bay of Bengal Initiative for Multi-Sectoral Technical and Economic Cooperation connects countries around the Bay of Bengal. It provides a platform linking South Asia and Southeast Asia.

Its membership comprises Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar, Nepal, Sri Lanka and Thailand.

Its major areas of cooperation include:

  • Trade, investment and development.
  • Transport and regional connectivity.
  • Energy cooperation and power-grid connectivity.
  • Security and counterterrorism.
  • Disaster management and climate-related resilience.
  • Technology, agriculture, fisheries and public health.

BIMSTEC is strategically important because it connects India's northeastern region with Bangladesh, Myanmar and Thailand, while also creating opportunities for maritime cooperation in the Bay of Bengal.

Its potential is constrained by infrastructure gaps, political instability in parts of the region, weak trade facilitation and limited implementation capacity.

9.2 South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation

SAARC was established in 1985 to promote cooperation among South Asian countries. Its members are Afghanistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka.

Its objectives include:

  • Improving the welfare and quality of life of South Asian people.
  • Promoting economic growth and social development.
  • Encouraging regional cooperation in agriculture, health, education and culture.
  • Supporting cooperation in poverty alleviation and disaster management.

SAARC's institutional framework includes the Summit, Council of Ministers, Standing Committee, Programming Committee and Secretariat based in Kathmandu.

9.3 SAFTA

The South Asian Free Trade Area was designed to promote regional trade by reducing tariffs and other trade barriers among SAARC members. Its implementation has been limited by political tensions, sensitive lists, non-tariff barriers and inadequate connectivity.

9.4 Why SAARC has underperformed

  • India-Pakistan political tensions have repeatedly affected regional cooperation.
  • Intra-regional trade remains below its potential.
  • Transport, energy and digital connectivity are inadequate.
  • Trade barriers and restrictive procedures raise transaction costs.
  • Political differences have obstructed regular high-level engagement.

India has therefore pursued other regional arrangements, including BIMSTEC, while continuing to recognise the potential value of wider South Asian cooperation.

9.5 Indian Ocean Rim Association

The Indian Ocean Rim Association is a regional organisation promoting cooperation among countries bordering the Indian Ocean. Its agenda includes maritime safety and security, trade and investment, fisheries, disaster risk management, academic cooperation and the blue economy.

Its importance for India includes:

  • Securing sea lanes of communication and maritime trade routes.
  • Promoting cooperation against piracy, trafficking and illegal fishing.
  • Strengthening disaster preparedness and humanitarian assistance.
  • Developing sustainable fisheries and ocean-based economic activity.
  • Supporting India's SAGAR approach: Security and Growth for All in the Region.

These three organisations have different geographical and political purposes. BIMSTEC focuses on the Bay of Bengal, SAARC on South Asia, and IORA on the wider Indian Ocean region.

10. Quad: Strategic Cooperation in the Indo-Pacific

10.1 Origin and development

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue brings together India, the United States, Japan and Australia. Its early development was associated with cooperation following the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami. It was later revived as a strategic consultation mechanism and has developed into a leaders-level partnership.

10.2 Nature of the Quad

The Quad is not a treaty-based military alliance and does not create an automatic collective-defence obligation. It is a diplomatic and strategic partnership focused on cooperation across several Indo-Pacific priorities.

10.3 Main areas of cooperation

  • Maritime security: information sharing, maritime domain awareness and cooperation to improve the security of sea lanes.
  • Critical and emerging technologies: cooperation on resilient technology ecosystems, standards and research.
  • Supply-chain resilience: diversification and reduction of excessive dependence on concentrated sources of critical inputs.
  • Health security: pandemic preparedness, vaccine cooperation and public-health capacity.
  • Infrastructure: promotion of transparent, sustainable and high-quality infrastructure.
  • Climate and clean energy: renewable energy, clean technologies and climate resilience.
  • Cybersecurity: cooperation on secure digital infrastructure and cyber capacity.
  • Humanitarian assistance: coordination in disaster relief and emergency response.

10.4 Importance for India

  • Strengthens India's role in shaping the Indo-Pacific order.
  • Provides strategic cooperation without requiring India to join a formal military alliance.
  • Supports maritime domain awareness in the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
  • Improves access to technology, investment and resilient supply chains.
  • Creates a platform for cooperation with major advanced economies while preserving strategic autonomy.

10.5 Challenges

  • Different threat perceptions and strategic priorities among members.
  • Limited institutionalisation and reliance on political commitment.
  • Risk of being perceived as an exclusive anti-China grouping.
  • Difficulty coordinating economic and security policies across four different political systems.
  • Need to deliver practical outcomes beyond summit declarations.

India should present the Quad as a constructive and inclusive Indo-Pacific partnership, emphasising international law, freedom of navigation, connectivity, development and regional stability.

11. AUKUS: Security and Advanced Technology Partnership

11.1 Membership and purpose

AUKUS is a security partnership among Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States, announced in 2021. It is designed to deepen defence and advanced-technology cooperation, particularly in the Indo-Pacific.

11.2 Two pillars

  • Pillar I: supports Australia's acquisition of conventionally armed, nuclear-powered submarines, alongside associated industrial, technological and workforce cooperation.
  • Pillar II: promotes cooperation in advanced capabilities, including artificial intelligence, autonomy, cyber, quantum technologies, electronic warfare and undersea capabilities.

11.3 Nuclear-powered versus nuclear-armed

A nuclear-powered submarine uses a nuclear reactor for propulsion. This does not mean that the submarine is nuclear-armed. The distinction is important for understanding AUKUS and nuclear non-proliferation debates.

11.4 Implications for India

  • May strengthen the maritime capabilities of partners in the Indo-Pacific.
  • Can contribute to a more capable regional balance, depending on implementation and regional responses.
  • Raises questions about nuclear-powered submarine technology and non-proliferation safeguards.
  • May create opportunities for complementary technology and maritime cooperation with India.
  • Requires India to assess regional security consequences without treating AUKUS as an Indian alliance commitment.

Distinction: AUKUS is a security and technology partnership among three countries. The Quad is a broader four-country diplomatic partnership and is not a collective-defence alliance.

12. I2U2: India, Israel, UAE and United States

12.1 Background

I2U2 brings together India, Israel, the United Arab Emirates and the United States. It developed from efforts to strengthen economic and technological cooperation among the four partners.

12.2 Main areas of cooperation

  • Food security and agricultural technology.
  • Renewable energy and clean-energy projects.
  • Infrastructure and logistics.
  • Water management and innovative technologies.
  • Private-sector investment and industrial cooperation.
  • Technology, research and innovation.

12.3 Strategic and economic significance

The grouping brings together India's manufacturing and technology capabilities, Israel's innovation ecosystem, UAE capital and logistics strengths, and the United States' technology and investment capacity.

It also reflects the growing interaction between West Asian diplomacy, Indo-Pacific strategy, food security and global infrastructure connectivity.

12.4 Limitations

  • It is a flexible cooperation framework rather than a treaty-based regional organisation.
  • Projects depend on commercial feasibility, investment and political continuity.
  • Regional conflicts and diplomatic tensions can affect implementation.
  • Its long-term influence depends on converting announcements into completed projects.

13. Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity

13.1 Background

The Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity was launched by the United States in 2022 with partners across the Indo-Pacific. It seeks to promote economic cooperation in areas where regional supply chains, trade standards and investment are increasingly important.

13.2 Four pillars

  1. Trade: cooperation on areas such as digital trade, labour, environment, agriculture and regulatory approaches.
  2. Supply Chains: improving resilience, transparency, diversification and coordination during disruptions.
  3. Clean Economy: cooperation on clean energy, decarbonisation, climate technologies and investment.
  4. Fair Economy: cooperation on anti-corruption, tax transparency and efforts to promote a fairer business environment.

13.3 India's participation

India participates in the supply-chain, clean-economy and fair-economy pillars. It has not joined the trade pillar. This distinction is important when assessing India's participation and the framework's coverage.

13.4 Why IPEF is not a conventional free trade agreement

IPEF does not operate like a conventional free trade agreement that exchanges tariff concessions and establishes a comprehensive preferential market-access arrangement. Its focus is on economic cooperation, standards, supply-chain resilience and coordination in selected policy areas.

13.5 Importance for India

  • Provides an opportunity to diversify supply chains and reduce vulnerabilities.
  • Supports clean-energy investment and technology cooperation.
  • Creates space for engagement on anti-corruption and tax transparency.
  • Allows India to participate in selected economic frameworks without accepting all trade commitments.

India must assess the balance between international standards, domestic policy space, export competitiveness and the interests of its farmers, workers and industry.

14. RCEP and CPTPP: Competing Models of Trade Integration

14.1 Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership

RCEP is a free trade agreement involving ASEAN and five of its major trade partners: Australia, China, Japan, New Zealand and South Korea. It was signed in 2020 and entered into force in stages from 2022.

RCEP includes the ten original ASEAN members and the five additional partners. It is one of the world's major regional trade arrangements by combined economic size and population.

14.2 Main provisions and significance

  • Reduction or elimination of tariffs on a large range of goods.
  • Common rules of origin that can facilitate regional production networks.
  • Commitments on customs procedures and trade facilitation.
  • Rules relating to services, investment and economic cooperation.
  • Greater integration of manufacturing and intermediate-goods supply chains.

14.3 India's decision not to join RCEP

India participated in the negotiations but withdrew in 2019. Its concerns included:

  • Risk of increased imports and pressure on domestic manufacturers.
  • Concerns about the trade deficit, particularly with China.
  • Insufficient confidence in safeguards against import surges.
  • Concerns over rules of origin and possible circumvention through third countries.
  • Potential effects on farmers, dairy producers and small businesses.
  • Limited comfort with the balance of market-access commitments.

14.4 Economic consequences of remaining outside

Staying outside RCEP provides India with greater freedom to calibrate tariff and trade policies. However, it may also reduce preferential access to some regional markets and make it harder for Indian firms to participate in supply chains operating within the RCEP area.

The policy challenge is to improve domestic competitiveness, logistics, standards and manufacturing capacity while negotiating trade agreements that provide adequate safeguards.

14.5 Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership

The CPTPP is a high-standard regional trade agreement that developed from the Trans-Pacific Partnership after the United States withdrew from the original arrangement.

Its membership includes countries in the Asia-Pacific and the United Kingdom. Its rules cover goods, services, investment, intellectual property, digital trade, state-owned enterprises, labour and environmental provisions.

India's trade-policy challenge: India must balance market access, export opportunities, domestic industrial policy, food security, regulatory autonomy and participation in global value chains.

15. Supply-Chain and Connectivity Initiatives

15.1 Supply Chain Resilience Initiative

The Supply Chain Resilience Initiative was launched by India, Japan and Australia in 2021. It seeks to reduce excessive dependence on concentrated production locations and improve resilience against disruptions.

Its broad objectives include:

  • Diversifying sources of critical inputs and intermediate goods.
  • Encouraging investment in alternative production locations.
  • Improving supply-chain transparency and risk assessment.
  • Strengthening cooperation among businesses and governments.
  • Supporting regional manufacturing and logistics networks.

The initiative is particularly relevant to pharmaceuticals, electronics, semiconductors, automotive components, critical minerals and other strategically important sectors.

15.2 India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor

The India-Middle East-Europe Economic Corridor was announced through a memorandum of understanding among participating partners on the margins of the 2023 G20 Summit.

It is intended to improve connectivity between India, the Middle East and Europe through a combination of transport and infrastructure links.

Its proposed components include:

  • Maritime connectivity between Indian and Middle Eastern ports.
  • Rail connections across participating Middle Eastern countries.
  • Links connecting the Middle East with European transport networks.
  • Energy infrastructure and potential electricity connectivity.
  • Digital connectivity through cables and associated infrastructure.

15.3 Strategic and economic importance

  • Could diversify trade routes connecting India with Europe.
  • May reduce logistics costs and improve transport efficiency if implemented effectively.
  • Could support energy, digital and industrial connectivity.
  • May create new investment opportunities in ports, railways, logistics and infrastructure.
  • Can strengthen India's economic engagement with West Asia and Europe.

15.4 Constraints

  • Regional conflicts and geopolitical instability.
  • Complex cross-border financing and regulatory requirements.
  • Need for coordination among several governments and infrastructure operators.
  • High capital costs and long implementation timelines.
  • Competition or overlap with existing connectivity corridors.

IMEC should be understood as a proposed connectivity initiative, not as an already completed transport corridor or a conventional free trade agreement.

15.5 Critical minerals cooperation

Critical minerals such as lithium, cobalt, nickel, graphite and rare earth elements are important for batteries, electric vehicles, renewable energy, electronics, defence and advanced manufacturing.

The Minerals Security Partnership was launched in 2022 by a group of partner countries to promote secure, diversified and responsible critical-mineral supply chains. Its work focuses on supporting projects across extraction, processing and recycling.

India has a strong interest in this area because its energy transition, electronics sector and manufacturing ambitions depend on reliable access to critical minerals. India's participation in any specific partnership mechanism should be distinguished from its broader bilateral and multilateral critical-minerals cooperation.

15.6 Global Biofuels Alliance

The Global Biofuels Alliance was launched during India's G20 Presidency in 2023. It seeks to promote cooperation in the development and adoption of sustainable biofuels.

Its objectives include:

  • Facilitating international cooperation on biofuel technologies.
  • Promoting sustainable production and use of biofuels.
  • Supporting capacity building and knowledge sharing.
  • Encouraging investment and market development.
  • Supporting energy diversification and emissions reduction.

For India, biofuels can support energy security, rural income, agricultural diversification and reduced dependence on imported fossil fuels. However, sustainability requires attention to food-versus-fuel concerns, land use, water consumption and lifecycle emissions.

15.7 International Solar Alliance

The International Solar Alliance was launched by India and France in 2015. It promotes solar-energy deployment, particularly in countries with high solar potential.

Its work includes:

  • Mobilising finance for solar projects.
  • Promoting technology cooperation and capacity building.
  • Supporting solar applications in agriculture, health and public infrastructure.
  • Encouraging affordable solar deployment in developing countries.

The ISA is an example of India's role in building institutions and coalitions around global public goods.

15.8 Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure

The Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure was launched by India in 2019. It promotes the resilience of infrastructure systems against natural hazards and climate-related risks.

It supports knowledge sharing, technical assistance, capacity building and collaboration among governments, international organisations and infrastructure stakeholders.

Its importance is increasing as climate-related disasters threaten transport networks, energy systems, communications, urban infrastructure and public services.

16. OPEC and OPEC+: Energy Markets and India's Economic Security

16.1 Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries

OPEC was established in 1960 by oil-producing countries to coordinate petroleum policies and protect the interests of its members.

It is a commodity-producer organisation rather than a trade agreement or a general political forum. Its decisions and production policies can influence global oil supply expectations and prices.

16.2 OPEC+

OPEC+ refers to cooperation between OPEC members and participating non-OPEC oil-producing countries. The arrangement coordinates production-related decisions and has become an important feature of global oil-market management.

16.3 Why it matters to India

India is a major importer of crude oil and is therefore sensitive to global oil-price movements.

  • Higher crude prices increase the import bill and can worsen the current account.
  • Higher energy costs can contribute to inflation and raise transport and production costs.
  • Price volatility affects government finances, subsidies and household expenditure.
  • Oil-price shocks can influence exchange-rate stability and monetary-policy decisions.
  • Stable energy markets are important for industrial production and long-term investment.

16.4 India's policy response

  • Diversification of crude-oil import sources.
  • Strategic petroleum reserves and improved emergency preparedness.
  • Expansion of renewable energy and biofuels.
  • Greater energy efficiency and electrification.
  • Long-term investment in domestic energy production and alternative fuels.

Prelims distinction: OPEC coordinates among petroleum-exporting countries. OPEC+ is a wider production cooperation arrangement. India is a major oil consumer and importer, not an OPEC member.

17. Development Finance Institutions and Economic Groupings

Development finance institutions are not interchangeable with political forums or free trade agreements. They provide financial resources for development projects, infrastructure, private-sector investment and economic resilience.

17.1 New Development Bank

The New Development Bank was established by BRICS countries. It finances infrastructure and sustainable development projects and provides an additional source of development finance for emerging economies.

17.2 Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank

The Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank is a multilateral development bank established to support sustainable infrastructure and regional connectivity. It is headquartered in Beijing.

India is a major member and borrower. AIIB financing can support transport, energy, urban development, water and other infrastructure projects.

17.3 Asian Development Bank

The Asian Development Bank was established in 1966 and is headquartered in Manila. It supports economic and social development in Asia and the Pacific through loans, grants, technical assistance and policy support.

17.4 IMF and World Bank

The International Monetary Fund focuses primarily on international monetary cooperation, macroeconomic stability, balance-of-payments support and surveillance. The World Bank Group focuses on long-term development, poverty reduction, institutional capacity and investment.

18. Global Trade Architecture and Economic Concepts

18.1 Trade groupings and the multilateral trading system

Regional trade agreements operate within a wider international trading system. The World Trade Organization provides the principal multilateral framework for trade rules, negotiations and dispute settlement among its members.

Regional agreements can deepen economic integration among participating countries, but they may also create different regulatory standards and overlapping trade obligations.

18.2 Tariffs

A tariff is a tax imposed on imported goods. Tariffs can protect domestic industries, raise government revenue or be used as negotiating instruments.

Potential effects include:

  • Higher prices for imported goods and intermediate inputs.
  • Protection for domestic producers from foreign competition.
  • Possible retaliation by trading partners.
  • Changes in import composition and trade flows.
  • Effects on export competitiveness where domestic production depends on imported inputs.

18.3 Non-tariff barriers

Non-tariff measures include regulations and administrative requirements that affect trade without taking the form of an ordinary customs tariff.

Examples include:

  • Technical standards and conformity assessment requirements.
  • Sanitary and phytosanitary measures.
  • Import licensing and quotas.
  • Customs procedures and documentation requirements.
  • Rules concerning packaging, labelling and product certification.
  • Subsidies and other domestic support measures.

Such measures may serve legitimate public purposes, including consumer safety and environmental protection. However, they can become disguised restrictions on trade if they are discriminatory, unnecessarily restrictive or inconsistently applied.

18.4 Rules of origin

Rules of origin determine the country in which a product is considered to have originated. They are important because trade agreements generally provide tariff preferences only to goods that satisfy agreed origin requirements.

Rules may be based on wholly obtained goods, a change in tariff classification, regional value content or specified production processes.

Effective rules of origin prevent goods from third countries from using a member's territory merely to obtain preferential access. They are therefore important for preventing trade deflection and protecting the integrity of free trade agreements.

18.5 Global value chains

A global value chain is a production system in which different stages of producing a good or service take place in multiple countries. Design, components, assembly, software, logistics, marketing and after-sales services may be distributed across several economies.

Benefits include:

  • Access to larger markets and specialised suppliers.
  • Productivity gains and economies of scale.
  • Technology transfer and learning.
  • Employment and export opportunities.
  • Integration into international production networks.

Risks include:

  • Exposure to geopolitical disputes and trade restrictions.
  • Disruption from pandemics, wars and natural disasters.
  • Concentration of critical production in a limited number of locations.
  • Dependence on imported components and technologies.
  • Vulnerability to shipping and logistics bottlenecks.

18.6 Supply-chain resilience

Supply-chain resilience refers to the ability of production and distribution networks to withstand disruptions and recover quickly. It does not necessarily mean abandoning global trade or producing everything domestically.

Common strategies include:

  • Diversification: sourcing from multiple countries and suppliers.
  • Strategic stockpiles: maintaining reserves of critical inputs.
  • Near-shoring: locating production closer to major markets.
  • Friend-shoring: building supply relationships with trusted or strategically aligned partners.
  • Domestic capacity: developing local capabilities in critical sectors.
  • Transparency: mapping suppliers and identifying vulnerabilities.

18.7 Protectionism and industrial policy

Protectionism refers to measures that restrict or discourage foreign competition in order to protect domestic economic interests. These measures can include tariffs, quotas, subsidies, local-content requirements and regulatory restrictions.

Industrial policy seeks to develop selected domestic industries through public investment, incentives, infrastructure, skills and technology support. It can support structural transformation, but poorly designed interventions may increase costs, reduce competition and create inefficient industries dependent on continuing protection.

18.8 Carbon-related trade measures

Climate policies increasingly affect international trade. Carbon border measures seek to address differences in carbon costs between domestic production and imports.

The European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is an important example. It creates reporting and financial obligations for specified carbon-intensive imports under its applicable rules.

Potential implications for India include:

  • Higher compliance costs for carbon-intensive exporters.
  • Pressure to improve emissions measurement and reporting.
  • Incentives for cleaner industrial production.
  • Concerns about trade competitiveness and climate-related discrimination.
  • Debates about the relationship between climate policy, development needs and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities.

18.9 Digital trade and services

Digital trade includes cross-border supply of digitally delivered services, electronic commerce, digital payments, data-enabled business and related activities.

International negotiations increasingly address data flows, privacy, cybersecurity, electronic authentication, consumer protection, digital taxation and the treatment of digital products.

India has significant interests in digital services, information technology exports, digital public infrastructure and the protection of citizens' data. Its policy challenge is to balance innovation and international market access with privacy, security, competition and regulatory autonomy.

18.10 Trade finance and currency arrangements

International trade requires finance for payments, working capital, insurance, guarantees and settlement across currencies. Access to trade finance is particularly important for small exporters and businesses in developing economies.

Discussions on local-currency settlement and alternative payment arrangements seek to reduce transaction costs or vulnerabilities associated with dependence on a limited number of international currencies and payment channels.

However, the international role of a currency depends on convertibility, liquidity, financial-market depth, confidence, macroeconomic stability and the availability of safe assets. A declaration supporting local-currency trade does not by itself create a common currency or a fully integrated monetary system.

19. Other Important Economic and Strategic Frameworks

19.1 Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation

APEC is a regional economic forum established in 1989. It has 21 member economies and promotes trade and investment liberalisation, business facilitation and economic cooperation across the Asia-Pacific.

APEC uses a voluntary and consensus-based approach. Its commitments are generally not legally binding in the manner of a free trade agreement.

Its economic significance includes customs simplification, trade facilitation, regulatory cooperation, digital connectivity and regional business integration.

India is not an APEC member. Its interest in the forum is linked to its wider engagement with the Asia-Pacific and the potential benefits of stronger economic integration.

19.2 Organisation of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and energy diplomacy

In addition to production coordination, OPEC and OPEC+ matter because energy prices influence inflation, external balances, fiscal planning and industrial costs in importing economies. India's energy diplomacy therefore involves both producer relationships and diversification into renewable and alternative sources.

19.3 Gas Exporting Countries Forum

The Gas Exporting Countries Forum is a platform for cooperation among major natural-gas producers. It discusses gas markets, investment, infrastructure and the role of natural gas in the global energy system.

It matters to India because natural gas is relevant to fertiliser production, electricity, city-gas distribution and industrial consumption. Global gas-price volatility and liquefied natural gas supply disruptions can affect India's energy security.

19.4 Organisation of Islamic Cooperation

The Organisation of Islamic Cooperation is an intergovernmental organisation of Muslim-majority countries. It discusses political, economic, cultural and social issues affecting its members and the wider Muslim world.

India is not a member. Its relationship with OIC positions is influenced by its bilateral relations with member countries, its domestic constitutional framework, counterterrorism concerns and its emphasis on sovereignty and non-interference.

19.5 European Union

The European Union is a distinctive regional political and economic union with institutions and legal arrangements that go beyond a conventional diplomatic forum.

It has a single market and common policies in several areas. Some members also participate in the euro area, while others retain national currencies.

India-EU relations are important for trade, investment, technology, climate policy, mobility, connectivity and strategic cooperation. The EU's regulatory standards can influence Indian exporters even where India is not directly participating in the relevant policy process.

19.6 Eurasian Economic Union

The Eurasian Economic Union is an economic integration arrangement among participating Eurasian states. It includes a customs-union framework and aims to facilitate movement of goods, services, capital and labour among its members.

Its relevance to India arises from trade, energy, connectivity and the possibility of broader economic engagement with Eurasian markets. Its regulatory framework and geopolitical environment must be considered when assessing potential agreements.

19.7 Pacific Islands Forum

The Pacific Islands Forum is a regional political and economic organisation that coordinates the interests of Pacific island countries and Australia and New Zealand. Its agenda includes climate change, ocean governance, fisheries, development and regional security.

It is relevant to India because of its Indo-Pacific engagement, climate diplomacy, maritime cooperation and development partnerships with island countries.

21. India's Strategy: Multi-Alignment and Strategic Autonomy

21.1 Why India participates in multiple groupings

India's participation in several groupings is not necessarily contradictory. Different forums serve different national objectives. India may cooperate with one group on maritime security, another on development finance, and another on trade or climate issues.

This approach is often described as multi-alignment: building issue-based partnerships with different countries while preserving independent decision-making.

21.2 Major elements of India's approach

  • Strategic autonomy: retaining the freedom to make foreign-policy decisions based on national interests.
  • Issue-based partnerships: cooperating with different countries according to the subject and objective.
  • Inclusive multilateralism: supporting a more representative international order.
  • South-South cooperation: strengthening partnerships with developing countries.
  • Regional leadership: promoting connectivity, maritime security and development in India's neighbourhood and the Indian Ocean.
  • Economic diversification: reducing excessive dependence on any one market, supplier or technology ecosystem.
  • Technology partnerships: expanding access to critical technologies while protecting domestic capabilities and security.

21.3 Balancing competing interests

India's participation in groupings can create difficult choices. It must manage relations with countries that may have conflicting strategic objectives, political systems or economic interests.

Examples of balancing challenges include:

  • Participating in BRICS while deepening strategic cooperation with the United States and its partners.
  • Engaging Russia on energy and defence while maintaining important relationships with Europe and other partners.
  • Working with China in multilateral forums while addressing bilateral border and security disputes.
  • Seeking market access through trade agreements while protecting vulnerable domestic sectors.
  • Supporting climate action while insisting on equitable climate finance and development space.

21.4 Economic interests that cut across groupings

  • Securing reliable access to energy, fertilisers, food and critical minerals.
  • Expanding exports of goods and services.
  • Increasing India's share in global value chains.
  • Attracting investment in manufacturing, infrastructure and technology.
  • Improving access to international finance and development resources.
  • Protecting the competitiveness of Indian businesses against discriminatory trade practices.
  • Strengthening digital trade and services exports.
  • Building resilient logistics, port and transport networks.

22. Challenges Facing International Groupings

22.1 Geopolitical rivalry

Strategic competition among major powers can divide groupings and prevent agreement on conflicts, sanctions, technology restrictions and regional security.

22.2 Weak implementation

Informal forums often lack enforcement mechanisms. Political declarations may not translate into domestic policy or completed projects.

22.3 Unequal economic power

Larger economies can exercise disproportionate influence over agendas, standards, investment and market access. Developing countries may struggle to protect their interests in negotiations.

22.4 Competing institutional frameworks

Overlapping memberships and different rules can create fragmentation. Countries may face conflicting standards, reporting requirements and trade obligations.

22.5 Protectionism and economic nationalism

Subsidies, export controls, tariffs, sanctions and local-content requirements can weaken the predictability of global trade.

22.6 Representation and legitimacy

Informal forums may be influential but do not represent every country equally. Their legitimacy depends partly on whether their decisions take account of the concerns of smaller and developing states.

22.7 Financial and technological inequality

Developing countries may lack the capital, technology and institutional capacity needed to meet international standards or benefit from new cooperation frameworks.

22.8 Domestic political changes

Elections, changes of government and shifting national priorities can affect the continuity of international commitments and infrastructure projects.

23. Way Forward for India

  1. Strengthen domestic competitiveness: improve logistics, infrastructure, skills, research and manufacturing productivity so that international partnerships translate into export growth.
  2. Adopt a strategic approach to trade agreements: negotiate market access while ensuring workable rules of origin, safeguards and appropriate protection for vulnerable sectors.
  3. Improve global value-chain participation: attract investment, strengthen component manufacturing and improve integration with regional production networks.
  4. Build resilient supply chains: diversify critical imports, strengthen domestic capabilities and develop trusted international partnerships.
  5. Lead on development priorities: continue advocating equitable climate finance, debt relief, technology access and more representative global institutions.
  6. Convert initiatives into projects: prioritise feasibility, financing, timelines, institutional coordination and measurable outcomes.
  7. Preserve strategic autonomy: avoid unnecessary bloc-based commitments and maintain constructive engagement across different international forums.
  8. Strengthen regional institutions: improve connectivity, trade facilitation and practical cooperation in South Asia, the Bay of Bengal and the Indian Ocean.
  9. Invest in standards and technology: participate actively in discussions on digital trade, artificial intelligence, critical minerals, clean technology and emerging regulatory frameworks.
  10. Improve institutional coordination: align diplomacy, trade policy, industrial policy, energy planning and national security decision-making.

25. Mains Analytical Framework

25.1 How to analyse the relevance of a grouping

An analytical answer should move beyond describing membership and objectives. It should assess whether the grouping advances India's national interests and whether its institutional arrangements can deliver practical outcomes.

  1. Context: explain the geopolitical or economic circumstances that led to the grouping's creation.
  2. Institutional design: identify membership, decision-making procedures and legal character.
  3. Strategic relevance: explain its implications for India's security, diplomacy and regional position.
  4. Economic relevance: assess trade, investment, technology, finance, energy and supply-chain effects.
  5. Achievements: identify concrete agreements, institutions, projects or policy outcomes.
  6. Limitations: examine geopolitical rivalry, weak implementation, unequal influence and institutional constraints.
  7. Way forward: suggest practical reforms and explain how India can use the platform effectively.

25.2 Illustrative analytical themes

Theme A: Are minilateral groupings weakening multilateralism?

Minilateral groupings can deliver faster cooperation among countries with common interests. They may also help address issues where universal institutions struggle to reach agreement. However, excessive reliance on exclusive groupings can fragment international rules and reduce the legitimacy of global decision-making.

India should support issue-based cooperation while strengthening universal institutions and ensuring that smaller and developing countries are not excluded from decisions affecting them.

Theme B: Can regional trade agreements replace the WTO?

Regional agreements can achieve deeper integration among participating economies, but they do not provide a universal framework for all trading nations. Overlapping agreements can create complex rules and increase compliance costs.

The WTO remains important for multilateral trade principles, transparency and dispute settlement. Regional agreements should complement rather than undermine the multilateral trading system.

Theme C: How can India balance strategic autonomy with strategic partnerships?

India's partnerships with the Quad, BRICS, SCO, G20 and other platforms reflect different national interests. Strategic autonomy should not be interpreted as avoiding cooperation. It means retaining independent judgement while pursuing mutually beneficial arrangements.

Theme D: Can supply-chain diversification support India's rise as a manufacturing power?

Global firms increasingly assess resilience, geopolitical risk, logistics and supplier concentration when choosing production locations. India can benefit from this shift if it improves infrastructure, skills, regulatory predictability and manufacturing productivity.

Diversification alone is insufficient. India must become a competitive and reliable production location capable of supplying high-quality goods and services at scale.

Theme E: Do critical-mineral partnerships create new dependencies?

Partnerships can diversify supply sources, but they may also reproduce dependence if processing, refining or technology remains concentrated in a small number of countries. India should combine international partnerships with domestic exploration, recycling, research and responsible processing.

25.3 Useful concluding perspective

International groupings are instruments of diplomacy, not ends in themselves. Their value to India depends on whether they contribute to national development, strategic autonomy, regional stability and a more representative international order. India's long-term approach should combine active multilateral engagement, selective partnerships and stronger domestic economic capabilities.
