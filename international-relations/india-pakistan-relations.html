1. Historical Background

India-Pakistan relations have been shaped by Partition, competing national narratives, territorial disputes, wars, cross-border terrorism, nuclearisation and periodic diplomatic initiatives. The relationship has important implications for South Asian stability, India's internal security and regional connectivity.

  • 1947: Partition and independence led to displacement, communal violence and the first Kashmir conflict.
  • 1947–48: The first India-Pakistan war over Jammu and Kashmir ended with a UN-mediated ceasefire.
  • 1965: A second war was fought, followed by the Tashkent Declaration in 1966.
  • 1971: The war resulted in the creation of Bangladesh; the Simla Agreement followed in 1972.
  • 1988: The two countries agreed not to attack each other's nuclear installations and facilities.
  • 1999: The Lahore Declaration sought to improve relations, but the Kargil conflict severely damaged confidence.
  • 2003: A ceasefire understanding was reached along the Line of Control.
  • 2008: The Mumbai terrorist attacks caused another major breakdown in bilateral engagement.
  • 2019: The Pulwama terror attack and Balakot developments sharply escalated tensions.

2. Core Issues in the Relationship

Jammu and Kashmir

The territorial dispute over Jammu and Kashmir has been a central source of conflict since Partition. The Simla Agreement of 1972 established a framework for bilateral settlement of differences through peaceful means, while the Line of Control remains a key military and political reference.

India's constitutional reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir in 2019 was strongly opposed by Pakistan. The issue continues to affect bilateral diplomacy and regional security.

Cross-border terrorism

India has consistently identified cross-border terrorism as a major obstacle to normal relations. Terrorist attacks have caused diplomatic breakdowns and strengthened domestic demands for a security-centred approach.

Counter-terrorism cooperation is constrained by mutual distrust, competing allegations and differences over attribution, evidence and accountability.

Border and ceasefire management

Violations and firing incidents along the Line of Control and International Border have affected civilian populations, displaced residents and increased military tensions. Effective communication and observance of ceasefire understandings are essential for reducing escalation risks.

Water sharing

The Indus Waters Treaty, signed in 1960 with the World Bank's involvement, provides a framework for the use of the Indus river system. It has historically survived several periods of conflict, although differences over projects and treaty interpretation have led to disputes.

Trade and connectivity

Political and security tensions have restricted trade, transit and people-to-people contacts. The economic cost includes lost market opportunities, limited regional supply chains and reduced connectivity across South Asia.

3. Major Agreements and Diplomatic Initiatives

  • Karachi Agreement, 1949: Established a ceasefire line in Jammu and Kashmir under UN auspices.
  • Indus Waters Treaty, 1960: Established principles and mechanisms for the use of the Indus river system.
  • Tashkent Declaration, 1966: Followed the 1965 war and committed both countries to withdraw forces and restore relations on agreed terms.
  • Simla Agreement, 1972: Committed the countries to resolve differences through peaceful bilateral negotiations and respect the Line of Control, without prejudice to their respective positions.
  • Agreement on Prohibition of Attack against Nuclear Installations and Facilities, 1988: Provides for annual exchange of lists of nuclear installations and facilities covered by the agreement.
  • Lahore Declaration, 1999: Emphasised peace, security, confidence-building and dialogue, including in the nuclear context.
  • 2003 ceasefire understanding: Helped reduce firing and violence along the Line of Control for a period.
  • Composite Dialogue process: Covered issues such as peace and security, Jammu and Kashmir, terrorism, trade, Siachen, Sir Creek and other bilateral matters.
  • 2021 reaffirmation of ceasefire understanding: The Directors General of Military Operations reiterated commitment to existing ceasefire arrangements.

4. Indus Waters Treaty

The Indus Waters Treaty was signed on 19 September 1960 by India and Pakistan, with the World Bank playing a facilitating role. It allocates the eastern rivers—Ravi, Beas and Sutlej—principally for India's unrestricted use, subject to treaty provisions, and the western rivers—Indus, Jhelum and Chenab—principally for Pakistan's use, while permitting specified uses by India under the treaty.

Institutional framework

  • Permanent Indus Commission: Provides a regular channel for communication, exchange of information and discussion of treaty-related matters.
  • Neutral Expert: A mechanism for specified technical differences.
  • Court of Arbitration: A mechanism for specified disputes, according to the treaty's provisions.

Significance

  • Demonstrates the possibility of institutionalised cooperation despite political conflict.
  • Provides predictability for water use and infrastructure planning.
  • Creates technical and legal processes for addressing disagreements.

Challenges

  • Disagreements over the interpretation of permitted hydropower projects.
  • Competing views on dispute-resolution procedures.
  • Climate change, changing river flows and growing water demand.
  • Political tensions affecting the atmosphere for technical cooperation.

Any assessment of the treaty's present operation or legal status should be based on current official notifications and proceedings rather than assumptions drawn from political statements alone.

5. Nuclear Dimension and Strategic Stability

Both India and Pakistan possess nuclear weapons. Nuclearisation has introduced a serious escalation risk while also influencing conventional military calculations and crisis diplomacy.

  • Nuclear deterrence may discourage full-scale war but does not eliminate the possibility of limited conflict.
  • Misinterpretation, misinformation and compressed decision-making timelines can increase crisis instability.
  • Confidence-building measures and communication channels reduce the risk of accidental escalation.
  • The agreement on non-attack of nuclear installations and annual exchange of lists is a notable bilateral confidence-building measure.

India's declared nuclear doctrine includes credible minimum deterrence and a stated no-first-use policy, subject to the official doctrine and subsequent clarifications. Pakistan has not adopted an equivalent no-first-use declaration.

6. Terrorism and Internal Security

Cross-border terrorism has been a central factor shaping India's Pakistan policy. Terrorist violence affects public safety, military preparedness, communal harmony, investment confidence and the political space for dialogue.

Policy dimensions

  • Strengthen intelligence coordination among central and State agencies.
  • Improve border surveillance and counter-infiltration capacity.
  • Disrupt terrorist financing, recruitment and logistical networks.
  • Enhance international cooperation against terrorist organisations and their support structures.
  • Maintain crisis communication to prevent terrorist incidents from triggering uncontrolled escalation.
  • Protect civilian populations and critical infrastructure.

Counter-terrorism policy must combine effective security measures with constitutional safeguards, rule of law and protection of civilian rights.

7. Trade, People-to-People Relations and Regional Costs

India and Pakistan have substantial potential for trade because of geographic proximity, complementary markets and historical commercial links. Political tensions have sharply constrained this potential.

  • Restrictions on direct trade increase transport and transaction costs.
  • Limited transit arrangements weaken regional supply chains.
  • Visa restrictions reduce family, academic, cultural and professional contacts.
  • Suspension or disruption of sporting and cultural exchanges reduces opportunities for public engagement.
  • Political hostility weakens wider South Asian cooperation.

People-to-people contact cannot replace security arrangements, but it can preserve social links and reduce the risk of complete regional isolation.

8. International and Multilateral Dimensions

India and Pakistan engage in several international institutions and regional platforms. Their bilateral tensions have also affected the functioning of South Asian regional cooperation.

  • United Nations: Both countries participate in multilateral diplomacy, although they differ on several security and territorial questions.
  • SAARC: India-Pakistan tensions have impeded the organisation's ability to sustain high-level regional cooperation.
  • Shanghai Cooperation Organisation: Provides a multilateral forum in which both countries participate, including on regional security and connectivity issues.
  • Financial Action Task Force: International standards on anti-money laundering and countering terrorist financing are relevant to the regional security environment.

India's approach has generally favoured bilateral resolution of disputes and opposed third-party mediation on issues it regards as bilateral, while continuing to use multilateral institutions for broader cooperation.

9. India's Policy Approach

  • Security first: Protect territorial integrity and citizens against terrorism and cross-border violence.
  • Deterrence: Maintain credible conventional and strategic capabilities.
  • Diplomatic communication: Preserve channels for crisis management and practical bilateral issues.
  • Simla framework: Emphasise peaceful bilateral resolution in accordance with existing commitments.
  • International cooperation: Build support for counter-terrorism and accountability through relevant institutions.
  • Regional stability: Prevent bilateral tensions from undermining India's wider economic and diplomatic objectives in South Asia.

10. Challenges and Way Forward

Normalisation is difficult because the relationship combines deep political disputes, security threats, domestic political sensitivities and nuclear risks. A durable improvement requires sustained political commitment and credible action against terrorism.

  1. Maintain effective ceasefire arrangements and military communication.
  2. Prevent terrorist groups from using violence to derail diplomatic processes.
  3. Use established bilateral mechanisms to address practical disputes.
  4. Preserve technical cooperation on water and other essential shared concerns.
  5. Strengthen crisis communication and nuclear risk-reduction measures.
  6. Encourage carefully calibrated people-to-people engagement when conditions permit.
  7. Keep wider regional cooperation open wherever functional interests converge.

Mains focus

Analyse the relationship through the interaction of terrorism, territorial disputes, nuclear deterrence, water cooperation and regional stability. A balanced answer should distinguish India's security concerns from the broader need to prevent escalation and preserve practical cooperation.
