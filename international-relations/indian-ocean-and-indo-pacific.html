1. Strategic Importance of the Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean is a major maritime space connecting Asia, Africa, Europe and the Pacific. It carries substantial international trade, energy shipments and communications infrastructure. Its security and stability are therefore central to global economic activity and India's national interests.

  • Major sea lines of communication connect the Persian Gulf, East Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia.
  • Important energy shipments pass through the Strait of Hormuz, Bab-el-Mandeb and routes around the Cape of Good Hope.
  • The Strait of Malacca links the Indian Ocean with the western Pacific and is a major international shipping corridor.
  • Undersea communication cables carry a significant share of international data traffic.
  • Island States and coastal communities are highly exposed to climate change, cyclones and sea-level rise.

India's peninsular geography, island territories and location near important maritime routes give it a significant stake in a free, open, stable and rules-based maritime order.

2. India's Maritime Geography

  • India has a long mainland coastline along the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal.
  • The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are located near the approaches to the Strait of Malacca.
  • Lakshadweep is strategically located in the Arabian Sea.
  • India's maritime interests extend to the western Indian Ocean, eastern African coast, island States and Southeast Asian approaches.
  • Major ports and coastal infrastructure are important for trade, logistics, energy security and naval operations.

The Andaman and Nicobar Islands are especially important for maritime domain awareness and India's engagement with the eastern Indian Ocean. Their development must balance strategic requirements with ecological sensitivity and the rights and welfare of local communities.

3. Maritime Security: Meaning and Dimensions

Maritime security includes the protection of national sovereignty, maritime commerce, offshore resources, coastal populations, ports, shipping routes and the marine environment.

Traditional security concerns

  • Naval competition and military presence.
  • Protection of territorial waters and maritime boundaries.
  • Security of ports, naval installations and offshore infrastructure.
  • Protection of sea lines of communication.

Non-traditional security concerns

  • Piracy and armed robbery at sea.
  • Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing.
  • Drug, arms and human trafficking.
  • Maritime terrorism and smuggling.
  • Marine pollution and ecological degradation.
  • Cyberattacks on ports, shipping and maritime infrastructure.
  • Natural disasters and humanitarian emergencies.

4. Indian Ocean Sea Lines of Communication

Sea lines of communication are maritime routes used for the movement of goods, energy and people. Their security is important because disruption can raise shipping costs, delay supplies and affect global economic stability.

  • Strait of Hormuz: A critical passage connecting the Persian Gulf with the Gulf of Oman and the Arabian Sea.
  • Bab-el-Mandeb: Connects the Red Sea with the Gulf of Aden and is strategically important for routes towards the Suez Canal.
  • Strait of Malacca: Connects the Andaman Sea with the South China Sea and is a major route between the Indian and Pacific Oceans.
  • Suez Canal: Provides a major maritime link between the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea.
  • Cape of Good Hope: An alternative route around southern Africa when passages through the Red Sea or Suez Canal are disrupted.

India's energy imports and export trade make the security of these routes a major national economic concern.

5. SAGAR: Security and Growth for All in the Region

SAGAR is India's vision for the Indian Ocean region, articulated in 2015. It places emphasis on maritime security, regional cooperation, capacity building and sustainable development.

Core elements

  • Strengthening India's maritime security and capabilities.
  • Supporting the security and development priorities of Indian Ocean partners.
  • Promoting collective action against maritime threats.
  • Improving disaster response and humanitarian assistance.
  • Encouraging sustainable use of ocean resources.
  • Building trust and partnerships with littoral and island States.

SAGAR reflects India's preference for cooperative maritime security rather than a purely military conception of regional influence.

6. Indo-Pacific: Concept and Strategic Relevance

The Indo-Pacific is a broad strategic and geographic concept linking the Indian and Pacific Oceans. It reflects the growing economic, security and technological interdependence between the two maritime regions.

There is no single universally accepted geographic definition of the Indo-Pacific. Different countries and institutions use the concept in accordance with their strategic outlook and priorities.

Key features

  • Concentration of major global trade and manufacturing networks.
  • Strategic importance of maritime routes and chokepoints.
  • Growing competition among major powers.
  • Importance of ASEAN and regional institutions.
  • Rising significance of maritime technology, digital infrastructure and supply-chain resilience.
  • Shared challenges involving climate change, disasters, piracy and illegal fishing.

7. India's Indo-Pacific Vision

India supports a free, open, inclusive and rules-based Indo-Pacific. Its approach emphasises respect for sovereignty, territorial integrity, peaceful resolution of disputes, freedom of navigation and overflight in accordance with international law, and ASEAN centrality.

  • Inclusiveness: The region should not be viewed as the exclusive domain of any one power or alliance.
  • ASEAN centrality: ASEAN-led mechanisms should remain important platforms for regional dialogue.
  • International law: Maritime conduct should be consistent with applicable international law, including the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea.
  • Connectivity: Infrastructure should be sustainable, transparent and respectful of sovereignty.
  • Cooperation: Regional partners should work together on maritime security, disaster relief, health, climate and economic resilience.
  • Strategic autonomy: India seeks partnerships without surrendering independent foreign policy decision-making.

8. International Maritime Law and UNCLOS

The United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), 1982, establishes the principal legal framework for the use of the world's oceans. It regulates maritime zones, navigation, resource rights, marine environmental protection and dispute settlement.

Major maritime zones

  • Territorial sea: Extends up to 12 nautical miles from the applicable baselines. The coastal State exercises sovereignty, subject to rights such as innocent passage under UNCLOS.
  • Contiguous zone: Extends up to 24 nautical miles from the baselines. The coastal State may exercise specified control to prevent and punish infringements of its customs, fiscal, immigration and sanitary laws within its territory or territorial sea.
  • Exclusive Economic Zone: May extend up to 200 nautical miles from the baselines. The coastal State has sovereign rights over natural resources and specified jurisdiction, while other States retain applicable freedoms under the Convention.
  • Continental shelf: Gives the coastal State rights over seabed and subsoil resources in accordance with UNCLOS, potentially extending beyond 200 nautical miles where the legal and geological criteria are met.
  • High seas: Areas beyond national jurisdiction, subject to freedoms and duties under international law.

UNCLOS also provides frameworks for maritime delimitation, marine environmental protection, scientific research and settlement of disputes.

9. Maritime Security Institutions and Partnerships

  • Indian Navy: Responsible for naval defence, maritime security, operational deployments and humanitarian assistance at sea.
  • Indian Coast Guard: Performs maritime law enforcement, coastal security, search and rescue, pollution response and related functions within its mandate.
  • Information Fusion Centre – Indian Ocean Region: Facilitates maritime information sharing and domain awareness with partner countries.
  • Indian Ocean Naval Symposium: Provides a forum for naval cooperation and dialogue among Indian Ocean littoral States.
  • IONS working groups: Support cooperation in areas such as maritime security, humanitarian assistance and information exchange.
  • Combined Maritime Forces: A multinational maritime partnership focused on security and stability in key maritime areas.
  • Bilateral and trilateral exercises: Improve interoperability, communication and operational cooperation with partner navies.

10. QUAD and Indo-Pacific Cooperation

The Quadrilateral Security Dialogue brings together India, Australia, Japan and the United States. It is a diplomatic partnership focused on practical cooperation in the Indo-Pacific rather than a formal collective-defence alliance.

Areas of cooperation

  • Maritime domain awareness and regional security.
  • Critical and emerging technologies.
  • Resilient supply chains and infrastructure.
  • Climate action and clean energy.
  • Health security and disaster response.
  • Cyber security and standards cooperation.

For India, the QUAD provides a platform for cooperation with important partners while retaining strategic autonomy and independent decision-making.

11. ASEAN and Regional Architecture

ASEAN is central to the diplomatic architecture of the Indo-Pacific. India engages with ASEAN through the East Asia Summit, ASEAN Regional Forum, ASEAN Defence Ministers' Meeting-Plus and other regional platforms.

  • Act East Policy: Strengthens India's political, economic and strategic engagement with Southeast Asia.
  • ASEAN-India relations: Cover trade, connectivity, maritime cooperation, education, technology and cultural links.
  • East Asia Summit: Provides a leaders-led forum for strategic dialogue on regional and global issues.
  • ASEAN Outlook on the Indo-Pacific: Emphasises inclusiveness, ASEAN centrality, cooperation and respect for international law.

India's engagement with ASEAN helps connect its continental and maritime interests and supports a multipolar regional order.

12. China's Maritime Presence and Strategic Competition

China's expanding economic, diplomatic and naval presence in the Indian Ocean has implications for India's maritime security and regional influence.

  • Growing naval deployments and port visits.
  • Infrastructure and port-related projects in Indian Ocean countries.
  • Strategic access and logistics arrangements.
  • Submarine deployments and maritime surveillance concerns.
  • Competition over regional partnerships and connectivity corridors.

India's response involves stronger maritime capabilities, partnerships with regional States, information sharing, infrastructure development and diplomatic engagement. It should also recognise that neighbouring countries have legitimate development interests and the right to maintain diverse external partnerships.

13. Blue Economy and Sustainable Ocean Governance

The blue economy refers to sustainable economic activity associated with oceans, seas and coastal resources. It includes fisheries, shipping, ports, marine biotechnology, offshore renewable energy, tourism and seabed-related activities.

Opportunities

  • Expansion of sustainable fisheries and aquaculture.
  • Port-led development and maritime logistics.
  • Offshore wind and ocean-based renewable energy.
  • Marine research, biotechnology and new technologies.
  • Coastal tourism and livelihood development.

Risks and safeguards

  • Overfishing and depletion of marine resources.
  • Plastic pollution, oil spills and coastal degradation.
  • Displacement of traditional fishing communities.
  • Damage to coral reefs, mangroves and marine biodiversity.
  • Climate change, ocean warming and sea-level rise.

Blue economy policies should integrate ecological sustainability, scientific assessment, community participation and equitable sharing of benefits.

14. Challenges to India's Maritime and Indo-Pacific Policy

  • Strategic competition among major powers.
  • Limited naval and coast guard resources relative to the scale of maritime responsibilities.
  • Threats to sea lines of communication and maritime chokepoints.
  • Illegal fishing, trafficking, piracy and maritime terrorism.
  • Vulnerability of ports, undersea cables and digital maritime infrastructure.
  • Climate risks affecting island States and coastal communities.
  • Uneven maritime capabilities among regional partners.
  • Balancing strategic partnerships with independent foreign policy choices.
  • Need for coordination among civilian, military, diplomatic and commercial agencies.

15. Way Forward

  1. Strengthen maritime capabilities: Improve surveillance, naval readiness, coast guard capacity and logistics.
  2. Expand information sharing: Strengthen maritime domain awareness and cooperation with regional partners.
  3. Protect critical infrastructure: Improve resilience of ports, shipping systems, offshore facilities and undersea cables.
  4. Promote international law: Support peaceful settlement of disputes and compliance with UNCLOS.
  5. Deepen regional partnerships: Work with ASEAN, island States and Indian Ocean littoral countries.
  6. Build disaster-response capacity: Strengthen humanitarian assistance, search and rescue and regional emergency coordination.
  7. Promote sustainable blue economy: Protect marine ecosystems and support coastal livelihoods.
  8. Improve institutional coordination: Integrate diplomatic, naval, coast guard, commercial and environmental planning.
  9. Maintain strategic autonomy: Cooperate with partners while preserving independent decision-making.
