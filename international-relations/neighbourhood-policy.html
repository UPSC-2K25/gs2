1. Meaning and Strategic Importance

Neighbourhood policy refers to India's political, economic, security, diplomatic and developmental engagement with countries in its immediate geographical neighbourhood. It is a central component of India's foreign policy because regional stability directly affects national security, economic growth and domestic development.

India's location, long land borders, extensive coastline and position in the Indian Ocean create deep interdependence with neighbouring States. Developments in these countries can affect border management, migration, trade, water security, energy, maritime routes and internal security.

  • Maintain peaceful and stable borders.
  • Promote regional trade, investment and connectivity.
  • Prevent the use of neighbouring territories by terrorist and criminal networks.
  • Strengthen energy, water and transport cooperation.
  • Respond to humanitarian emergencies and natural disasters.
  • Preserve regional influence and prevent destabilising external competition.
  • Develop people-to-people, cultural, educational and religious links.

2. India's Neighbourhood: Geographic and Strategic Context

India's immediate neighbourhood includes countries sharing land or maritime proximity with it. The wider regional environment also includes the Indian Ocean, the Bay of Bengal, the Arabian Sea and the Himalayan region.

Land neighbours

  • Pakistan
  • China
  • Nepal
  • Bhutan
  • Bangladesh
  • Myanmar

Maritime neighbours and close regional partners

  • Sri Lanka
  • Maldives
  • Other Indian Ocean island and littoral States, including Mauritius and Seychelles, which are important partners in India's wider maritime engagement.

Afghanistan is also strategically relevant to India's continental neighbourhood, connectivity interests and regional security, although India does not share a currently administered open land border with it.

3. Evolution of Neighbourhood Policy

Early regional engagement

After independence, India sought regional stability through diplomacy, cooperation and support for decolonisation. Partition, disputes over borders and water, and the security environment shaped early relations with neighbouring States.

Regional cooperation

India participated in the creation of the South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation in 1985 and later supported subregional and connectivity initiatives. Regional cooperation has, however, been constrained by political tensions and uneven economic integration.

Look East and Act East

The Look East Policy, launched in the early 1990s, expanded India's economic and strategic engagement with Southeast Asia. The Act East Policy, announced in 2014, gave renewed emphasis to connectivity, commerce, culture and strategic cooperation, particularly through India's northeastern region and the Bay of Bengal.

Neighbourhood First

The Neighbourhood First approach prioritises relations with immediate neighbours through high-level engagement, development cooperation, connectivity, capacity building, humanitarian assistance and security cooperation.

SAGAR and wider maritime engagement

The SAGAR vision—Security and Growth for All in the Region—emphasises maritime cooperation, capacity building, regional stability and sustainable development across the Indian Ocean.

4. Core Instruments of Neighbourhood Policy

  • Diplomatic engagement: Political dialogue, high-level visits, bilateral commissions and crisis communication.
  • Development partnership: Grants, concessional credit, infrastructure projects, training and capacity building.
  • Connectivity: Roads, railways, inland waterways, ports, energy grids and digital links.
  • Trade and investment: Market access, customs cooperation, border trade and regional value chains.
  • Security cooperation: Intelligence sharing, border management, counter-terrorism and maritime surveillance.
  • Humanitarian assistance: Disaster relief, medical supplies, evacuation and emergency support.
  • People-to-people ties: Education, tourism, cultural exchanges, religious links and cross-border communities.
  • Regional platforms: SAARC, BIMSTEC, BBIN, IORA and other functional arrangements.

5. Country-specific Priorities

Bangladesh

  • Cooperation on trade, connectivity, power and energy, inland waterways and border management.
  • Management of migration, smuggling, trafficking and transnational crime.
  • Resolution of outstanding river-water issues through dialogue and cooperative mechanisms.
  • Implementation and maintenance of connectivity arrangements linking India's northeastern region with wider regional networks.
  • Maintaining constructive relations through political transitions and domestic developments.

The Land Boundary Agreement implemented in 2015 was a major achievement in resolving the enclave issue. The Teesta water-sharing question remains a significant bilateral concern.

Nepal

  • Deep historical, cultural, religious and social links, including extensive cross-border movement.
  • Trade, transit, hydropower, petroleum infrastructure and connectivity cooperation.
  • Border management and dialogue on territorial differences.
  • Support for Nepal's development priorities while respecting its sovereignty and domestic political choices.

The 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship remains an important framework in bilateral relations and has also been the subject of debate and calls for review.

Bhutan

  • Close cooperation in hydropower development, trade and infrastructure.
  • Security coordination in the Himalayan region.
  • Educational, technical and development assistance.
  • Support for Bhutan's development priorities and strategic autonomy.

Hydropower cooperation is important for both countries, but it requires attention to project economics, environmental sustainability and long-term energy arrangements.

Sri Lanka

  • Maritime security and cooperation in the Indian Ocean.
  • Trade, investment, energy connectivity and port-related cooperation.
  • Fishermen-related concerns and maritime boundary management.
  • Reconciliation, devolution and the rights of Tamil communities.
  • Humanitarian and financial support during periods of economic distress.

India's approach must balance Sri Lanka's sovereignty with legitimate concerns about the welfare and rights of affected communities and the stability of the wider region.

Maldives

  • Maritime security, surveillance and protection of sea lines of communication.
  • Infrastructure, tourism, health, education and development cooperation.
  • Climate resilience and support for vulnerable island communities.
  • Respect for domestic political choices while maintaining strategic and economic engagement.

Maldives is strategically important because of its location near major Indian Ocean shipping routes.

Myanmar

  • Border security and prevention of insurgent and criminal networks.
  • Connectivity with India's northeastern region and Southeast Asia.
  • Energy, trade and infrastructure cooperation.
  • Humanitarian concerns arising from conflict and displacement.
  • Balancing engagement with security interests, democratic concerns and regional stability.

Myanmar's internal conflict creates serious challenges for India's border management, connectivity projects and humanitarian responsibilities.

Pakistan

  • Cross-border terrorism and security concerns.
  • Territorial disputes and the wider conflict environment.
  • Trade, transit and people-to-people links constrained by political tensions.
  • Management of ceasefire arrangements and communication channels.

India has maintained that meaningful normalisation requires an environment free from terrorism and violence. Relations remain affected by long-standing security and political disputes.

China

  • Border stability and the unresolved boundary question.
  • Trade asymmetry, investment and technology dependencies.
  • Regional influence and strategic competition in South Asia and the Indian Ocean.
  • Multilateral cooperation where interests converge.

China is both a major economic partner and a strategic competitor. Border peace and tranquillity are essential for broader bilateral relations.

6. India's Northeastern Region and Neighbourhood Connectivity

India's northeastern States are central to its neighbourhood and Act East strategies. Their geographic position creates opportunities for trade and connectivity with Bangladesh, Bhutan, Nepal and Myanmar and onward access to Southeast Asia.

  • Kaladan Multi-Modal Transit Transport Project: Intended to connect India's eastern seaboard with Mizoram through maritime, inland-waterway and road links via Myanmar.
  • India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway: Intended to improve road connectivity between India and mainland Southeast Asia.
  • BBIN Motor Vehicles Agreement: A subregional initiative involving Bangladesh, Bhutan, India and Nepal to facilitate movement of passenger, personal and cargo vehicles, subject to implementation arrangements.
  • Bangladesh connectivity: Inland waterways, rail links, road links and access to ports can improve the economic integration of India's Northeast.
  • Border haats: Support local trade and livelihoods in designated border areas.

Connectivity projects require political stability, local consultation, environmental safeguards, security arrangements and viable commercial operations.

7. Regional Security and Border Management

India's neighbourhood presents a range of traditional and non-traditional security challenges.

  • Cross-border terrorism and extremist networks.
  • Insurgency, arms smuggling and organised crime.
  • Human trafficking, narcotics and illegal trade.
  • Irregular migration and humanitarian displacement.
  • Cyber threats and illicit financial flows.
  • Maritime piracy, illegal fishing and trafficking by sea.
  • Border infrastructure and surveillance requirements in difficult terrain.

Effective border management requires cooperation with neighbouring governments, intelligence sharing, technology, local development and protection of legitimate cross-border livelihoods.

8. Water, Energy and Environmental Cooperation

Shared rivers, watersheds, ecosystems and energy networks make regional cooperation essential for sustainable development.

  • Transboundary rivers: Require data sharing, flood forecasting, water allocation dialogue and ecological safeguards.
  • Hydropower: Offers opportunities for regional electricity trade but requires equitable benefit-sharing and environmental assessment.
  • Climate resilience: Cooperation is important for floods, cyclones, glacial risks, coastal erosion and disaster preparedness.
  • Renewable energy: Cross-border power trade can support energy security and regional integration.
  • Disaster response: Shared early-warning systems and emergency coordination can reduce loss of life and economic damage.

Water cooperation should be based on equitable and reasonable utilisation, relevant treaty commitments, reliable scientific data and the interests of affected communities.

9. External Powers and Strategic Competition

Major powers increasingly engage South Asian countries through infrastructure finance, defence cooperation, trade, technology and diplomatic partnerships. This creates opportunities for regional development but can also intensify strategic competition.

  • China's infrastructure, connectivity and maritime engagement in the region.
  • External investment and financing in ports, energy and transport projects.
  • Defence and security partnerships involving extra-regional powers.
  • Debt sustainability, transparency and long-term economic viability of infrastructure projects.
  • Competition over standards, digital infrastructure and critical technologies.

India's response should focus on credible, timely and financially sustainable alternatives rather than treating every external project as an automatic security threat.

10. Challenges in India's Neighbourhood Policy

  • Political instability and frequent changes in government or policy priorities.
  • Historical mistrust and perceptions of asymmetry between India and smaller neighbours.
  • Unresolved territorial, water and security disputes.
  • Slow project execution and bureaucratic delays.
  • Limited regional trade integration and inadequate transport infrastructure.
  • External power competition and strategic uncertainty.
  • Cross-border terrorism, organised crime and trafficking.
  • Climate vulnerability and shared environmental risks.
  • Domestic political sensitivities that complicate bilateral negotiations.
  • Public perception and communication gaps concerning India's intentions and projects.

11. Reform Priorities

  1. Respect sovereignty: Build partnerships based on mutual respect, equality and sensitivity to domestic political contexts.
  2. Improve delivery: Complete development and connectivity projects on time with transparent monitoring.
  3. Promote economic integration: Reduce non-tariff barriers, improve customs procedures and facilitate regional value chains.
  4. Strengthen subregional cooperation: Use BIMSTEC, BBIN and other functional arrangements where wider regional platforms are constrained.
  5. Build local benefits: Ensure projects generate employment, livelihoods and opportunities for border communities.
  6. Improve water diplomacy: Use joint data, technical dialogue and transparent benefit-sharing arrangements.
  7. Deepen maritime cooperation: Enhance information sharing, disaster response and maritime domain awareness.
  8. Offer sustainable financing: Support projects with realistic costs, maintenance plans and environmental safeguards.
  9. Strengthen public diplomacy: Communicate India's policies and development assistance transparently.
  10. Institutionalise crisis management: Maintain diplomatic channels and mechanisms for rapid response during political or humanitarian crises.

12. Prelims Knowledge and Mains Analysis

Prelims focus

  • Countries sharing land borders with India.
  • Neighbourhood First, Act East and SAGAR.
  • SAARC, BIMSTEC and BBIN membership and objectives.
  • Kaladan project and India-Myanmar-Thailand Trilateral Highway.
  • Major bilateral treaties and connectivity arrangements.
  • Shared rivers and relevant water cooperation mechanisms.

Mains focus

Analyse neighbourhood policy through the interdependence of security, development, connectivity, political trust and regional power competition. A balanced answer should recognise India's legitimate security concerns while emphasising sovereignty, mutual benefit and credible regional partnerships.
