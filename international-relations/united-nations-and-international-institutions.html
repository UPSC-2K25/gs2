1. Evolution of the United Nations

The United Nations was established in 1945 after the Second World War to maintain international peace and security, develop friendly relations among nations, promote international cooperation and encourage respect for human rights.

The UN Charter was signed on 26 June 1945 and entered into force on 24 October 1945. The organisation succeeded the League of Nations, whose institutional weaknesses and inability to prevent global conflict had exposed the need for a more effective system of collective security.

Purposes of the United Nations

  • Maintain international peace and security.
  • Develop friendly relations among nations based on equal rights and self-determination.
  • Achieve international cooperation in solving economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems.
  • Promote and encourage respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms.
  • Serve as a centre for harmonising the actions of nations.

2. Principles of the UN Charter

  • Sovereign equality of all members.
  • Good-faith fulfilment of Charter obligations.
  • Peaceful settlement of international disputes.
  • Refraining from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any State, or otherwise inconsistently with UN purposes.
  • Assistance to the United Nations in actions taken in accordance with the Charter.
  • Ensuring that non-member States act consistently with these principles where necessary for international peace and security.
  • Non-intervention in matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction, subject to the Charter's enforcement provisions.

These principles form the foundation of the post-1945 international legal order. Their interpretation and application remain contested in situations involving intervention, self-defence, humanitarian crises and competing claims of sovereignty.

3. Principal Organs of the United Nations

General Assembly

The General Assembly is the main deliberative, policymaking and representative organ of the UN. All member States have equal representation, with one vote each.

  • Discusses issues within the scope of the Charter.
  • Approves the UN budget and assesses contributions.
  • Elects non-permanent members of the Security Council and members of other bodies as provided by the Charter.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General on the recommendation of the Security Council.
  • Adopts resolutions and recommendations, which are generally not binding on member States except where the Charter provides otherwise.

Security Council

The Security Council has primary responsibility for maintaining international peace and security.

  • Has 15 members: five permanent and ten non-permanent members.
  • The permanent members are China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States.
  • Non-permanent members are elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms, with seats distributed by regional groups.
  • Substantive decisions require the prescribed majority and the absence of a veto by a permanent member.
  • It may impose sanctions and authorise enforcement measures under the Charter.

The veto is a central source of controversy because it allows any permanent member to block substantive decisions even where there is broad support among other members.

Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)

ECOSOC coordinates economic, social and related work within the UN system. It has 54 members elected by the General Assembly for staggered three-year terms.

  • Promotes international cooperation on economic and social issues.
  • Coordinates specialised agencies and functional commissions.
  • Provides a forum for policy dialogue and development cooperation.
  • Facilitates engagement with non-governmental organisations through consultative arrangements.

International Court of Justice (ICJ)

The ICJ is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations. It is located at The Hague in the Netherlands.

  • Consists of 15 judges elected by the General Assembly and Security Council.
  • Decides contentious cases between States that have accepted its jurisdiction.
  • Provides advisory opinions on legal questions referred by authorised UN organs and agencies.
  • Its contentious jurisdiction is based on State consent.

Individuals and private corporations cannot directly bring contentious cases before the ICJ.

Secretariat

The Secretariat carries out the day-to-day administrative and operational work of the United Nations. It is headed by the Secretary-General, who is appointed by the General Assembly on the recommendation of the Security Council.

  • Supports UN organs and international negotiations.
  • Conducts research and prepares reports.
  • Coordinates peacekeeping and humanitarian operations.
  • Performs administrative and diplomatic functions.

Trusteeship Council

The Trusteeship Council was established to supervise trust territories and guide them towards self-government or independence. It suspended regular operations in 1994 after the last trust territory, Palau, became independent.

4. UN Security Council and Collective Security

The Charter gives the Security Council primary responsibility for international peace and security. Its authority includes determining threats to peace, breaches of peace and acts of aggression, and deciding appropriate responses.

Key provisions

  • Chapter VI: Encourages peaceful settlement of disputes.
  • Chapter VII: Provides for action in cases of threats to peace, breaches of peace and acts of aggression.
  • Article 51: Recognises the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs, until the Security Council takes necessary measures, subject to the Charter's conditions.

Challenges

  • Veto power can produce institutional paralysis.
  • Permanent membership reflects the power structure of 1945.
  • Geographic and demographic representation is unequal.
  • Political interests of powerful States influence decisions.
  • Enforcement of resolutions depends on State cooperation and institutional capacity.

5. UN Security Council Reform

Reform of the Security Council is a long-standing demand because the contemporary distribution of economic, political and demographic power differs substantially from that of 1945.

India's position

  • Supports expansion in both permanent and non-permanent membership.
  • Seeks a permanent seat for India based on its population, economic importance, peacekeeping contribution and international role.
  • Advocates greater representation for Africa, Asia-Pacific and Latin America.
  • Supports a more transparent and accountable Council.
  • Participates in the G4 initiative with Brazil, Germany and Japan.

Major obstacles

  • Disagreement over the number and categories of new members.
  • Differences over extension of veto power to new permanent members.
  • Competing regional claims and rivalries.
  • Requirement of Charter amendment, including ratification by the permanent members.

Reform requires broad political agreement, not merely a General Assembly resolution expressing support for expansion.

6. UN Peacekeeping Operations

UN peacekeeping operations help countries move from conflict towards peace. They are generally deployed with the consent of the main parties, operate with impartiality and use force only in self-defence and defence of their mandate, subject to mission-specific rules.

Functions

  • Monitor ceasefires and peace agreements.
  • Protect civilians within the mission mandate.
  • Support disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration.
  • Assist elections and institution-building.
  • Support human rights monitoring and rule-of-law institutions.
  • Facilitate humanitarian access where mandated.

India's contribution

India has been a major contributor to UN peacekeeping personnel and has deployed military, police and medical contingents in several missions.

  • Reflects India's commitment to multilateral peace and security.
  • Enhances India's diplomatic standing and operational experience.
  • Provides an opportunity to advocate for better safety, equipment and representation of troop-contributing countries in decision-making.

Challenges

  • Complex conflicts without clear political settlements.
  • Threats to peacekeepers and civilians.
  • Limited resources and unclear mandates.
  • Political differences between the Security Council and troop-contributing countries.
  • Accountability and conduct concerns in some missions.

7. UN Human Rights System

The UN human rights architecture includes Charter-based bodies, treaty-monitoring committees and specialised mechanisms.

Human Rights Council

  • Established in 2006, replacing the Commission on Human Rights.
  • Has 47 members elected by the General Assembly.
  • Conducts the Universal Periodic Review of the human rights record of all UN member States.
  • Appoints Special Rapporteurs and other special procedure mandate holders.
  • May establish commissions of inquiry and fact-finding missions.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

Adopted by the General Assembly in 1948, the UDHR sets out a common standard of human rights. It is not itself a treaty, but it has had a profound influence on international law and constitutional systems.

Major human rights treaties

  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights.
  • Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child.
  • Convention against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.
  • International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination.

8. Specialised Agencies and Related Institutions

  • World Health Organization (WHO): International public health coordination and technical guidance.
  • UNESCO: Education, science, culture and heritage protection.
  • International Labour Organization (ILO): Labour standards, decent work and tripartite cooperation.
  • Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO): Food security, agriculture and nutrition.
  • International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO): International civil aviation standards and cooperation.
  • International Maritime Organization (IMO): Shipping safety, maritime security and pollution prevention.
  • International Telecommunication Union (ITU): Global telecommunications and radio-frequency coordination.
  • World Meteorological Organization (WMO): Weather, climate and hydrological cooperation.
  • World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO): International intellectual property cooperation.
  • International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD): Rural development and agricultural finance.

Specialised agencies are autonomous international organisations linked to the UN through agreements. They have their own constitutions, membership arrangements and governing bodies.

9. UN Development System

The United Nations supports development through funds, programmes, specialised agencies and country-level coordination.

  • UNDP: Poverty reduction, governance, resilience and sustainable development.
  • UNICEF: Child rights, health, education and protection.
  • UNFPA: Population, reproductive health and gender equality.
  • UN Women: Gender equality and women's empowerment.
  • WFP: Emergency food assistance and food security.
  • UN-Habitat: Sustainable urbanisation and human settlements.
  • UNHCR: Protection of refugees and stateless persons and assistance to displaced populations.

10. Sustainable Development Goals

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development was adopted by UN member States in 2015. It contains 17 Sustainable Development Goals and 169 targets.

The SDGs integrate economic, social and environmental dimensions of development and are intended to be universal, interconnected and applicable to all countries.

Key principles

  • Leave no one behind.
  • Integrate poverty reduction with social inclusion and environmental sustainability.
  • Mobilise domestic and international resources.
  • Promote partnerships among governments, businesses and civil society.
  • Use measurable targets and indicators for accountability.

Relevance to India

The SDGs connect with India's priorities in poverty reduction, health, education, gender equality, water, sanitation, clean energy, sustainable cities and climate action. Implementation requires coordination between the Union, States, local governments and non-governmental stakeholders.

11. International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)

The IAEA is an autonomous international organisation related to the UN system. It promotes the peaceful use of nuclear energy and seeks to prevent the diversion of nuclear technology to military purposes through safeguards and verification arrangements.

Major functions

  • Administer safeguards agreements and verification activities.
  • Promote peaceful nuclear applications in energy, health, agriculture and industry.
  • Develop nuclear safety and security standards.
  • Provide technical cooperation and capacity building.

India is not a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty but has entered into safeguards arrangements with the IAEA covering specified facilities. Its civil nuclear cooperation framework must be distinguished from the safeguards obligations applicable to individual facilities.

12. Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW)

The OPCW implements the Chemical Weapons Convention, which prohibits the development, production, stockpiling, transfer and use of chemical weapons.

  • Monitors compliance with the Convention.
  • Conducts inspections and verification.
  • Supports the destruction of declared chemical weapons stockpiles.
  • Promotes peaceful uses of chemistry and international cooperation.

India is a State Party to the Chemical Weapons Convention and has undertaken obligations under its verification and declaration framework.

13. International Criminal Court (ICC)

The ICC is a permanent international criminal court established by the Rome Statute, which entered into force in 2002. It prosecutes individuals for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and the crime of aggression, subject to the Statute's jurisdictional conditions.

Key features

  • It is independent of the United Nations, although the Security Council has a role under the Rome Statute.
  • It is based on the principle of complementarity: national jurisdictions have primary responsibility where they are genuinely willing and able to investigate or prosecute.
  • It prosecutes individuals, not States.
  • Its jurisdiction is limited by the Rome Statute and relevant jurisdictional conditions.

India is not a State Party to the Rome Statute. India's concerns have included jurisdiction, sovereignty, the role of the Security Council and the balance between international accountability and national legal systems.

14. International Organisation for Migration (IOM)

The IOM is an intergovernmental organisation and a related organisation of the United Nations. It works on migration governance, migrant protection, humanitarian response and migration data.

  • Supports orderly and humane migration management.
  • Provides assistance to migrants and displaced populations.
  • Supports governments in migration policy and border management.
  • Collects data and conducts research on migration trends.

Its work is relevant to India because of international labour migration, diaspora welfare, remittances and the need for safe and regular migration pathways.

15. Challenges Facing the United Nations

  • Security Council composition and veto-related paralysis.
  • Unequal representation of developing countries.
  • Financial constraints and dependence on member contributions.
  • Difficulty in enforcing international decisions.
  • Geopolitical divisions among major powers.
  • Complex conflicts involving non-State actors.
  • Challenges in responding to cyber threats, pandemics and climate-related risks.
  • Need to strengthen transparency, accountability and institutional efficiency.

16. India's Approach to the United Nations

  • Advocate comprehensive reform of the Security Council.
  • Promote representation of developing countries in global institutions.
  • Support peaceful settlement of disputes and the rule of international law.
  • Contribute to UN peacekeeping and humanitarian activities.
  • Promote sustainable development and climate justice.
  • Strengthen multilateral responses to terrorism and transnational crime.
  • Preserve national sovereignty while engaging constructively with international institutions.

17. Way Forward

  1. Reform the Security Council to improve legitimacy and representativeness.
  2. Strengthen accountability and transparency across UN institutions.
  3. Improve peacekeeping mandates, resources and troop-contributor consultation.
  4. Strengthen international cooperation on emerging technologies and cyber security.
  5. Ensure predictable financing for development and humanitarian programmes.
  6. Improve coordination among specialised agencies and national governments.
  7. Increase participation of developing countries in international standard-setting.
  8. Strengthen the capacity of multilateral institutions to respond to global crises.

  • Human Rights Council and Universal Periodic Review.

Mains focus

Critically examine the relevance and limitations of the United Nations in contemporary global governance. Discuss Security Council reform, India's permanent membership claim, peacekeeping, human rights and the need for stronger multilateral institutions.
