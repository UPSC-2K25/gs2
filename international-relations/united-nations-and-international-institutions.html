United Nations and International Institutions

1. Evolution and Foundations of the United Nations

The United Nations (UN) was established in 1945 after the Second World War to prevent another global conflict and create an institutional framework for international peace, security, cooperation and human rights.

  • Atlantic Charter, 1941: Articulated principles that influenced the post-war international order.
  • Declaration by United Nations, 1942: Allied countries committed themselves to the principles of the Atlantic Charter.
  • Dumbarton Oaks Conference, 1944: Developed proposals for the structure of the future organisation.
  • Yalta Conference, 1945: Addressed important questions relating to the proposed Security Council voting arrangements.
  • San Francisco Conference, 1945: Representatives of 50 countries negotiated and signed the UN Charter on 26 June 1945.
  • 24 October 1945: The UN Charter entered into force. This date is observed as United Nations Day.

Why was the UN created?

  • The League of Nations had failed to prevent aggression and the Second World War.
  • There was a need for a stronger collective-security system.
  • Economic instability and conflict demonstrated the importance of international cooperation.
  • Decolonisation and self-determination required an international institutional framework.
  • Human rights protection needed to become a central concern of international relations.

Purposes under Article 1 of the UN Charter

  1. Maintain international peace and security.
  2. Develop friendly relations among nations based on respect for equal rights and self-determination of peoples.
  3. Achieve international cooperation in solving economic, social, cultural and humanitarian problems and promoting human rights.
  4. Serve as a centre for harmonising the actions of nations in pursuit of common ends.

The UN is an organisation of sovereign States. It is not a world government and does not possess general authority to legislate for all countries.

2. Principles of the UN Charter

Article 2 of the Charter establishes principles governing the conduct of the organisation and its members.

  • Sovereign equality: All members are formally sovereign equals, regardless of population, territory or economic power.
  • Good-faith performance: Members must fulfil their Charter obligations in good faith.
  • Peaceful settlement: International disputes should be resolved through peaceful means without endangering international peace, security and justice.
  • Prohibition on the use of force: Members must refrain from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any State, or otherwise inconsistently with UN purposes.
  • Assistance to the UN: Members must assist the organisation in actions taken in accordance with the Charter.
  • Non-member States: The UN shall ensure that non-members act consistently with Charter principles so far as necessary for international peace and security.
  • Domestic jurisdiction: The Charter does not generally authorise intervention in matters essentially within domestic jurisdiction, subject to Chapter VII enforcement measures.

Important legal qualifications

  • The prohibition on force is not absolute: Article 51 recognises individual and collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs, subject to its conditions.
  • The Security Council may authorise enforcement measures under Chapter VII.
  • Consent-based intervention and other contested legal doctrines must be assessed under international law rather than treated as automatic exceptions.
  • Humanitarian concerns do not by themselves create an unrestricted unilateral right to use force.

These principles reflect the tension between sovereignty, territorial integrity, human rights, collective security and international responsibility.

3. Membership and Institutional Structure

Membership

  • The UN began with 51 members, including the original 50 signatories and Poland.
  • It has 193 member States.
  • The Holy See and the State of Palestine participate as non-member observer States.
  • Admission of a new member requires a recommendation by the Security Council followed by a decision of the General Assembly.

Admission, suspension and expulsion

  • Article 4: Membership is open to peace-loving States that accept Charter obligations and are able and willing to carry them out.
  • Article 5: The General Assembly may suspend a member from the exercise of membership rights on the recommendation of the Security Council.
  • Article 6: The General Assembly may expel a member that persistently violates Charter principles, on the recommendation of the Security Council.

Six principal organs

  1. General Assembly.
  2. Security Council.
  3. Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC).
  4. International Court of Justice (ICJ).
  5. Secretariat.
  6. Trusteeship Council.

The principal organs are established by the Charter. The UN system also includes funds, programmes, specialised agencies, commissions, offices and related organisations with different legal and institutional relationships to the UN.

4. General Assembly

The General Assembly is the principal deliberative, policymaking and representative organ of the UN. All member States are represented, with one vote each.

Composition and sessions

  • All 193 member States participate.
  • Regular sessions are held annually, generally beginning in September.
  • Special and emergency special sessions may be convened under the Charter and established procedures.
  • Six main committees examine issues before the Assembly.

Major functions

  • Discusses questions within the scope of the Charter.
  • Considers and approves the UN budget.
  • Elects non-permanent members of the Security Council and members of other bodies as provided by the Charter.
  • Appoints the Secretary-General on the recommendation of the Security Council.
  • Elects members of ECOSOC and, jointly with the Security Council, judges of the ICJ.
  • Promotes international cooperation and the development of international law.
  • Considers reports of other UN organs.

Voting

  • Each member has one vote.
  • Important questions require a two-thirds majority of members present and voting.
  • Other questions are decided by a majority of members present and voting.
  • Abstentions are not counted as votes cast for determining the majority.

Legal character of resolutions

Most General Assembly resolutions are recommendations and are not legally binding on member States. However, certain decisions within the Assembly's institutional authority, including budgetary and internal matters, have specific legal effects. General Assembly resolutions may also contribute to the development or evidence of customary international law, depending on their content and State practice.

Uniting for Peace Resolution, 1950

Under the Uniting for Peace procedure, where the Security Council fails to exercise its primary responsibility because of a lack of unanimity among permanent members, the General Assembly may consider the matter and recommend collective measures. Such recommendations do not automatically confer the binding enforcement powers of the Security Council.

Significance for India

  • Provides a forum where India has an equal formal vote with other members.
  • Supports coalition-building among developing countries.
  • Enables India to advocate reform of global governance and development institutions.
  • Provides a platform for debates on decolonisation, development, climate justice and international law.

5. UN Security Council

The Security Council has primary responsibility for maintaining international peace and security under the UN Charter.

Composition

  • 15 members in total.
  • Five permanent members (P5): China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States.
  • Ten non-permanent members elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms.
  • Non-permanent seats are allocated among regional groups.
  • Retiring non-permanent members are not immediately eligible for re-election.

Voting procedure

  • Procedural decisions require at least nine affirmative votes.
  • Substantive decisions require at least nine affirmative votes and no negative vote by a permanent member.
  • A permanent member's negative vote on a substantive matter is commonly called a veto.
  • Abstention by a permanent member does not ordinarily amount to a veto.
  • A party to a dispute is expected to abstain in certain decisions under Article 27(3), subject to the Charter's provisions.

Functions and powers

  • Investigates disputes and situations that may lead to international friction.
  • Recommends methods of peaceful settlement.
  • Determines the existence of a threat to peace, breach of peace or act of aggression.
  • Imposes non-military sanctions under Article 41.
  • May authorise military enforcement action under Article 42.
  • Establishes peacekeeping missions and special political missions.
  • Recommends the appointment of the Secretary-General and admission of new members.

Sanctions under Article 41

Non-military measures may include economic restrictions, arms embargoes, travel bans, asset freezes and restrictions on diplomatic or other relations. Sanctions may be imposed on States, entities or individuals, depending on the relevant resolution.

Challenges

  • Veto power can produce paralysis during major-power disagreements.
  • The membership structure reflects the power distribution of 1945.
  • Africa, Latin America and other regions lack permanent representation.
  • Selective enforcement creates concerns about legitimacy and consistency.
  • Implementation depends on State cooperation and institutional capacity.

6. Security Council Reform and India's Claim

The demand for Security Council reform arises from the gap between the contemporary distribution of global power and the institutional structure created in 1945.

Major reform demands

  • Expansion of both permanent and non-permanent membership.
  • Improved representation of Africa, Asia-Pacific and Latin America.
  • Greater transparency in Council working methods.
  • More accountable use of veto power.
  • Improved consultation with troop-contributing countries and affected States.

India's case for permanent membership

  • One of the world's most populous countries.
  • Major economy and important participant in global trade and development.
  • Long-standing contribution to UN peacekeeping.
  • Role in decolonisation, non-alignment and South-South cooperation.
  • Growing diplomatic, technological and strategic significance.
  • Claim to more representative and equitable global governance.

G4 initiative

India, Brazil, Germany and Japan coordinate their campaign for permanent membership and reform of the Security Council. Their proposal has encountered opposition and competing reform models.

Uniting for Consensus

The Uniting for Consensus group, which includes countries such as Italy, Pakistan, Mexico and Argentina, has generally opposed the creation of additional permanent seats and advocated alternative forms of expansion, particularly in the non-permanent category.

African position

The Ezulwini Consensus and Sirte Declaration articulate the African Union's demand for at least two permanent seats and five non-permanent seats for Africa, along with a position on veto rights.

Why reform is difficult

  • Disagreement over the number and categories of new seats.
  • Competing regional and national claims.
  • Disagreement over whether new permanent members should receive veto power.
  • Existing permanent members have a decisive role in Charter amendment ratification.
  • Different coalitions have not agreed on a single reform formula.

Legal procedure

Under Article 108, Charter amendments require adoption by a two-thirds majority of the General Assembly and ratification by two-thirds of UN members, including all permanent members of the Security Council. A General Assembly political declaration alone cannot change the Council's composition.

Intergovernmental negotiations

Security Council reform is discussed through intergovernmental negotiations in the General Assembly. These negotiations continue to address categories of membership, regional representation, veto, working methods and the relationship between the Council and the wider membership.

7. Economic and Social Council (ECOSOC)

ECOSOC is the principal UN organ for coordination, policy review and dialogue on international economic, social, cultural, educational, health and related issues.

Composition

  • Has 54 members elected by the General Assembly.
  • Members serve staggered three-year terms.
  • Seats are distributed among regional groups.

Functions

  • Coordinates economic and social work within the UN system.
  • Provides a forum for policy dialogue and development cooperation.
  • Coordinates functional commissions and regional commissions.
  • Promotes cooperation with specialised agencies.
  • Facilitates engagement with non-governmental organisations through consultative status.
  • Supports follow-up and review of international development commitments.

Important functional commissions

  • Commission on the Status of Women.
  • Commission for Social Development.
  • Statistical Commission.
  • Commission on Population and Development.
  • Commission on Narcotic Drugs.
  • Commission on Crime Prevention and Criminal Justice.
  • Commission on Science and Technology for Development.

Regional commissions

  • Economic Commission for Africa (ECA).
  • Economic Commission for Europe (ECE).
  • Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC).
  • Economic and Social Commission for Asia and the Pacific (ESCAP).
  • Economic and Social Commission for Western Asia (ESCWA).

Relevance for India

ECOSOC provides a forum for India to engage on sustainable development, social policy, technology, gender equality, poverty reduction and regional cooperation. Its work is also relevant to India's participation in the UN development system.

8. International Court of Justice (ICJ)

The ICJ is the principal judicial organ of the United Nations. It is located at The Hague, Netherlands, and operates under the UN Charter and its own Statute.

Composition

  • Consists of 15 judges elected for nine-year terms.
  • Judges are elected independently by the General Assembly and Security Council.
  • One-third of the membership is renewed every three years.
  • No two judges may be nationals of the same State.
  • Judges are elected in their individual capacity, not as government representatives.

Two principal functions

  • Contentious jurisdiction: Decides legal disputes between States that have accepted its jurisdiction.
  • Advisory jurisdiction: Gives advisory opinions on legal questions referred by authorised UN organs and specialised agencies.

Jurisdiction in contentious cases

ICJ contentious jurisdiction is based on State consent. Consent may arise through:

  • A special agreement between States referring a dispute to the Court.
  • A jurisdiction clause in a treaty.
  • Declarations accepting compulsory jurisdiction under Article 36(2) of the Statute, subject to their terms and reservations.

Legal effect of judgments

  • Judgments are binding on the parties to the particular case.
  • There is no general appeal against an ICJ judgment.
  • Article 94 of the Charter requires UN members to comply with judgments in cases to which they are parties.
  • If a party fails to perform its obligations, the other party may have recourse to the Security Council under Article 94(2).

Advisory opinions

Advisory opinions are generally not binding in the same manner as contentious judgments, although they carry significant legal authority and may clarify international law.

India and the ICJ

India has participated in contentious proceedings and advisory processes. The Kulbhushan Jadhav case, involving interpretation of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, illustrates the relevance of the ICJ to consular access and international legal obligations.

9. Secretariat and Secretary-General

The Secretariat carries out the day-to-day administrative, analytical, diplomatic and operational work of the United Nations.

Secretary-General

  • Appointed by the General Assembly on the recommendation of the Security Council.
  • Acts as the chief administrative officer of the organisation under Article 97.
  • May bring matters that in the Secretary-General's opinion threaten international peace and security to the attention of the Security Council under Article 99.
  • Uses good offices, mediation and diplomatic engagement to prevent and resolve conflicts.
  • Prepares reports and policy proposals and coordinates Secretariat activities.

Role and limitations

The Secretary-General has significant diplomatic influence but does not possess independent authority to compel sovereign States to comply with policy recommendations. The office operates within the Charter, mandates given by member States and available resources.

Administrative structure

The Secretariat includes departments, offices, regional commissions, country-level operations and field missions. Its responsibilities extend across peace and security, development, humanitarian affairs, human rights and institutional administration.

10. Trusteeship Council and Decolonisation

The Trusteeship Council was established to supervise trust territories and guide them towards self-government or independence.

  • It was one of the six principal organs of the UN.
  • Its work contributed to the decolonisation process.
  • Its regular operations were suspended in November 1994 after Palau, the last trust territory, became independent.
  • It has not been formally abolished; its procedures may be reactivated if required.

Decolonisation and self-determination

The UN played an important role in the international decolonisation process through the Charter, General Assembly resolutions and the work of its committees.

General Assembly Resolution 1514 (XV), the 1960 Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples, became a major expression of the international commitment to decolonisation.

India's own experience of colonialism shaped its support for self-determination, anti-colonial movements and the political equality of newly independent States.

11. UN Peacekeeping Operations

UN peacekeeping is a practical instrument for supporting countries transitioning from conflict towards peace. It is distinct from enforcement action authorised under Chapter VII, although some peacekeeping mandates may include robust powers to protect civilians and implement the mission's mandate.

Three traditional principles

  • Consent of the main parties: Peacekeeping operations generally require the consent of the principal parties to the conflict.
  • Impartiality: Peacekeepers must implement their mandate impartially; impartiality does not mean neutrality towards violations of the mandate.
  • Non-use of force except in self-defence and defence of the mandate: Force may be used within the mission's rules and mandate, including protection of civilians where authorised.

Functions

  • Monitor ceasefires and peace agreements.
  • Protect civilians within the mission mandate.
  • Support disarmament, demobilisation and reintegration.
  • Assist electoral processes and institution-building.
  • Support security-sector reform and rule-of-law institutions.
  • Monitor human rights and facilitate humanitarian access where mandated.
  • Support restoration of State authority and political processes.

Peacekeeping, peacemaking and peacebuilding

  • Peacemaking: Diplomatic efforts to bring parties to a conflict to agreement.
  • Peacekeeping: Deployment of personnel and resources to help maintain peace and security.
  • Peacebuilding: Long-term efforts to address root causes of conflict and strengthen institutions and resilience.
  • Peace enforcement: Coercive action, including use of force, authorised under the Charter where required.

Financing

Peacekeeping is financed through assessed contributions from UN members under a special scale of assessments approved by the General Assembly. The Security Council establishes or changes mission mandates, while the General Assembly approves budgets and allocates financial responsibility.

The UN does not maintain a standing army. Member States voluntarily contribute military, police and civilian personnel. The UN reimburses contributing countries under established arrangements for eligible personnel, equipment and services.

India's contribution

  • India has been a major contributor of military and police personnel to UN peacekeeping.
  • Indian contingents have served in several missions across different regions.
  • India has contributed medical, engineering, logistical and other specialist capabilities.
  • Indian women peacekeepers have contributed to efforts to improve gender-sensitive peacekeeping and community engagement.

Challenges

  • Complex conflicts involving non-State armed groups.
  • Weak or absent political settlements.
  • Threats to peacekeepers and civilians.
  • Inadequate resources, equipment and intelligence.
  • Unclear or unrealistic mandates.
  • Limited consultation with troop-contributing countries.
  • Accountability and misconduct concerns.

12. Peacebuilding Commission and Conflict Prevention

The Peacebuilding Commission (PBC) was established in 2005 as an intergovernmental advisory body to support countries emerging from conflict and improve coordination among relevant actors.

Functions

  • Bring together governments, UN entities, international financial institutions and other stakeholders.
  • Mobilise resources and advise on integrated peacebuilding strategies.
  • Support national ownership and inclusive political processes.
  • Promote long-term institutional and economic recovery.
  • Draw attention to countries at risk of relapse into conflict.

Peacebuilding Fund

The Peacebuilding Fund is a UN financing instrument that supports activities addressing conflict risks, institutional weaknesses and drivers of instability. It is distinct from the Peacebuilding Commission, which is an intergovernmental advisory body.

Prevention rather than reaction

Effective conflict prevention requires attention to political exclusion, inequality, weak institutions, resource competition, human rights violations and lack of economic opportunity. Peacekeeping alone cannot resolve these structural causes.

13. UN Human Rights System

The UN human rights system consists of Charter-based institutions, treaty-monitoring bodies, special procedures and the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights.

Human Rights Council (HRC)

  • Established by the General Assembly in 2006, replacing the Commission on Human Rights.
  • Has 47 members elected by the General Assembly.
  • Members serve three-year terms and are not eligible for immediate re-election after two consecutive terms.
  • Conducts the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) of all UN member States.
  • Appoints Special Rapporteurs and other special procedure mandate holders.
  • May establish commissions of inquiry and fact-finding missions.

Universal Periodic Review

The UPR is a peer-review mechanism through which the human rights record of every UN member State is examined periodically. It is based on State reports, UN information and contributions from other stakeholders.

  • It is universal and applies to all UN member States.
  • It is conducted through an intergovernmental review process.
  • Recommendations are made to the State under review.
  • It encourages dialogue, follow-up and domestic implementation.

Special Procedures

Special Rapporteurs, independent experts and working groups examine thematic or country-specific human rights issues. They may undertake visits, communicate concerns to governments and report to the Human Rights Council or General Assembly.

Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (OHCHR)

OHCHR leads UN efforts to promote and protect human rights. It provides technical assistance, monitors human rights situations and supports the work of UN human rights mechanisms.

14. International Human Rights Treaties and Treaty Bodies

International human rights treaties create legal obligations for States that become parties to them. Their monitoring bodies examine implementation and interpret treaty provisions within their respective mandates.

Major international instruments

  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), 1948: A declaration setting out a common standard of human rights; it is not itself a treaty.
  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR), 1966: Protects civil and political rights.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), 1966: Covers economic, social and cultural rights.
  • Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW), 1979: Addresses discrimination against women.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), 1989: Establishes rights and protections for children.
  • Convention against Torture (CAT), 1984: Requires States Parties to prevent and address torture.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD), 2006: Promotes equality and rights of persons with disabilities.
  • International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD), 1965: Prohibits racial discrimination.
  • International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance, 2006: Addresses enforced disappearance.

Major treaty-monitoring committees

  • Human Rights Committee – ICCPR.
  • Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights – ICESCR.
  • Committee on the Elimination of Discrimination against Women – CEDAW.
  • Committee on the Rights of the Child – CRC.
  • Committee against Torture – CAT.
  • Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities – CRPD.
  • Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination – ICERD.

How treaty bodies work

  • Examine periodic reports submitted by States Parties.
  • Issue concluding observations and recommendations.
  • Develop general comments or recommendations interpreting treaty obligations.
  • Some committees may consider individual communications where the State has accepted the relevant procedure.
  • Some treaties provide for inquiry procedures or inter-State complaints under specified conditions.

India and treaty obligations

India is party to several major international human rights treaties, including the ICCPR, ICESCR, CEDAW, CRC and CRPD. It has not ratified every human rights treaty. For examination purposes, distinguish signature, ratification, accession, entry into force and acceptance of optional complaint procedures.

International treaty obligations do not automatically operate as domestic legislation in every instance. Their implementation depends on constitutional provisions, domestic law and the nature of the obligation.

15. UN Development System: Funds, Programmes and Agencies

The UN development system works through funds, programmes, specialised agencies and country-level coordination mechanisms.

Funds and programmes

  • United Nations Development Programme (UNDP): Poverty reduction, governance, resilience and sustainable development.
  • United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF): Child rights, health, education, nutrition and protection.
  • United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA): Reproductive health, population data, gender equality and adolescent development.
  • UN Women: Gender equality and women's empowerment.
  • World Food Programme (WFP): Emergency food assistance, humanitarian response and food security.
  • UN-Habitat: Sustainable urbanisation and human settlements.
  • United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP): Environmental assessment, coordination and global environmental cooperation.
  • United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC): Work against drugs, organised crime, corruption and terrorism.
  • United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD): Trade, investment, development and economic policy analysis.

UN specialised agencies

Specialised agencies are autonomous international organisations linked to the UN through agreements. They have their own constitutions, membership arrangements, budgets and governing bodies.

  • World Health Organization (WHO): International public health coordination and technical guidance.
  • UNESCO: Education, science, culture and heritage protection.
  • International Labour Organization (ILO): Labour standards, decent work and tripartite cooperation among governments, employers and workers.
  • Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO): Food security, agriculture, nutrition and sustainable food systems.
  • International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO): International civil aviation standards and cooperation.
  • International Maritime Organization (IMO): Shipping safety, maritime security and prevention of marine pollution from ships.
  • International Telecommunication Union (ITU): Telecommunications, radio-frequency coordination and global connectivity standards.
  • World Meteorological Organization (WMO): Weather, climate and hydrological cooperation.
  • World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO): International intellectual property cooperation.
  • International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD): Rural development and agricultural finance.
  • International Civil Service Commission (ICSC): Not a specialised agency; it regulates conditions of service for the UN common system.

UN country teams

UN country teams coordinate the work of UN entities at country level. The Resident Coordinator leads the UN development system's country-level coordination, supporting national development priorities and cooperation among agencies.

16. Humanitarian System and Emergency Coordination

The UN coordinates international humanitarian action in response to armed conflict, natural disasters, displacement, food insecurity and other emergencies.

Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA)

  • Coordinates humanitarian response among UN agencies and partners.
  • Supports needs assessments and humanitarian planning.
  • Mobilises resources and promotes coordination among donors and implementing organisations.
  • Supports the humanitarian cluster coordination system.

Major humanitarian actors

  • UNHCR: Refugee protection and assistance to stateless and displaced populations within its mandate.
  • WFP: Food assistance and emergency logistics.
  • UNICEF: Child protection, nutrition, education and health in emergencies.
  • WHO: Public health coordination and emergency health support.
  • International Organization for Migration (IOM): Migration management and assistance to migrants and displaced populations.
  • UNRWA: Services and assistance for Palestine refugees within its mandate.

Humanitarian principles

  • Humanity: Address human suffering wherever it is found.
  • Impartiality: Provide assistance based on need without discrimination.
  • Neutrality: Do not take sides in hostilities or political, racial, religious or ideological controversies.
  • Independence: Maintain autonomy from political, economic, military or other objectives.

Challenges

  • Restricted humanitarian access in conflict zones.
  • Insufficient or unpredictable funding.
  • Security risks to humanitarian personnel.
  • Displacement and protracted refugee situations.
  • Coordination among governments, UN agencies and non-governmental organisations.

17. Sustainable Development Goals and the 2030 Agenda

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development was adopted by all UN member States in September 2015. It contains 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and 169 targets.

Five broad themes

  • People: End poverty and hunger and ensure dignity and equality.
  • Planet: Protect natural resources and address climate change.
  • Prosperity: Promote inclusive economic growth and decent work.
  • Peace: Promote peaceful, just and inclusive societies.
  • Partnership: Strengthen global cooperation and implementation resources.

Important characteristics

  • Universal: Applicable to developed and developing countries.
  • Integrated: Economic, social and environmental dimensions are interconnected.
  • Inclusive: Emphasises leaving no one behind.
  • Measurable: Uses targets and indicators to assess progress.
  • Partnership-based: Requires government, private-sector and civil-society participation.

Follow-up and review

The High-Level Political Forum on Sustainable Development (HLPF), convened under ECOSOC and the General Assembly, plays a central role in follow-up and review. Voluntary National Reviews (VNRs) allow countries to present progress, challenges and policy responses.

India and the SDGs

India's SDG implementation involves national and State-level planning, indicator frameworks, local institutions and sectoral programmes. NITI Aayog plays an important role in coordinating and monitoring SDG progress at the national level, including through the SDG India Index.

Implementation challenges

  • Financing gaps and limited fiscal capacity.
  • Unequal progress across regions and social groups.
  • Data gaps and differences in measurement capacity.
  • Coordination challenges among levels of government.
  • Climate change, conflict and economic shocks.

18. International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA)

The IAEA is an autonomous international organisation related to the UN system. It was established in 1957 and is headquartered in Vienna.

Core objectives

  • Promote peaceful uses of nuclear energy.
  • Prevent the diversion of nuclear technology from peaceful uses to nuclear weapons or other explosive devices.
  • Develop nuclear safety and security standards.
  • Provide technical cooperation in energy, health, agriculture and industry.

Safeguards

IAEA safeguards involve verification measures intended to ensure that nuclear material and facilities covered by relevant agreements are not diverted from peaceful purposes.

  • Safeguards agreements may arise under the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) or other arrangements.
  • Comprehensive safeguards agreements generally apply to non-nuclear-weapon States Parties to the NPT.
  • Additional Protocols provide supplementary access and information for strengthened verification where accepted.
  • Facility-specific safeguards may apply to States outside the NPT framework.

India and the IAEA

India is not a party to the NPT but has entered into safeguards arrangements covering specified civilian nuclear facilities. India-specific safeguards must be distinguished from the comprehensive safeguards obligations applicable to non-nuclear-weapon States Parties to the NPT.

19. Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons (OPCW)

The OPCW implements the Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC), which prohibits the development, production, acquisition, stockpiling, transfer and use of chemical weapons.

Principal functions

  • Monitor compliance with the Convention.
  • Conduct inspections and verification activities.
  • Verify destruction of declared chemical weapons stockpiles and production facilities.
  • Promote peaceful uses of chemistry.
  • Provide technical assistance and capacity building.

India is a State Party to the CWC and has undertaken obligations under its declaration, verification and destruction framework. The OPCW is an independent treaty organisation, not one of the six principal UN organs.

20. International Criminal Court (ICC)

The ICC is a permanent international criminal court established by the Rome Statute, which entered into force in 2002. It is headquartered in The Hague.

Crimes within its jurisdiction

  • Genocide.
  • Crimes against humanity.
  • War crimes.
  • Crime of aggression, subject to specific jurisdictional conditions.

Important principles

  • Individual criminal responsibility: The Court prosecutes individuals, not States.
  • Complementarity: National jurisdictions have primary responsibility where they are genuinely willing and able to investigate or prosecute.
  • Jurisdictional limits: Jurisdiction depends on the Rome Statute's territorial, nationality, temporal and other conditions.
  • Independent institution: The ICC is not a principal organ of the UN, although the Security Council has certain powers under the Rome Statute.

India and the ICC

India is not a State Party to the Rome Statute. India's concerns have included sovereignty, jurisdiction, the role of the Security Council and the relationship between international accountability and national legal systems.

21. International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS)

ITLOS is an independent judicial body established under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS). It is based in Hamburg, Germany.

Functions

  • Decides disputes concerning interpretation and application of UNCLOS where it has jurisdiction.
  • May prescribe provisional measures in specified circumstances.
  • Deals with prompt-release applications relating to detained vessels and crews.
  • Provides advisory opinions where authorised under the relevant legal framework.

Distinction from ICJ

The ICJ is the principal judicial organ of the UN and has broader jurisdiction under international law based on State consent. ITLOS is a specialised tribunal under UNCLOS, dealing with law-of-the-sea disputes. Neither is a general criminal court.

22. International Organization for Migration (IOM)

IOM is an intergovernmental organisation and a related organisation of the United Nations. It works on migration governance, migrant protection, humanitarian response and migration data.

  • Supports safe, orderly and regular migration.
  • Provides assistance to migrants and displaced populations.
  • Supports governments in migration policy and border management.
  • Collects data and conducts research on migration trends.
  • Supports humanitarian responses to displacement and emergencies.

IOM is relevant to India because of international labour migration, diaspora welfare, remittances, migrant protection and the need for safe and regular migration pathways.

23. Responsibility to Protect (R2P)

The Responsibility to Protect is a political commitment endorsed at the 2005 World Summit. It concerns the prevention of genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity.

Three pillars

  1. State responsibility: Every State has the primary responsibility to protect its population from the four specified crimes.
  2. International assistance: The international community should help States fulfil this responsibility through capacity building, diplomacy and prevention.
  3. Collective response: Where national authorities manifestly fail to protect populations, the international community may take collective action through the Security Council, in accordance with the UN Charter.

Debates and concerns

  • Risk of selective application by powerful States.
  • Potential misuse of humanitarian justifications for geopolitical intervention.
  • Tension between sovereignty and international protection responsibilities.
  • Difficulty in distinguishing protection from regime-change objectives.
  • Need to prioritise prevention, diplomacy and peaceful measures.

India's position

India has emphasised the importance of national sovereignty, peaceful means, Security Council authorisation and safeguards against misuse. It has also supported the responsibility of States to protect their populations and the need for international assistance.

24. UN80 Initiative and Institutional Reform

The UN80 Initiative was launched in March 2025 as the UN approached its eightieth anniversary. It seeks to make the organisation more efficient, coherent and effective in responding to contemporary challenges.

Three broad workstreams

  • Efficiency and improvement: Improve Secretariat operations, reduce duplication, strengthen administrative efficiency and make better use of resources.
  • Mandate implementation review: Improve how mandates are created, implemented, monitored and reviewed, including addressing duplication and reporting burdens.
  • Structural and programmatic realignment: Examine how UN entities and programmes can work more coherently across peace and security, development, humanitarian action and human rights.

Key institutional questions

  • How can overlapping mandates and fragmented delivery be reduced?
  • How can funding become more predictable and aligned with agreed priorities?
  • How can country-level coordination improve?
  • How can digital technology, data and shared services improve performance?
  • How can efficiency reforms preserve essential field-level services?

Developments in 2026

The UN80 process has moved into implementation and member-state review. General Assembly Resolution 80/251, adopted in March 2026, provides a framework for improving mandate creation, implementation and review. The initiative includes proposals and actions requiring different levels of authority: some can be undertaken by the Secretariat, while others require decisions by member States.

Critical assessment

  • Reform can improve efficiency and reduce administrative duplication.
  • Member-state ownership is essential because the UN is an intergovernmental organisation.
  • Cost reduction should not weaken humanitarian, development and human rights delivery.
  • Structural changes require consultation with affected States and stakeholders.
  • Reform should be evaluated through measurable outcomes, not organisational restructuring alone.

UN80 reform is distinct from Security Council reform. It does not by itself alter the composition or veto arrangements of the Security Council.

25. India's Approach to the United Nations

Multilateralism and strategic autonomy

India views the UN as an important forum for international cooperation but argues that multilateral institutions must become more representative, effective and responsive to the interests of developing countries.

Major priorities

  • Permanent membership and comprehensive reform of the Security Council.
  • Greater voice for developing countries in international decision-making.
  • Peaceful settlement of disputes and respect for international law.
  • Counter-terrorism cooperation and action against transnational crime.
  • Climate justice, sustainable development and equitable access to technology.
  • Reform of global financial and development institutions.
  • Protection of the interests of the Global South.

Peacekeeping contribution

India's long-standing contribution to UN peacekeeping demonstrates its role in international security. India has also called for greater consultation with troop-contributing countries in decisions concerning mandates, resources and operational priorities.

Development cooperation

India supports development partnerships, capacity building, training, humanitarian assistance and technology cooperation. Its development engagement is linked to the broader principles of South-South cooperation and national ownership.

Climate and sustainable development

India advocates climate equity, climate finance, technology transfer and the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities in the context of international climate negotiations.

26. Challenges Facing the United Nations

  • Representational deficit: The Security Council does not adequately reflect contemporary demographic and economic realities.
  • Veto paralysis: Major-power disagreements can prevent timely action during international crises.
  • Selective enforcement: Perceived double standards undermine institutional credibility.
  • Financial constraints: Delayed contributions and earmarked funding affect programme delivery.
  • Fragmentation: Overlapping mandates and agencies can create duplication and inefficiency.
  • Enforcement limitations: The organisation depends heavily on the cooperation of sovereign States.
  • New security threats: Cyberattacks, terrorism, pandemics, autonomous technologies and climate-related risks challenge existing institutional arrangements.
  • Humanitarian crises: Conflict, displacement and restrictions on humanitarian access create major operational difficulties.
  • Development financing gaps: Many countries lack resources to achieve agreed development goals.
  • Trust deficit: Some member States question whether the organisation reflects their interests and priorities.

27. Reform Agenda and Way Forward

  1. Reform the Security Council to improve representativeness, legitimacy and accountability.
  2. Strengthen transparency in the use of veto power and improve working methods.
  3. Ensure adequate and predictable financing for peacekeeping, humanitarian and development operations.
  4. Improve coordination among UN entities and reduce unnecessary duplication.
  5. Strengthen the role of the General Assembly and ECOSOC in global policy coordination.
  6. Improve consultation with troop-contributing countries and affected communities.
  7. Strengthen preventive diplomacy, mediation and peacebuilding.
  8. Improve accountability and effectiveness of human rights mechanisms.
  9. Strengthen global cooperation on cyber security, pandemics, climate change and emerging technologies.
  10. Promote national ownership and measurable results in development programmes.
  11. Ensure that UN reform is inclusive and led by member States.

28. Prelims Factual Reference Points

  • UN Charter: Signed on 26 June 1945; entered into force on 24 October 1945.
  • UN membership: 193 member States.
  • Principal organs: Six.
  • General Assembly: One State, one vote.
  • Security Council: 15 members; five permanent and ten elected.
  • ECOSOC: 54 members elected by the General Assembly.
  • ICJ: 15 judges; principal judicial organ of the UN.
  • Trusteeship Council: Suspended regular operations in 1994; not formally abolished.
  • UN Human Rights Council: 47 members.
  • Universal Periodic Review: Reviews the human rights record of every UN member State.
  • UDHR: Adopted in 1948; not itself a treaty.
  • SDGs: 17 goals and 169 targets, adopted in 2015.
  • IAEA: Established in 1957; safeguards and peaceful nuclear cooperation.
  • OPCW: Implements the Chemical Weapons Convention.
  • ICC: Independent court under the Rome Statute; not a principal UN organ.
  • ITLOS: Tribunal established under UNCLOS.
  • UN Peacekeeping: Based on consent, impartiality and restricted use of force under mission mandates.
  • Peacebuilding Commission: Intergovernmental advisory body established in 2005.
  • UN80 Initiative: Launched in 2025 to improve efficiency, mandate implementation and system-wide delivery.

29. Important Institutional Distinctions

Institutions or concepts Important distinction
General Assembly and Security Council Broad deliberative and representative organ versus organ with primary responsibility for international peace and security.
ICJ and ICC ICJ decides disputes between States and gives advisory opinions; ICC prosecutes individuals for specified international crimes.
ICJ and ITLOS Principal judicial organ of the UN versus specialised tribunal under UNCLOS.
Human Rights Council and treaty bodies Charter-based intergovernmental body versus expert committees monitoring specific treaties.
Specialised agency and UN programme Autonomous treaty-based organisation versus programme or fund established within the UN system.
Peacekeeping and peace enforcement Generally consent-based operation versus coercive action authorised under the Charter.
Peacekeeping and peacebuilding Mission-based support for peace and security versus longer-term efforts addressing causes of conflict.
UNHCR and UNRWA UN refugee agency with a global mandate versus agency specifically serving Palestine refugees within its mandate.
IAEA and OPCW Nuclear safeguards and peaceful nuclear cooperation versus chemical weapons prohibition and verification.
UN and ICC Intergovernmental organisation versus independent international criminal court.
UN80 and Security Council reform System-wide efficiency and mandate reform versus proposed changes to Security Council composition and working methods.

30. Mains Analytical Framework

How to evaluate the UN

  1. Historical context: Why was the UN established and what problems was it designed to address?
  2. Institutional structure: Do its organs represent contemporary global realities?
  3. Effectiveness: Can it prevent conflict, protect civilians and coordinate international cooperation?
  4. Legitimacy: Are its decisions representative, consistent and transparent?
  5. Implementation: Do member States provide the resources and political support required?
  6. India's interests: How does the institution affect India's strategic autonomy, development and global role?
  7. Reform: What changes can improve its effectiveness without undermining its founding principles?

Possible analytical themes

  • Relevance of the United Nations in a multipolar world.
  • Security Council reform and India's claim to permanent membership.
  • Veto power and the crisis of collective security.
  • Peacekeeping and the protection of civilians.
  • Human rights mechanisms and State sovereignty.
  • UN development system and the implementation of SDGs.
  • Reform of multilateral institutions and the UN80 Initiative.
  • India's role in strengthening multilateralism and representing the Global South.

Balanced conclusion

The United Nations remains indispensable as a forum for international cooperation, even though its institutional structure and enforcement capacity face serious limitations. Reform should make it more representative, accountable and effective while preserving sovereign equality, peaceful settlement and collective responsibility for global challenges.
