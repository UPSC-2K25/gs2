1. Framework for Understanding India's Major-Power Relations

India's relations with major powers are shaped by national security, economic development, technological capability, energy requirements, global governance and the objective of maintaining strategic autonomy. India's foreign policy is not based on permanent alignment with one power bloc; it seeks issue-based partnerships while retaining independent decision-making.

The end of the Cold War, China's rise, globalisation, technological competition and changes in the international distribution of power have expanded India's diplomatic choices and responsibilities.

Key determinants

  • Protection of sovereignty, territorial integrity and national security.
  • Access to capital, markets, technology and critical resources.
  • Defence modernisation and diversification of military partnerships.
  • Energy security and resilient supply chains.
  • Reform of international institutions and representation of developing countries.
  • Stability in the Indo-Pacific, Eurasia, West Asia and other strategic regions.

2. India-United States Relations

India-US relations have evolved from Cold War differences to a broad strategic partnership. Shared concerns about regional stability, maritime security, technology and resilient supply chains have expanded cooperation, while differences remain on trade, sanctions, strategic autonomy and aspects of global governance.

Historical evolution

  • During the Cold War, India's non-alignment and US alliance relationships created periodic divergence.
  • The 1991 economic reforms and the end of the Cold War opened new opportunities for engagement.
  • The 2005 civil nuclear initiative and subsequent cooperation transformed the relationship.
  • Defence, technology, trade and Indo-Pacific cooperation have become major pillars.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Defence: Military exercises, logistics cooperation, defence trade, maritime awareness and interoperability.
  • Technology: Cooperation in semiconductors, critical technologies, space, artificial intelligence and advanced manufacturing.
  • Indo-Pacific: Cooperation through the QUAD and other regional arrangements.
  • Energy: Trade and cooperation in hydrocarbons, civil nuclear energy and clean energy.
  • Education and diaspora: Academic links, research collaboration and extensive people-to-people ties.
  • Counter-terrorism: Intelligence cooperation and international efforts against terrorist networks.

Defence foundational agreements

  • LEMOA (2016): Provides a framework for reciprocal logistics support on a case-by-case basis.
  • COMCASA (2018): Facilitates access to specified secure communications equipment and systems.
  • BECA (2020): Provides for sharing specified geospatial information and products.

These agreements facilitate cooperation; they do not create an automatic mutual-defence obligation or a military alliance.

Challenges

  • Differences over trade, tariffs and market access.
  • US sanctions and export-control policies affecting India's strategic choices.
  • India's continued defence and energy relations with Russia.
  • Different approaches to strategic autonomy and regional crises.
  • Concerns over technology transfer, data governance and immigration policy.

Way forward: Expand practical cooperation while maintaining transparent dialogue on disagreements, diversifying technology partnerships and protecting India's independent foreign policy choices.

3. India-Russia Relations

India-Russia relations have a long history of political, defence and technological cooperation. Russia remains an important partner in India's defence ecosystem, nuclear energy, space cooperation and certain energy sectors.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Defence: Long-standing supply, maintenance and upgrade relationships, alongside selected joint production and technology projects.
  • Nuclear energy: Cooperation in civil nuclear power, including the Kudankulam project.
  • Space: Historical cooperation in space technology and human spaceflight-related capabilities.
  • Energy: Trade in crude oil and other energy resources, subject to market conditions and applicable restrictions.
  • Multilateral platforms: Engagement through BRICS, SCO and other international forums.
  • Eurasian connectivity: Interest in the International North-South Transport Corridor and related connectivity initiatives.

Challenges

  • Russia's growing strategic and economic engagement with China.
  • Sanctions and financial settlement constraints.
  • Disruptions in defence supply chains and delivery schedules.
  • India's need to diversify defence procurement and domestic production.
  • Changing geopolitical alignments following the war in Ukraine.

Way forward: Preserve mutually beneficial cooperation, improve defence indigenisation, diversify sources of critical technology and maintain dialogue with all relevant international partners.

4. India-European Union Relations

The European Union is a major economic and regulatory actor. India-EU relations are important for trade, investment, climate policy, technology, education, maritime security and global governance.

Areas of cooperation

  • Trade and investment, including negotiations and implementation of economic agreements.
  • Climate action, renewable energy and green technology.
  • Digital governance, data protection and emerging technology standards.
  • Research, higher education and skills cooperation.
  • Maritime security and Indo-Pacific engagement.
  • Connectivity, infrastructure and sustainable development.

Challenges

  • Differences over market access, tariffs and regulatory standards.
  • Carbon-related trade measures and climate responsibilities.
  • Data protection, privacy and digital regulation.
  • Human rights and governance-related differences in diplomatic discourse.
  • Different approaches to sanctions and international conflicts.

Way forward: Build a predictable trade and investment framework, deepen research and technology cooperation and use the partnership to support sustainable and diversified supply chains.

5. India-Japan Relations

India and Japan have developed a special strategic and global partnership based on economic cooperation, infrastructure development, maritime security and a shared interest in a stable Indo-Pacific.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Infrastructure: Japanese assistance and investment in transport, urban development and industrial corridors.
  • Connectivity: Cooperation in high-speed rail, logistics and regional connectivity.
  • Defence: Maritime exercises, security dialogue and defence technology cooperation.
  • Technology: Collaboration in semiconductors, manufacturing, digital systems and research.
  • QUAD: Cooperation with Australia and the United States on Indo-Pacific issues.
  • Human resources: Skills, education and mobility partnerships.

Challenges

  • Implementation delays in major infrastructure projects.
  • Differences in business practices and regulatory environments.
  • Regional security uncertainty and strategic competition.
  • Need to expand private-sector investment and supply-chain integration.

Way forward: Strengthen project implementation, industrial partnerships, skills mobility and maritime cooperation while deepening coordination on regional rules and standards.

6. India-France Relations

France is an important strategic partner in defence, aerospace, nuclear energy, space, maritime security and the Indo-Pacific. The relationship is characterised by a high degree of political trust and cooperation across multiple strategic sectors.

Major areas of cooperation

  • Defence platforms, aviation, submarines and defence industrial cooperation.
  • Civil nuclear energy and related technology.
  • Space research and satellite cooperation.
  • Maritime security and coordination in the Indian Ocean.
  • Climate action, renewable energy and the International Solar Alliance.
  • Education, research, innovation and cultural exchange.

Strategic importance

France's presence in the Indian Ocean through its overseas territories and military facilities gives the partnership a distinct maritime dimension. Both countries have an interest in maritime domain awareness, freedom of navigation and regional stability.

Way forward: Expand co-development and co-production, improve technology collaboration and deepen cooperation on ocean governance and emerging security challenges.

7. India-United Kingdom Relations

India and the United Kingdom share historical, educational, linguistic, commercial and people-to-people connections. The partnership has expanded into trade, technology, defence, climate and education.

Areas of cooperation

  • Trade, investment and financial services.
  • Education, research and professional mobility.
  • Defence, maritime security and counter-terrorism.
  • Climate action, clean energy and sustainable finance.
  • Science, technology and digital innovation.
  • Cooperation through the Commonwealth and other multilateral forums.

Challenges

  • Market access and regulatory differences.
  • Immigration, visa and mobility concerns.
  • Security concerns involving extremist networks and violent activities.
  • Differences over historical narratives and political issues.

Way forward: Focus on predictable trade arrangements, educational links, technology partnerships and practical security cooperation.

8. India-Germany Relations

Germany is a major European economic and technological partner. Its strengths in advanced manufacturing, engineering, renewable energy and vocational training complement India's development priorities.

  • Manufacturing, industrial technology and investment.
  • Renewable energy, green hydrogen and climate cooperation.
  • Vocational training, skills development and higher education.
  • Research, innovation and digital technologies.
  • Cooperation on multilateral reform and sustainable development.

India can use the partnership to strengthen manufacturing quality, industrial skills, energy transition and access to advanced technology.

9. India and Other Major Powers

Australia

India-Australia relations have expanded in education, trade, critical minerals, defence, maritime cooperation and the QUAD. Cooperation is relevant to Indo-Pacific stability and supply-chain resilience.

Canada

India-Canada relations have included trade, education, investment, energy and diaspora links. Security allegations and diplomatic disputes have periodically strained the relationship. Stable ties require credible legal processes, mutual respect and effective communication.

Brazil

India and Brazil cooperate through BRICS, IBSA, G20 and other platforms. Both support stronger representation of developing countries in global governance and have interests in agriculture, energy, technology and South-South cooperation.

South Africa

India-South Africa relations are rooted in anti-apartheid solidarity and cooperation in BRICS, IBSA and multilateral forums. Areas include trade, development, health, education and reform of international institutions.

10. Strategic Autonomy and Multi-alignment

Strategic autonomy refers to India's ability to make independent foreign policy and security decisions according to its national interests. It is not equivalent to isolation or refusal to cooperate.

Multi-alignment describes India's simultaneous engagement with different countries and groupings across issue areas. India may cooperate with one partner on defence technology, another on energy, and a different group on development or climate issues.

Benefits

  • Preserves flexibility in a changing international order.
  • Reduces excessive dependence on any single partner.
  • Expands access to markets, technology and capital.
  • Improves India's bargaining position in global negotiations.

Risks

  • Managing competing expectations among partners.
  • Exposure to sanctions, export controls and geopolitical pressure.
  • Need for greater diplomatic and institutional capacity.
  • Possibility of policy ambiguity if priorities are not clearly communicated.

11. Comparative Overview

  • United States: Defence, advanced technology, Indo-Pacific and economic cooperation.
  • Russia: Defence legacy, nuclear energy, space and Eurasian engagement.
  • European Union: Trade, investment, regulation, climate and technology.
  • Japan: Infrastructure, manufacturing, technology and Indo-Pacific security.
  • France: Defence, nuclear, space and Indian Ocean cooperation.
  • United Kingdom: Education, finance, trade, technology and diaspora ties.
  • Germany: Industrial technology, manufacturing, skills and green transition.
  • Australia: Critical minerals, maritime security, education and regional cooperation.

12. Way Forward

  1. Maintain strategic autonomy while building reliable and issue-based partnerships.
  2. Diversify defence procurement and strengthen indigenous research and manufacturing.
  3. Prioritise technology partnerships in semiconductors, artificial intelligence, space and clean energy.
  4. Improve market access and investment conditions to deepen economic ties.
  5. Build resilient supply chains for critical minerals, pharmaceuticals and strategic components.
  6. Coordinate with partners on maritime security, cyber security and counter-terrorism.
  7. Strengthen India's role in global governance and representation of developing countries.
  8. Invest in diplomatic expertise, economic analysis and institutional capacity.
