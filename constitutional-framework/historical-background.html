The Indian Constitution emerged from a long process of institutional change, colonial legislation, political struggle and constitutional negotiation. Understanding this history explains both the institutional inheritance and the deliberate departures embodied in the Constitution of 1950.

1. Understanding the Historical Context

The constitutional development of British India was not a continuous or uniform transfer of power to Indians. It was primarily shaped by the changing needs of colonial administration, commercial interests, political pressures in Britain and the growth of Indian nationalism.

Early legislation concentrated authority in the East India Company and later in the British Crown. Subsequent reforms introduced legislative councils, limited representation, elections, provincial institutions and responsible government in restricted forms. Indian political movements increasingly challenged the limited nature of these arrangements and demanded self-government.

Central idea: Colonial constitutional reforms introduced several institutions and administrative arrangements later adapted by independent India. However, the democratic legitimacy, popular sovereignty and rights-based character of the Indian Constitution came from the freedom struggle and the Constituent Assembly's deliberations, not simply from the transfer of colonial institutions.

For study purposes, the period can be understood through four broad developments:

  • Parliamentary control over the East India Company's administration.
  • Centralisation of colonial authority under the Crown.
  • Limited expansion of legislative representation and provincial government.
  • Constitutional negotiations leading to independence and constitution-making.

2. Parliamentary Regulation of Company Rule

Regulating Act, 1773

The Regulating Act was the first major step by the British Parliament to regulate the political and administrative affairs of the East India Company in India. It responded to administrative abuses, financial difficulties and the growing political responsibilities of the Company.

  • The Governor of Bengal was designated the Governor-General of Bengal, assisted by a council of four.
  • Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal under this arrangement.
  • The Presidencies of Bombay and Madras were subordinated to Bengal in specified matters, particularly war and peace.
  • A Supreme Court was established at Calcutta in 1774, consisting of a Chief Justice and three other judges.
  • Company servants were restricted from accepting presents and engaging in certain private financial transactions.

Institutional significance: The Act marked the beginning of parliamentary supervision over the Company's territorial administration. It also exposed the difficulties of defining the relationship between the executive government and the judiciary.

Pitt's India Act, 1784

Pitt's India Act sought to strengthen British governmental supervision over the Company's political affairs. It created a system commonly described as dual control.

  • A Board of Control was established to supervise the Company's civil, military and revenue affairs.
  • The Court of Directors continued to manage the Company's commercial affairs and certain administrative functions.
  • The distinction between commercial management and political control became more clearly institutionalised.

Institutional significance: Political authority increasingly rested with the British government, even though the Company continued to function as the formal administrative instrument.

Charter Acts of 1793, 1813 and 1833

The Charter Acts periodically renewed the Company's authority while progressively changing its commercial and administrative role.

  • Charter Act, 1793: Renewed the Company's charter and continued the existing framework of Company administration.
  • Charter Act, 1813: Ended the Company's trade monopoly in India, while retaining its monopoly over trade with China and the tea trade. It also permitted missionary activity under the statutory framework.
  • Charter Act, 1833: Ended the Company's remaining commercial activities and transformed it into an administrative agency. The Governor-General of Bengal became the Governor-General of India, with Lord William Bentinck as the first to hold that designation.

The Charter Act of 1833 centralised legislative authority. It provided for a law member in the Governor-General's Council and strengthened the process of legal codification. The first Law Commission was established in 1834 under the chairmanship of Thomas Babington Macaulay.

Charter Act, 1853

The Charter Act of 1853 made further changes to the legislative machinery.

  • Legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council were differentiated through an expanded legislative council.
  • Legislative participation was widened by adding members representing different provincial areas.
  • The Act provided for open competition as the basis for recruitment to the civil services, subject to subsequent implementation arrangements.
  • The Company's charter was not renewed for a fixed twenty-year period, allowing Parliament greater flexibility over its future administration.

Institutional significance: The 1853 Act advanced legislative differentiation and administrative recruitment reforms, but did not introduce responsible government or popular control.

3. Crown Rule and the Growth of Legislative Councils

Government of India Act, 1858

The Revolt of 1857 led to a fundamental restructuring of colonial administration. The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred the Company's governing authority to the British Crown.

  • The rule of the East India Company came to an end.
  • The office of Secretary of State for India was created in the British Cabinet.
  • The Secretary of State was assisted by a Council of India.
  • The Governor-General continued as the principal executive authority in India and was also known as the Viceroy, representing the Crown.
  • Lord Canning became the first Viceroy under Crown rule.

The Queen's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 announced the transfer of government to the Crown and made assurances concerning religious non-interference, equal treatment under the law and the protection of treaties with Indian princes.

Institutional significance: The change was a transfer of authority from Company rule to Crown rule, not a transfer of sovereignty to the Indian people. The colonial executive remained largely unaccountable to representative Indian institutions.

Indian Councils Act, 1861

The Indian Councils Act, 1861 restored legislative powers to the Presidencies of Bombay and Madras in specified areas and introduced limited Indian participation through nomination.

  • The legislative council of the Governor-General was expanded for legislative purposes.
  • Indians could be nominated to the legislative councils.
  • Legislative powers were restored to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies, beginning a limited process of legislative decentralisation.
  • The portfolio system, under which members of the executive council handled designated departments, received statutory recognition.
  • The Governor-General was given ordinance-making powers for emergencies, subject to statutory limitations.

Indian participation remained nominated and restricted. The councils did not exercise effective control over the executive.

Indian Councils Act, 1892

The Act of 1892 enlarged the legislative councils and modestly expanded their deliberative functions.

  • The number of additional members in central and provincial legislative councils was increased.
  • Members were permitted to discuss the annual financial statement, subject to restrictions.
  • Members could ask questions on matters of public interest under prescribed conditions.
  • Certain representative bodies could recommend persons for nomination to legislative councils.

Although the Act did not expressly establish a system of direct elections, the nomination process incorporated recommendations from representative institutions. It is therefore associated with the gradual emergence of the electoral principle in colonial legislation.

Institutional significance: Legislative councils became somewhat more deliberative, but their powers remained limited. They could not make the executive politically responsible to elected representatives.

4. Limited Representation and Communal Electorates

Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley-Minto Reforms)

The Indian Councils Act, 1909 was introduced in the context of growing nationalist demands and political developments in the early twentieth century. It expanded legislative councils and introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

  • The size of the central and provincial legislative councils was increased.
  • The electoral element in legislative councils was expanded.
  • Separate electorates were introduced for Muslim voters, enabling them to elect Muslim representatives through separate electoral constituencies.
  • Members received greater opportunities to discuss budgets, move resolutions and ask supplementary questions, subject to procedural restrictions.
  • Satyendra Prasanna Sinha became the first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council.

Institutional significance: The reforms widened representation but institutionalised communal political representation through separate electorates. They did not establish parliamentary responsibility at the central level or give legislative councils control over the executive.

Important distinction: The 1892 reforms are associated with the indirect emergence of electoral representation through recommendations for nominations. The 1909 reforms formally introduced separate electorates for Muslims.

5. Dyarchy and the Expansion of Provincial Institutions

Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms)

The Government of India Act, 1919 followed the declaration of 20 August 1917, which stated the British government's policy of the increasing association of Indians in every branch of administration and the gradual development of self-governing institutions.

The Act introduced dyarchy in the provinces and expanded legislative representation at the central level.

Dyarchy in the provinces

Provincial subjects were divided into two categories:

  • Reserved subjects: Administered by the Governor and his executive councillors, who were not responsible to the provincial legislature. These included important areas such as finance, land revenue, police and justice.
  • Transferred subjects: Administered by ministers chosen from among the elected members of the provincial legislative council. These ministers were expected to work with the legislature. Subjects included areas such as education, public health, local self-government and agriculture.

The arrangement attempted to introduce a limited form of responsible government while retaining control over key administrative areas in the hands of the colonial executive.

Other major provisions

  • A bicameral central legislature was established, consisting of the Council of State and the Legislative Assembly.
  • Direct elections were introduced for legislative representation, although the franchise remained restricted.
  • Separate electorates and communal representation were extended to additional communities and interests.
  • The legislative powers of provinces were expanded, though the central government retained overriding authority.
  • The Act provided for the establishment of a Public Service Commission.
  • The office of the High Commissioner for India was created in London.

Institutional significance: The Act introduced a formal division of provincial subjects and a limited ministerial system. However, the Governor retained substantial powers, the franchise excluded most Indians, and the central executive was not responsible to the legislature.

The dyarchical arrangement was widely criticised because ministers were responsible for transferred subjects without control over several essential administrative and financial resources.

6. Provincial Autonomy and the Federal Scheme

Government of India Act, 1935

The Government of India Act, 1935 was the most extensive constitutional statute enacted by the British Parliament for governing British India. It reflected recommendations and negotiations associated with the Simon Commission, Round Table Conferences and subsequent constitutional deliberations.

Proposed All-India Federation

The Act proposed an All-India Federation comprising British Indian provinces and princely states. The participation of princely states depended on their accession. The federation did not come into operation because the required accession conditions were not fulfilled.

Provincial autonomy

  • Dyarchy in the provinces was abolished.
  • Provincial autonomy was introduced, and provincial ministers became responsible to elected provincial legislatures.
  • Provincial governors retained special responsibilities and discretionary powers that limited the extent of responsible government.
  • Provincial autonomy came into operation in 1937.

Dyarchy at the Centre

The Act proposed dyarchy at the federal level, dividing federal subjects between reserved and transferred categories. The proposed federal government did not come into operation, so this central dyarchical arrangement was not implemented.

Distribution of legislative powers

The Act divided legislative subjects into three lists:

  • Federal List: Subjects assigned to the federal legislature.
  • Provincial List: Subjects assigned to provincial legislatures.
  • Concurrent List: Subjects on which both federal and provincial legislatures could legislate, subject to the statutory framework.

Residuary legislative powers were vested in the Governor-General. The Act also provided for a Federal Court, which was established in 1937.

Other provisions

  • Bicameral legislatures were provided for in certain provinces.
  • The franchise was expanded, but remained limited by property, tax, educational and other qualifications.
  • The Reserve Bank of India had already been established under separate legislation, the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934; the 1935 constitutional statute was not its establishing law.
  • The Act provided for the separation of Burma from British India, which took effect in 1937.
  • Federal, provincial and joint public service commissions were provided for within the statutory framework.

Institutional significance: The Act supplied an important institutional framework for provincial government and legislative distribution of powers. Its three-list arrangement, provincial institutions and federal ideas influenced later constitutional design.

However, the Act did not establish full responsible government at the Centre. Governors and the Governor-General retained extensive special powers, and the British Parliament remained sovereign over the colonial constitutional system.

7. Independence and the End of British Sovereignty

Indian Independence Act, 1947

The Indian Independence Act, 1947 implemented the transfer of power following the Mountbatten Plan of 3 June 1947. It provided for the creation of two independent Dominions: India and Pakistan.

  • British India was partitioned into the Dominions of India and Pakistan with effect from 15 August 1947.
  • The authority of the British Parliament to legislate for the new Dominions was brought to an end, subject to the statutory arrangements for the transition.
  • The office of the Secretary of State for India was abolished.
  • The suzerainty of the British Crown over princely states lapsed, along with the relevant treaty relationships.
  • Each Dominion's Constituent Assembly was empowered to frame its constitution and exercise legislative powers until the new constitution came into operation.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 continued as the provisional constitutional framework, with adaptations, until the Constitution of India commenced.

The Constituent Assembly of India therefore performed two important functions: it acted as the constitution-making body and, after Independence, also exercised legislative authority for the Dominion of India.

Institutional significance: Independence ended British constitutional supremacy and enabled the Indian people, through their Constituent Assembly, to frame a sovereign constitutional order.

9. Constitutional Legacy: Continuity and Change

Several institutional arrangements developed during colonial rule influenced the Constitution of independent India. Their adoption, however, was selective and accompanied by important constitutional changes.

Colonial institutional development Connection with the constitutional system of India
Legislative councils and central legislation Development of representative legislative institutions, transformed into legislatures deriving authority from the Constitution.
Provincial legislative and administrative arrangements Influenced the development of State institutions within a constitutionally defined federal system.
Three legislative lists under the 1935 Act The Union, State and Concurrent Lists became part of the Seventh Schedule, with a different constitutional allocation of residuary powers.
Federal Court under the 1935 Act Provided an institutional predecessor to the Supreme Court, though the latter derives its authority from the Constitution.
Public Service Commission framework Constitutionalised through provisions for the Union and State Public Service Commissions.
Emergency and special executive powers Some administrative ideas informed later constitutional provisions, but were placed within a written Constitution with defined conditions and safeguards.

Important constitutional departures

  • Popular sovereignty: The Constitution derives its authority from the people of India rather than the British Parliament or Crown.
  • Universal adult franchise: Political participation is not restricted by colonial property, tax or educational qualifications.
  • Fundamental Rights: Individual rights and constitutional remedies are constitutionally protected.
  • Responsible government: The Union and State parliamentary systems operate under constitutional rules of legislative responsibility.
  • Constitutional supremacy: Public institutions exercise powers within constitutional limits, subject to judicial review.
  • Federal distribution of powers: Legislative and executive authority is distributed constitutionally, rather than being dependent on the discretionary authority of a colonial executive.
Analytical perspective: The Indian Constitution did not reject every institution developed under colonial rule. It retained or adapted administrative and institutional experience while changing the source of authority, the basis of representation, the relationship between institutions and the legal protection of individual liberty.

10. How to Interpret the Historical Evolution

Historical constitutional development should be studied through institutional questions rather than memorising legislation alone.

  • Where did authority lie? Examine the changing relationship between the East India Company, the British Parliament, the Crown, the Governor-General and Indian representative institutions.
  • Who could participate? Distinguish nomination, indirect representation, restricted elections and universal adult franchise.
  • Who controlled the executive? Examine whether legislative councils could question, influence or hold the executive responsible.
  • How were powers distributed? Trace the movement from centralised colonial administration to provincial arrangements and the later federal structure.
  • What changed in 1947 and 1950? Distinguish the transfer of political power in 1947 from the commencement of the sovereign constitutional order in 1950.

This approach connects the historical background with later constitutional topics such as federalism, parliamentary government, judicial institutions, public services, fundamental rights and emergency provisions.
