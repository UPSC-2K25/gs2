The Constitution of India was framed by a Constituent Assembly between December 1946 and November 1949. Its work was not merely the drafting of a legal document: it involved defining the political identity of independent India, reconciling competing interests, establishing democratic institutions and creating constitutional safeguards for a diverse society.

1. The Demand for a Constituent Assembly

The idea that Indians should frame their own Constitution developed alongside the national movement's demand for self-government. The central principle was that constitutional authority should arise from the people of India rather than from a constitution imposed by the colonial government.

Evolution of the demand

  • 1934: M.N. Roy is credited with making an early formal proposal for a Constituent Assembly.
  • 1935: The Indian National Congress formally adopted the demand that a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise should frame India's Constitution.
  • 1940 – August Offer: The British government accepted, in principle, that Indians would have a role in framing the future constitutional framework.
  • 1942 – Cripps Proposals: The proposals envisaged a constitution-making body after the Second World War, but were rejected by major Indian political forces.
  • 1946 – Cabinet Mission Plan: The plan provided the framework for establishing the Constituent Assembly that actually undertook constitution-making.

The Cabinet Mission Plan was therefore the immediate constitutional basis for the formation of the Constituent Assembly. The Assembly was not directly elected by all adult citizens, but it was created through an agreed political and constitutional process during the final phase of British rule.

2. Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946

The Cabinet Mission, comprising Lord Pethick-Lawrence, Sir Stafford Cripps and A.V. Alexander, arrived in India in 1946 to negotiate the transfer of power and the constitutional future of the country.

Its plan of 16 May 1946 provided for a Constituent Assembly to frame the Constitution of a united India. It proposed a federal arrangement with a relatively limited Union authority and grouped provinces into sections.

Constitutional arrangements for the Assembly

  • The proposed Assembly would have a total strength of 389 members.
  • 292 seats were allocated to British Indian provinces represented through their legislative assemblies.
  • Four seats were allocated to the Chief Commissioners' Provinces.
  • Ninety-three seats were allocated to the princely states.
  • Provincial representatives were to be elected indirectly by members of the provincial legislative assemblies.
  • Representatives of princely states were to be nominated by their respective rulers.

The allocation of seats broadly reflected population. Within provincial legislatures, representatives were chosen through proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote, with seats allocated among major communities under the Cabinet Mission scheme.

Limitations of the arrangement

The Assembly was not elected through universal adult franchise. The provincial legislative assemblies themselves had been elected under a restricted colonial franchise. Princely-state representatives were nominated rather than directly elected.

These limitations are important when assessing the democratic character of the Assembly. Its legitimacy did not arise from a direct nationwide popular election. It developed through its representative composition, public deliberations, the freedom movement's political mandate and the eventual adoption of the Constitution in the name of the people.

Impact of Partition

The partition of British India in 1947 fundamentally changed the Assembly's composition. Members representing territories that became part of Pakistan ceased to be members of the Indian Constituent Assembly.

Its membership was consequently reduced from 389 to 299: 229 representatives from the provinces and 70 representatives from the princely states.

The Assembly also acquired a new constitutional position after Independence. It no longer functioned under the authority of the Cabinet Mission Plan or the British constitutional framework. It became a sovereign body exercising constitution-making authority for independent India.

Representation and participation

The Assembly included lawyers, administrators, political leaders, social reformers, academics and representatives of different communities and regions. Women members participated in the debates and contributed to discussions on rights, equality, social reform and the structure of the State.

There were 15 women members in the Assembly. Their participation was significant in a period when women's political representation remained limited in many parts of the world.

The Assembly was not socially or politically identical to the entire population. Its composition and the restricted franchise are legitimate subjects of historical evaluation. At the same time, its proceedings provide evidence of sustained deliberation, disagreement and constitutional compromise.

4. First Meeting and Leadership

The Constituent Assembly met for the first time on 9 December 1946 in Constitution Hall, now known as the Central Hall of Parliament House, New Delhi.

  • Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha presided as the temporary Chairman at the first sitting.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected President of the Constituent Assembly on 11 December 1946.
  • H.C. Mookherjee served as Vice-President.
  • B.N. Rau served as the Constitutional Adviser and prepared important preliminary constitutional material for the Assembly and its committees.

The President guided the proceedings, maintained order and presided over deliberations. The Constitutional Adviser provided technical and comparative constitutional assistance. These roles were distinct from the work of the Drafting Committee, which was constituted later.

5. Objectives Resolution

On 13 December 1946, Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution before the Constituent Assembly. It was adopted on 22 January 1947.

The Resolution articulated the fundamental principles that were to guide constitution-making. It was an important political and philosophical statement about the nature of the future Indian State.

Major principles

  • India was to be an independent and sovereign republic.
  • The constituent units were to form a Union with defined constitutional powers.
  • All authority and sovereignty of independent India would derive from the people.
  • Justice, social, economic and political, was to be secured.
  • Equality of status and opportunity was to be guaranteed.
  • Freedom of thought, expression, belief, faith, worship, vocation, association and action was to be protected, subject to law and public morality.
  • Adequate safeguards were to be provided for minorities, backward and tribal areas and other disadvantaged groups.
  • India was to contribute to the promotion of world peace and the welfare of humanity.

Relationship with the Preamble

The Objectives Resolution became the philosophical foundation for the Preamble. The Preamble did not simply reproduce the Resolution word for word; it expressed the constitutional objectives in their final form after deliberations by the Assembly.

The relationship is important because it connects the historical process of constitution-making with the interpretative role of the Preamble in understanding constitutional values.

Constitutional connection: The Objectives Resolution expressed the political and philosophical commitments of the Assembly. The Preamble gives the Constitution's final expression to its foundational ideals and declares the source of constitutional authority.

6. Committees of the Constituent Assembly

The Assembly distributed its work among specialised committees. This enabled detailed examination of institutional design, rights, federal arrangements, minority safeguards and administrative provisions. Committee reports formed important inputs into the larger constitution-making process.

Why the committee system mattered

  • It enabled specialised examination of complex constitutional questions before they reached the full Assembly.
  • It provided a structured way to reconcile competing political, regional and social interests.
  • It connected broad constitutional principles with detailed institutional and legal provisions.
  • It allowed the Assembly to consider committee reports and debate their recommendations rather than relying on a single drafting authority.

The committee system should not be confused with the Drafting Committee alone. The Drafting Committee was important, but it worked within a larger process involving the Constitutional Adviser, several specialised committees, the Assembly's debates and the consideration of amendments.

7. Drafting Committee and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar

The Constituent Assembly established the Drafting Committee on 29 August 1947. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar was appointed its Chairman.

Original members

  1. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar – Chairman
  2. N. Gopalaswami Ayyangar
  3. Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar
  4. K.M. Munshi
  5. Syed Mohammad Saadulla
  6. B.L. Mitter
  7. D.P. Khaitan

Changes occurred in the Committee's membership during its work. B.L. Mitter resigned and was replaced by N. Madhava Rau. Following the death of D.P. Khaitan, T.T. Krishnamachari joined the Committee.

Role of the Drafting Committee

  • It examined the reports and recommendations of the Assembly's committees.
  • It considered the Constitutional Adviser's draft and other source material.
  • It converted broad principles and committee decisions into a structured legal text.
  • It considered the consistency, arrangement and legal expression of constitutional provisions.
  • It revised the draft in response to deliberations and amendments moved in the Assembly.

Dr. Ambedkar's leadership was central to presenting, explaining and defending the Draft Constitution. However, the Constitution was not the work of one individual or committee. Its final form emerged from collective deliberation by the Constituent Assembly and the work of multiple committees and advisers.

Constitutional Adviser and Chief Draftsman

B.N. Rau, as Constitutional Adviser, prepared a preliminary draft based on committee reports and comparative constitutional study. The Drafting Committee then examined and revised the material.

S.N. Mukherjee, the Chief Draftsman, played an important technical role in translating constitutional decisions into precise legal language and arranging the provisions in a coherent draft.

These distinct roles help explain why the making of the Constitution should be understood as a multi-stage institutional process.

8. Preparation, Publication and Debate of the Draft

Preliminary draft and public comments

The Constitutional Adviser prepared an initial draft for consideration by the Drafting Committee. After the Committee's work, a Draft Constitution was published in February 1948 to invite comments and suggestions.

The draft was considered in the light of comments received from individuals, institutions and public bodies. The Drafting Committee revised the text before it was formally introduced in the Assembly.

Introduction and stages of consideration

Dr. Ambedkar introduced the Draft Constitution in the Constituent Assembly on 4 November 1948. The Assembly then undertook detailed consideration through successive stages.

  • First reading: General discussion on the principles and broad structure of the Draft Constitution.
  • Second reading: Clause-by-clause consideration of the draft, with detailed debate and the moving and consideration of amendments.
  • Third reading: Final consideration of the Constitution as a whole before adoption.

Extent of deliberation

  • Approximately 7,635 amendments were tabled.
  • Approximately 2,473 amendments were actually moved and considered in the Assembly.
  • The Assembly held 11 sessions over 165 days.
  • 114 days were devoted to consideration of the Draft Constitution.

The figures demonstrate the extent of scrutiny and debate. They also show that the final text was not simply accepted as a document prepared by a drafting group; it underwent extensive consideration by the full Assembly.

9. Important Decisions During Constitution-Making

The Constituent Assembly dealt with several major questions that shaped the political and institutional character of independent India.

National Flag

On 22 July 1947, the Constituent Assembly adopted the national flag in its present basic form: a horizontal tricolour of saffron, white and green, with the navy-blue Ashoka Chakra at the centre.

Language provisions

The Assembly debated the official language question extensively. On 14 September 1949, it adopted the constitutional arrangement under which Hindi in the Devanagari script would be the official language of the Union, with provisions for the continued use of English.

The Constitution did not declare any language as the national language of India. The distinction between an official language and a national language is important.

Universal adult franchise

The Assembly chose universal adult franchise as the basis of democratic elections. This was a significant departure from the restricted franchise that had characterised colonial legislative arrangements.

The decision reflected the commitment to political equality and the principle that citizenship, rather than property, education or social status, should form the basis of electoral participation.

Parliamentary government

The Assembly adopted a parliamentary system in which the executive is politically responsible to the elected legislature. The choice reflected institutional experience, political preferences and the need for accountable government in a diverse and newly independent country.

Federal structure with a strong Union

The Assembly adopted a federal distribution of powers while giving the Union substantial authority. The design reflected concerns about national unity, security, economic integration and the administrative challenges of the period.

Rights and social transformation

The Assembly considered the relationship between individual liberty, equality, social reform and the State's responsibility to address historical disadvantage. These debates contributed to the structure of Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy and provisions for protective and affirmative measures.

10. Adoption, Signing and Commencement

Adoption on 26 November 1949

The Constituent Assembly adopted the Constitution on 26 November 1949. The date is recorded in the concluding portion of the Preamble.

The adoption of the Constitution marked the completion of the Assembly's principal constitution-making task. Certain provisions came into force immediately, while the remaining provisions were brought into effect on 26 January 1950.

Signing on 24 January 1950

On 24 January 1950, members of the Constituent Assembly signed the Constitution. A total of 284 members actually signed it.

The Assembly also elected Dr. Rajendra Prasad as the first President of India under the new constitutional arrangement.

Commencement on 26 January 1950

The Constitution came fully into force on 26 January 1950. India became a sovereign democratic republic governed by its own Constitution.

The date was chosen to commemorate the declaration of Purna Swaraj, or complete independence, adopted by the Indian National Congress in 1929 and observed on 26 January 1930.

Why are the three dates different?

Date Event Constitutional significance
26 November 1949 Constitution adopted Constituent Assembly approved the final constitutional text.
24 January 1950 Constitution signed Members authenticated the Constitution; Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected first President.
26 January 1950 Constitution fully commenced India began functioning as a republic under the new constitutional framework.

The distinction between adoption and commencement is essential. The Constitution was adopted in November 1949, but its full constitutional framework came into operation on Republic Day in January 1950.

The Constituent Assembly continued as the Provisional Parliament after the Constitution commenced, until the first Parliament elected under the new constitutional framework was constituted in 1952.

11. Time, Cost and the Final Constitution

The Constituent Assembly took two years, eleven months and seventeen days from its first meeting to the adoption of the Constitution.

  • First meeting: 9 December 1946.
  • Adoption: 26 November 1949.
  • Sessions: 11.
  • Total sitting days: 165.
  • Days spent considering the Draft Constitution: 114.
  • Members who signed: 284.
  • Original Constitution: 395 Articles, 22 Parts and 8 Schedules.

The Constitution was handwritten in English and Hindi. The calligraphic versions were prepared by Prem Behari Narain Raizada, while the original manuscripts were decorated by artists associated with the Shantiniketan tradition under the direction of Nandalal Bose.

The original constitutional text was therefore not simply a printed legislative document. It was also a carefully prepared historical record of the founding constitutional settlement.

12. Critical Evaluation of the Constituent Assembly

Arguments supporting its legitimacy

  • It included representatives from different provinces, communities, political traditions and professional backgrounds.
  • Its proceedings involved extensive debate and consideration of amendments.
  • It adopted a constitutional framework that established universal adult franchise and parliamentary democracy.
  • It sought to reconcile national unity with regional diversity and individual rights with social transformation.
  • Its authority was ultimately expressed through the Constitution's adoption in the name of the people of India.

Limitations and criticisms

  • The Assembly was not elected through universal adult franchise.
  • The restricted colonial franchise shaped the composition of the provincial legislative assemblies that elected its members.
  • Representatives of princely states were nominated rather than directly elected.
  • The Assembly's membership did not proportionately reflect every social, economic and political section of Indian society.
  • The political circumstances of Partition and the absence of some major political groups affected the nature of deliberation.

Balanced assessment

The Constituent Assembly should neither be treated as a perfectly representative body nor dismissed as lacking democratic legitimacy simply because it was not directly elected by universal adult franchise.

Its legitimacy can be examined through several dimensions: the political mandate of the freedom movement, the representative character of its membership, the openness and depth of its debates, the consideration of amendments and the constitutional commitment to popular sovereignty.

The decision to introduce universal adult franchise in a country with widespread poverty, illiteracy and social inequality was itself a major democratic commitment. It extended political equality beyond the limited representative institutions inherited from colonial rule.

13. Constitutional Significance and Institutional Connections

The process of constitution-making explains several foundational features of the present constitutional system.

Popular sovereignty

The Preamble begins with the words "We, the People of India". This expresses the principle that constitutional authority originates from the people. It is distinct from the colonial system in which legislative authority was ultimately derived from the British Parliament.

Constitutional supremacy

The Constitution establishes the legal framework within which the legislature, executive and judiciary exercise their powers. No institution is above the Constitution.

Federalism

The debates on Union and provincial powers shaped the distribution of legislative and executive authority. The resulting constitutional structure combines a federal division of powers with provisions intended to preserve national unity.

Rights and social justice

The discussions on Fundamental Rights, minority safeguards and social reform influenced the Constitution's commitment to liberty, equality and dignity. Directive Principles complement these commitments by directing the State towards social and economic transformation.

Parliamentary democracy

The adoption of responsible parliamentary government established the basis for executive accountability to elected legislatures at the Union and State levels.

Constitutional amendment and continuity

The Constitution provides a formal amendment procedure. This reflects the Assembly's effort to create a framework capable of adapting to changing circumstances while preserving constitutional continuity and institutional stability.

15. Framework for Understanding the Making of the Constitution

The constitution-making process can be understood through five connected questions:

  1. Source of authority: How did the demand for a Constituent Assembly emerge, and how did the Assembly acquire constitution-making authority?
  2. Representation: Who participated in the Assembly, how were members selected, and what were the limitations of its composition?
  3. Deliberation: How did committees, advisers and Assembly debates shape the draft?
  4. Constitutional choices: Why were parliamentary government, federalism, universal adult franchise, Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles adopted?
  5. Constitutional legitimacy: How did the adoption of the Constitution in the name of the people establish a new source of political and legal authority?

These questions connect the historical making of the Constitution with the study of its Preamble, salient features, rights, federal structure, parliamentary system and amendment procedure.
