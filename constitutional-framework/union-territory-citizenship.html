The Constitution defines India as a Union of States, provides Parliament with powers to reorganise the territory of the country, and establishes the constitutional framework for citizenship. These subjects determine the territorial organisation of the Indian Union, the relationship between the Union and its constituent units, and membership of the Indian political community.

1. India as a Union of States

Article 1 declares that "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States". It also provides that the territory of India shall comprise:

  1. The territories of the States.
  2. The Union Territories specified in the First Schedule.
  3. Such other territories as may be acquired by India.

Why the expression "Union of States"?

The Constituent Assembly adopted the expression "Union" to indicate that the Indian federation was not the result of an agreement among sovereign States. The States do not possess a constitutional right to secede from India.

The expression also reflects the constitutional capacity of Parliament to reorganise the territorial units of the country under Articles 2, 3 and 4, subject to the constitutional procedure.

Territory of India and Union of India

The expressions "Union of India" and "territory of India" are not interchangeable in every constitutional context.

  • Union of India: Generally refers to the federal political entity comprising the States.
  • Territory of India: Has a wider meaning under Article 1 and includes States, Union Territories and territories that may be acquired.

The distinction is important because Union Territories form part of the territory of India but do not have the same constitutional status as States.

2. Constitutional Framework for Territorial Organisation

Part I of the Constitution, comprising Articles 1 to 4, deals with the Union and its territory.

Article Subject Constitutional significance
Article 1 Name and territory of the Union Defines India and its territorial composition.
Article 2 Admission or establishment of new States Enables Parliament to admit or establish States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit.
Article 3 Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States Provides the principal procedure for internal territorial reorganisation.
Article 4 Supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions Allows related changes to the First and Fourth Schedules through laws under Articles 2 and 3.

Article 2: Admission or establishment of new States

Parliament may by law admit into the Union, or establish, new States on such terms and conditions as it thinks fit. This provision concerns the admission or establishment of States and is distinct from the internal reorganisation of existing States under Article 3.

Article 3: Formation and alteration of States

Parliament may by law:

  • Form a new State by separation of territory from any State or by uniting two or more States or parts of States, or by uniting any territory to a part of any State.
  • Increase the area of any State.
  • Diminish the area of any State.
  • Alter the boundaries of any State.
  • Alter the name of any State.

Procedure under Article 3

  1. A Bill for the purpose may be introduced in either House of Parliament only on the recommendation of the President.
  2. Where the proposal affects the area, boundaries or name of a State, the President refers the Bill to the legislature of that State for expressing its views within a specified period.
  3. The President may extend the period specified for the State legislature.
  4. Parliament is not constitutionally bound to accept the views of the State legislature.
  5. The Bill must be passed by Parliament and receive the President's assent.

The State legislature's role is consultative, not one of constitutional consent. Its views must be sought where the proviso to Article 3 applies, but Parliament may proceed even if the State legislature disagrees.

Explanation to Article 3

For the purposes of Article 3, the expression "State" includes a Union Territory. However, the proviso requiring reference to a State legislature does not apply to a Union Territory in the same manner as it applies to a State.

3. Article 4 and the Constitutional Amendment Procedure

Article 4 permits laws made under Articles 2 and 3 to include provisions for amendment of the First Schedule and Fourth Schedule and such supplemental, incidental and consequential matters as Parliament considers necessary.

Article 4 expressly provides that such a law shall not be deemed to be an amendment of the Constitution for the purposes of Article 368.

Constitutional implications

  • A law under Article 3 can alter the entries in the First Schedule relating to States and Union Territories.
  • It can make consequential changes to the allocation of seats in the Council of States in the Fourth Schedule.
  • It may include provisions necessary to deal with representation, assets, liabilities, services and administrative arrangements arising from reorganisation.
  • It does not require the special majority procedure under Article 368 merely because it makes such consequential constitutional changes.

Important distinction

A law under Article 3 is an ordinary law passed by Parliament, although it deals with the territorial organisation of the Union. It is not an amendment under Article 368.

4. Acquisition and Cession of Territory

Article 1 recognises that the territory of India may include territories acquired by India. Acquisition of territory and cession of Indian territory involve distinct constitutional questions.

Acquisition of territory

Territory may be acquired through constitutionally recognised means consistent with international law. Once incorporated into India, the territory becomes part of the territorial framework of the Union.

Cession of Indian territory

In In re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960), the Supreme Court considered the constitutional procedure for implementing an agreement involving the transfer of Indian territory to Pakistan.

The Court held that cession of Indian territory to a foreign country could not be accomplished merely through a law under Article 3. A constitutional amendment under Article 368 was required.

The Constitution (Ninth Amendment) Act, 1960 was enacted in connection with the implementation of the India-Pakistan agreement concerning the transfer of specified territories.

Territorial reorganisation versus cession

  • Internal reorganisation changes the arrangement of territory within India.
  • Cession involves the transfer of Indian territory to another sovereign State.
  • Article 3 is the principal provision for internal reorganisation.
  • Cession of Indian territory requires constitutional amendment, as established in the Berubari Reference.

5. Evolution of State Reorganisation

India's territorial organisation has evolved through constitutional laws, parliamentary legislation, commissions and political decisions. Linguistic, administrative, cultural and economic considerations have influenced the formation and reorganisation of States.

5.1 Linguistic reorganisation

After Independence, demands emerged for States organised substantially on linguistic lines. The movement for a separate Telugu-speaking State became a major turning point.

Andhra State was created in 1953 from the Telugu-speaking areas of the former Madras State. This intensified demands for wider territorial reorganisation.

5.2 States Reorganisation Commission

The Government of India appointed the States Reorganisation Commission in 1953, chaired by Fazl Ali, with H.N. Kunzru and K.M. Panikkar as members.

The Commission examined the principles and practical considerations for reorganising States. Its work contributed to the States Reorganisation Act, 1956 and the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956.

5.3 States Reorganisation Act, 1956

The Act reorganised the territorial structure of India with effect from 1 November 1956. The constitutional changes removed the earlier classification of Part A, Part B and Part C States and established a more uniform arrangement of States and Union Territories.

5.4 Later territorial changes

Subsequent decades saw the creation of additional States and the alteration of State boundaries. Important developments include:

  • Maharashtra and Gujarat were formed in 1960 following the division of Bombay State.
  • Haryana was created in 1966 following the reorganisation of Punjab.
  • Several north-eastern States were formed or reorganised to address regional, ethnic and administrative considerations.
  • Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand were created in 2000.
  • Telangana was created in 2014 following the reorganisation of Andhra Pradesh.

5.5 Jammu and Kashmir reorganisation

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 reorganised the former State of Jammu and Kashmir into the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh.

In In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (2023), the Supreme Court considered the constitutional measures concerning Article 370 and the reorganisation of the former State. The Court upheld the constitutional validity of the abrogation measures and addressed the status and restoration of statehood in its directions.

The case is important for understanding the relationship between parliamentary power, Union Territories, federalism and constitutional procedure.

6. Union Territories: Constitutional Framework

Union Territories are constituent parts of the territory of India administered under the constitutional framework applicable to them. Unlike States, they do not all possess the same degree of legislative and executive autonomy.

Article 239: Administration of Union Territories

Article 239 provides that every Union Territory shall be administered by the President acting, to such extent as the President thinks fit, through an administrator appointed by the President with such designation as may be specified.

The administrator functions under the constitutional authority of the President. The administrative arrangement may differ according to the special provisions applicable to a particular Union Territory.

Article 239A: Local legislatures or Council of Ministers

Article 239A empowers Parliament to create by law a legislature, a Council of Ministers, or both for certain Union Territories, subject to the constitutional provision.

It is particularly relevant to the special constitutional arrangements for Puducherry.

Article 239AA: Special provisions for Delhi

Article 239AA provides a special constitutional framework for the National Capital Territory of Delhi.

  • Delhi has a Legislative Assembly with elected representatives.
  • The Assembly may legislate on matters in the State List and Concurrent List, except the constitutionally excluded subjects.
  • Public order, police and land are excluded from the legislative competence of the Delhi Legislative Assembly.
  • Parliament retains legislative power over Delhi, including on matters within the Assembly's legislative field.
  • A Council of Ministers headed by the Chief Minister aids and advises the Lieutenant Governor in matters within its constitutional competence, subject to the applicable provisions.

Article 239AB: Failure of constitutional machinery

Article 239AB provides for action where the administration of the National Capital Territory cannot be carried on in accordance with Article 239AA or laws made under it. It provides a constitutional mechanism for addressing specified failures in the functioning of the NCT's constitutional arrangement.

Article 239B: Power to promulgate ordinances

Article 239B provides for the promulgation of ordinances by the administrator of Puducherry during the recess of its legislature, subject to constitutional conditions and the directions of the President.

Article 240: Regulation-making power

Article 240 empowers the President to make regulations for the peace, progress and good government of specified Union Territories. Such regulations may have the force of an Act of Parliament and may repeal or amend applicable laws, subject to the constitutional provision.

Article 241: High Courts for Union Territories

Article 241 empowers Parliament to establish a High Court for a Union Territory or declare an existing court to be a High Court for such territory, subject to the constitutional framework.

8. Special Territorial and Regional Arrangements

The Constitution provides different institutional arrangements to address India's geographical, historical, cultural and administrative diversity.

National Capital Territory of Delhi

Delhi has a special constitutional status under Article 239AA. Its arrangement combines an elected legislature and Council of Ministers with the constitutional role of the Lieutenant Governor and the legislative authority of Parliament.

Puducherry

Puducherry has a legislature and Council of Ministers under the framework of Article 239A and the Government of Union Territories Act, 1963. Its constitutional arrangement differs from that of Delhi.

Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh

The Jammu and Kashmir Reorganisation Act, 2019 created two Union Territories with distinct administrative arrangements. Jammu and Kashmir has a legislative framework under the applicable law, while Ladakh is administered as a Union Territory without a legislature under the existing statutory arrangement.

Fifth and Sixth Schedules

The Fifth and Sixth Schedules provide special arrangements for the administration and governance of specified tribal areas. These arrangements operate within the constitutional framework and should not be confused with the constitutional status of Union Territories.

9. Citizenship: Constitutional Framework

Citizenship establishes the legal relationship between an individual and the Indian State. It determines membership of the political community and is relevant to political rights, constitutional protections and public obligations.

Part II of the Constitution, comprising Articles 5 to 11, deals with citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution and empowers Parliament to regulate citizenship by law.

Single citizenship

India follows the principle of single citizenship. A person is a citizen of India rather than holding separate citizenship of an individual State within the Union.

This supports a common national political identity and is consistent with the constitutional framework of a Union of States.

10. Citizenship at the Commencement of the Constitution

Articles 5 to 11 primarily addressed who would be recognised as an Indian citizen when the Constitution commenced on 26 January 1950. They must be distinguished from the continuing statutory framework under the Citizenship Act, 1955.

Article 5: Citizenship by domicile

At the commencement of the Constitution, every person who had domicile in the territory of India and satisfied one of the following conditions was a citizen of India:

  • The person was born in the territory of India.
  • Either of the person's parents was born in the territory of India.
  • The person had been ordinarily resident in the territory of India for not less than five years immediately preceding the commencement of the Constitution.

Article 5 therefore combined domicile with specified connections of birth, parentage or residence.

Article 6: Rights of citizenship of certain persons who migrated from Pakistan to India

Article 6 dealt with persons who migrated to India from the territory now included in Pakistan before the commencement of the Constitution.

It prescribed conditions relating to the place of birth of the person or parents or grandparents, and the date of migration.

  • For migration before 19 July 1948, ordinary residence in India since migration was relevant.
  • For migration on or after 19 July 1948, registration as a citizen was required under the prescribed procedure.
  • The registration route required residence in India for at least six months immediately preceding the application.

Article 7: Migrants to Pakistan

Article 7 dealt with persons who migrated from India to Pakistan after 1 March 1947. Such persons were generally excluded from citizenship under Articles 5 and 6.

An exception was provided for persons who returned to India under a permit for resettlement or permanent return issued under the relevant legal framework. Such persons were treated under the provisions applicable to migration from Pakistan after 19 July 1948.

Article 8: Persons of Indian origin residing outside India

Article 8 dealt with persons of Indian origin who ordinarily resided outside India and who were registered as citizens by an Indian diplomatic or consular representative in the country where they resided.

The provision recognised specified ancestral connections with India and provided a route to citizenship through registration.

Article 9: Voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship

Article 9 provides that a person shall not be a citizen of India under Articles 5, 6 or 8 if that person has voluntarily acquired the citizenship of a foreign State.

This reflects the constitutional rejection of simultaneous Indian citizenship and voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship under the framework applicable to the commencement of the Constitution.

Article 10: Continuance of citizenship

Article 10 provides for the continuance of citizenship of persons who are or are deemed to be citizens under the preceding provisions, subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament.

Article 11: Parliament's power to regulate citizenship

Article 11 empowers Parliament to make laws concerning the acquisition and termination of citizenship and all other matters relating to citizenship.

This provision is the constitutional basis for the Citizenship Act, 1955 and subsequent amendments.

11. Citizenship Act, 1955

Parliament enacted the Citizenship Act, 1955 under Article 11. It provides the statutory framework for acquisition and determination of Indian citizenship after the commencement of the Constitution.

Modes of acquisition of citizenship

The Citizenship Act, 1955 provides five principal modes of acquiring citizenship:

  1. Citizenship by birth.
  2. Citizenship by descent.
  3. Citizenship by registration.
  4. Citizenship by naturalisation.
  5. Citizenship by incorporation of territory.

11.1 Citizenship by birth

The conditions for citizenship by birth have changed over time. The applicable rule depends on the date of birth.

  • 26 January 1950 to 30 June 1987: A person born in India during this period was generally a citizen by birth, irrespective of the citizenship of the parents, subject to the statutory exceptions.
  • 1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004: Citizenship by birth generally required at least one parent to be an Indian citizen at the time of birth.
  • On or after 3 December 2004: Citizenship by birth generally requires both parents to be Indian citizens, or one parent to be an Indian citizen and the other not to be an illegal migrant at the time of birth.

The Act contains exceptions, including specified cases involving foreign diplomatic representatives and enemy aliens. The exact statutory conditions must be applied to the individual case.

11.2 Citizenship by descent

Citizenship by descent concerns persons born outside India to Indian citizen parents. The applicable conditions have changed according to the date of birth and statutory amendments.

  • For births outside India before 10 December 1992, citizenship generally depended on the father being an Indian citizen.
  • For births outside India on or after 10 December 1992, either parent could transmit citizenship, subject to the applicable statutory conditions.
  • For births on or after 3 December 2004, registration of the birth at an Indian consulate within the prescribed period is generally required, subject to the statutory conditions and exceptions.

11.3 Citizenship by registration

The Citizenship Act permits specified categories of persons to acquire citizenship through registration, subject to eligibility and prescribed conditions.

Categories include, among others:

  • Persons of Indian origin ordinarily resident in India for the prescribed period.
  • Persons of Indian origin ordinarily resident in a country or place outside undivided India.
  • Certain persons married to Indian citizens and satisfying the residence requirements.
  • Minor children of Indian citizens.
  • Certain persons whose parents are registered as Indian citizens.
  • Certain persons registered as Overseas Citizen of India Cardholders for the prescribed period and satisfying the applicable conditions.

11.4 Citizenship by naturalisation

A foreign national may acquire Indian citizenship by naturalisation if the statutory conditions are satisfied and the Central Government grants a certificate of naturalisation.

The Third Schedule to the Citizenship Act specifies conditions relating to residence or service in India, good character, knowledge of a language specified in the Eighth Schedule and intention to reside in India or serve under an Indian government.

The general residence requirement includes the prescribed period of residence or government service immediately before the application and the aggregate period during the preceding fourteen years. Statutory exceptions and special provisions may apply to specified categories.

11.5 Citizenship by incorporation of territory

Where a territory becomes part of India, the Central Government may specify the persons who shall become citizens of India by reason of their connection with that territory.

This mode recognises the citizenship consequences of territorial changes and is distinct from acquisition by birth, descent, registration or naturalisation.

12. Loss and Termination of Citizenship

The Citizenship Act, 1955 provides three principal modes through which Indian citizenship may cease:

12.1 Renunciation

An Indian citizen of full age and capacity may make a declaration renouncing Indian citizenship. Upon registration of the declaration, the person ceases to be an Indian citizen, subject to the statutory provisions.

Where the declaration is made during a period in which India is engaged in war, its registration may be withheld by the Central Government under the Act.

12.2 Termination

Where an Indian citizen voluntarily acquires the citizenship of another country, Indian citizenship terminates under the statutory framework, subject to the provisions of the Act.

12.3 Deprivation

The Central Government may deprive a person of Indian citizenship in specified cases where citizenship was acquired by naturalisation, registration or other covered statutory routes.

Grounds include specified forms of disloyalty, unlawful trade or communication with an enemy during war, certain serious criminal conduct within the prescribed period, prolonged residence abroad in specified circumstances and acquisition through fraud or concealment of material facts.

Deprivation is subject to statutory safeguards and procedural requirements. It should not be confused with automatic termination following voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.

Mode Nature Key distinction
Renunciation Voluntary declaration Initiated by the citizen.
Termination Statutory consequence Generally follows voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship.
Deprivation Government action Applies on specified statutory grounds and subject to procedure.

13. Major Amendments to Citizenship Law

The Citizenship Act has been amended several times to address migration, overseas Indian connections, illegal migration and naturalisation.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1986

The amendment changed the conditions for citizenship by birth by requiring, for persons born after the specified commencement date, at least one parent to be an Indian citizen.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 1992

The amendment modified the descent provision to permit citizenship through either parent for persons born outside India on or after the relevant commencement date.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2003

The amendment introduced significant changes relating to illegal migration, citizenship by birth and the framework for Overseas Citizen of India registration.

It also provided for the concept of a National Register of Indian Citizens through amendments to the Act.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2005

The amendment developed the Overseas Citizen of India framework and expanded specified eligibility categories.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2015

The amendment reorganised the Person of Indian Origin and Overseas Citizen of India schemes into the Overseas Citizen of India Cardholder framework.

Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019

The 2019 amendment created a special statutory route to citizenship by registration or naturalisation for specified communities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014.

The specified communities are Hindus, Sikhs, Buddhists, Jains, Parsis and Christians. The amendment provides that members of these specified communities covered by the provision shall not be treated as illegal migrants for the purposes of the Citizenship Act.

The amendment also reduced the specified aggregate residence or government-service requirement for eligible persons under the relevant naturalisation provisions, subject to the statutory conditions.

The Act excludes specified tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Mizoram and Tripura included in the Sixth Schedule and areas covered by the Inner Line Permit regime, as provided by the amendment.

Rules and implementation

The Citizenship (Amendment) Rules, 2024 were notified to operationalise the 2019 amendment. The rules prescribe application, scrutiny, documentation and certificate procedures for the specified categories.

The statutory eligibility criteria and the prescribed administrative procedure should be distinguished from broader political and constitutional debates concerning the amendment.

14. Overseas Citizen of India (OCI)

The Overseas Citizen of India Cardholder scheme provides specified privileges to eligible foreign citizens of Indian origin. It does not confer full Indian citizenship or create dual citizenship.

Nature of OCI status

  • OCI status is a statutory immigration and registration arrangement.
  • An OCI Cardholder remains a foreign citizen of the country whose passport the person holds.
  • OCI status does not confer the right to vote in Indian elections.
  • OCI Cardholders are not eligible to become members of Parliament or State legislatures.
  • OCI status does not confer eligibility for constitutional offices reserved for Indian citizens.
  • The statutory scheme provides specified travel, residence and other privileges, subject to applicable conditions.

OCI and dual citizenship

India does not recognise dual citizenship in the sense of permitting a person to hold Indian citizenship simultaneously with voluntarily acquired foreign citizenship.

OCI is not a second citizenship. It is a special statutory status for eligible foreign nationals of Indian origin.

16. Citizenship, Federalism and National Integration

India's system of single citizenship complements its federal structure. Individuals are members of one national political community even though governmental authority is distributed between the Union and the States.

Constitutional significance

  • It reinforces a common national identity across State boundaries.
  • It supports the constitutional principle of equality of citizenship.
  • It facilitates freedom of movement, residence and occupation, subject to constitutional restrictions.
  • It connects national political rights with the federal distribution of governmental powers.

Important distinction

Single citizenship does not mean that all States have identical administrative, cultural or territorial arrangements. The Constitution permits special provisions and differentiated arrangements while retaining a common national citizenship.

17. Constitutional and Governance Issues

17.1 Federalism and State reorganisation

Parliament's power under Article 3 enables flexible territorial reorganisation, but it also raises questions about the role of affected State legislatures and the balance between national authority and regional democratic consent.

A sound approach requires constitutional procedure, consultation, transparent reasoning and attention to administrative and fiscal consequences.

17.2 Governance of Union Territories

Union Territories are administered through different institutional arrangements. Where elected legislatures exist, disputes may arise concerning the division of responsibilities between elected governments and administrators.

Clear allocation of functions, cooperative administration, accountability and respect for constitutional boundaries are essential.

17.3 Citizenship and migration

Citizenship law must address migration, documentation, statelessness, humanitarian concerns and national security within constitutional and statutory limits.

Determination of citizenship can involve difficult questions of evidence, historical records, family connections and administrative fairness.

17.4 Equality and non-discrimination

Citizenship legislation and its implementation must be examined in light of constitutional guarantees, particularly equality before law, non-discrimination and the protection of life and personal liberty.

17.5 Documentation and due process

Administrative procedures relating to citizenship require reliable records, transparent criteria, accessible remedies and safeguards against arbitrary decisions. The consequences of incorrect determination can be severe for individuals and families.

18. Important Judicial Decisions

In re: Berubari Union and Exchange of Enclaves (1960)

Established that cession of Indian territory to a foreign country requires a constitutional amendment and cannot be achieved merely through Article 3.

State of West Bengal v. Union of India (1963)

Examined the nature of the Indian Union and the constitutional relationship between the Union and States. The judgment is important for understanding the distinctive features of Indian federalism.

Pradeep Jain v. Union of India (1984)

Considered residence and domicile-related preferences in the context of admissions to educational institutions and discussed the constitutional significance of national unity and common citizenship.

Louis De Raedt v. Union of India (1991)

Considered the position of foreign nationals under Article 21 and recognised that non-citizens are entitled to the protection of life and personal liberty, while the right to reside and settle in India under Article 19(1)(e) is reserved for citizens.

National Human Rights Commission v. State of Arunachal Pradesh (1996)

The Supreme Court addressed the protection of Chakmas in Arunachal Pradesh and emphasised the obligation of the State to protect the life and liberty of persons, including those who are not citizens.

In Re: Article 370 of the Constitution (2023)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional measures relating to Article 370 and the reorganisation of Jammu and Kashmir. The judgment is significant for questions involving constitutional federalism, Union Territories and parliamentary power.
