1. Constitutional Framework and Purpose

Emergency provisions are constitutional mechanisms through which the Union can respond to exceptional threats to national security, constitutional government in a State or financial stability. They permit temporary departures from ordinary federal and institutional arrangements while remaining subject to constitutional conditions and safeguards.

Part XVIII of the Constitution, comprising Articles 352 to 360, deals with emergency provisions. These provisions seek to protect the sovereignty, unity, integrity and constitutional functioning of India. Their extraordinary powers must be distinguished from ordinary administrative and legislative powers.

Type Article Constitutional trigger
National Emergency 352 War, external aggression or armed rebellion.
President's Rule 356 Failure of constitutional machinery in a State.
Financial Emergency 360 Threat to the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory.

Article 355 is an important connecting provision. It places a duty on the Union to protect every State against external aggression and internal disturbance and to ensure that the government of every State is carried on in accordance with the Constitution.

2. National Emergency — Article 352

2.1 Grounds for Proclamation

The President may proclaim a National Emergency if satisfied that the security of India or any part of its territory is threatened by:

  • War.
  • External aggression.
  • Armed rebellion.

The proclamation may be made before the actual occurrence of war, external aggression or armed rebellion if there is imminent danger of such an occurrence.

The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978 replaced the expression "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion". This narrowed the ground for declaring a National Emergency and responded to the misuse of emergency powers during 1975.

2.2 Procedure and Parliamentary Approval

  1. The President issues the proclamation on the advice of the Union Council of Ministers.
  2. The decision of the Union Cabinet must be communicated to the President in writing. This written advice requirement was introduced by the 44th Amendment.
  3. The proclamation must be laid before both Houses of Parliament.
  4. It must be approved by both Houses within one month from its issue. Otherwise, it ceases to operate.
  5. Approval requires a special majority in each House: a majority of the total membership of that House and not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.
  6. Once approved, the proclamation continues for six months at a time and may be extended through further parliamentary approvals using the same special majority.

Where the Lok Sabha is dissolved or its dissolution takes place during the prescribed approval period, the Constitution provides a special procedure involving approval by the Rajya Sabha and subsequent approval by the newly constituted Lok Sabha within the prescribed period.

2.3 Revocation and Parliamentary Control

  • The President may revoke a National Emergency by a subsequent proclamation. A fresh parliamentary approval is not required for revocation.
  • The President must revoke it if the Lok Sabha passes a resolution disapproving its continuation.
  • The Lok Sabha may pass a disapproval resolution by a simple majority of members present and voting.
  • If at least one-tenth of the total members of the Lok Sabha give written notice of their intention to move a disapproval resolution, a special sitting must be held within fourteen days.

2.4 Territorial Scope

A National Emergency may apply to the whole of India or only a specified part of its territory. The Constitution also permits a proclamation where the threat arises from events in a part of India or outside India, subject to the constitutional conditions.

3. Effects of National Emergency

3.1 Effect on Federal Distribution of Legislative Powers

During a National Emergency, Parliament's legislative authority expands beyond the ordinary Union and Concurrent List framework.

  • Article 250: Parliament may legislate on matters in the State List while a National Emergency is in operation.
  • Article 251: Where a parliamentary law under Article 250 conflicts with a State law, the parliamentary law prevails to the extent of inconsistency while it remains operative.
  • Article 353: The executive power of the Union extends to giving directions to a State regarding the manner in which its executive power is to be exercised. Parliament's legislative powers and the Union's executive authority may extend to matters beyond their ordinary constitutional limits, subject to the Article's conditions.

A law made by Parliament under Article 250 ceases to have effect to the extent of its incompetence six months after the Emergency ceases, except as regards things done or omitted before that expiry.

3.2 Effect on the Tenure of Legislatures

  • Article 83(2): Parliament may extend the normal five-year term of the Lok Sabha by law for a period not exceeding one year at a time during a National Emergency. Such extension cannot continue beyond six months after the Emergency ceases.
  • Article 172(1): The same principle applies to the legislative assembly of a State.

3.3 Effect on Distribution of Revenue

Under Article 354, the President may modify the constitutional provisions relating to distribution of revenues between the Union and States during the period specified by the Article. Such an order must be laid before each House of Parliament.

3.4 Effect on Fundamental Rights: Article 358

Article 358 deals specifically with the effect of a National Emergency on Article 19.

  • Article 19 is automatically affected only when the Emergency is declared on the grounds of war or external aggression.
  • Article 19 is not automatically suspended when the Emergency is declared on the ground of armed rebellion.
  • Article 358 protects qualifying State action from challenge under Article 19 during the relevant period, subject to the constitutional conditions.
  • Following the 44th Amendment, a law claiming this protection must contain a recital that it relates to the proclamation, and the protection is limited by the constitutional text to the relevant period and actions taken under such law.

3.5 Effect on Enforcement of Fundamental Rights: Article 359

Article 359 empowers the President to issue an order suspending the right to move courts for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during a National Emergency.

  • The Presidential order must identify the rights whose enforcement is affected.
  • The order may apply to the whole or a specified part of India.
  • It may suspend pending proceedings for enforcement of the specified rights, subject to its terms.
  • Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included in an Article 359 order.
  • The suspension concerns the judicial remedy for enforcement of specified rights; it does not mean that all Fundamental Rights are automatically extinguished.

4. President's Rule — Article 356

4.1 Constitutional Ground

Article 356 applies where the President, on receipt of a report from the Governor of a State or otherwise, is satisfied that a situation has arisen in which the government of the State cannot be carried on in accordance with the provisions of the Constitution.

The President's satisfaction is subject to judicial review. The provision is not intended to be a routine instrument for removing a State government because of political disagreement or a loss of confidence that has not been established through constitutionally appropriate means.

4.2 Procedure and Approval

  1. The President issues a proclamation under Article 356.
  2. The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months.
  3. If approved, it continues for six months from the date of issue.
  4. Parliament may extend it for further periods of six months through resolutions passed by both Houses.
  5. Ordinarily, President's Rule cannot continue beyond three years.

Extension beyond one year is constitutionally permitted only when the prescribed conditions are satisfied: a National Emergency must be in operation in the whole of India or in the whole or any part of the State concerned, and the Election Commission must certify that continuation is necessary because of difficulties in holding general elections to the State Legislative Assembly.

The President may revoke the proclamation at any time. Revocation does not require parliamentary approval.

4.3 Consequences of President's Rule

  • The President may assume the functions of the State Government and powers vested in the Governor or other State authorities, subject to constitutional limitations.
  • The powers of the State Legislature may be exercised by or under the authority of Parliament.
  • The State Legislative Assembly may be dissolved or kept under suspension, as specified in the proclamation and subsequent constitutional action.
  • The High Court's constitutional position and powers cannot be assumed by the President.
  • Parliament may delegate legislative powers concerning the State to the President or another authority in accordance with Article 357.

4.4 Legislative Consequences

Article 357 enables Parliament to exercise the legislative powers of the State Legislature during President's Rule. It may authorise the President to make laws for the State and permit further delegation within the constitutional framework.

A law made under Article 357 continues in force after President's Rule ends until altered, repealed or amended by a competent legislature or authority. The end of President's Rule does not automatically invalidate all such laws.

5. Judicial Review and Safeguards under Article 356

5.1 S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

S.R. Bommai is the leading decision on the constitutional limits of Article 356. The Supreme Court established important principles governing the exercise of President's Rule.

  • The President's satisfaction is subject to judicial review.
  • The material on which the proclamation is based may be examined by the courts.
  • A proclamation based on irrelevant, extraneous or mala fide considerations can be invalidated.
  • Where a government is alleged to have lost its majority, the appropriate place to test legislative confidence is ordinarily the floor of the House.
  • The State Legislative Assembly should not be irreversibly dissolved before parliamentary approval of the proclamation.
  • If the proclamation is invalidated, the court may restore the dismissed government and the Legislative Assembly where appropriate.
  • Secularism is part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution; conduct that violates this constitutional principle may be relevant to Article 356 in an appropriate case.

5.2 Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)

The Supreme Court invalidated the dissolution of the Bihar Legislative Assembly before it had met, holding that the material relied upon did not justify the constitutional action. The judgment reinforced judicial scrutiny of the exercise of Article 356.

5.3 State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977)

The case addressed the scope of judicial review of a proclamation under Article 356. Its approach must be understood alongside the more detailed safeguards subsequently articulated in S.R. Bommai.

5.4 Constitutional Safeguards

  • Parliamentary approval within two months.
  • Six-monthly parliamentary review of continuation.
  • Constitutional restrictions on continuation beyond one year and an absolute three-year ceiling.
  • Judicial review of the President's satisfaction.
  • Floor test as the normal method for determining legislative majority.
  • Protection of the institutional independence and constitutional jurisdiction of High Courts.

6. Financial Emergency — Article 360

6.1 Ground for Proclamation

The President may proclaim a Financial Emergency if satisfied that a situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or any part of its territory is threatened.

The Constitution does not require an actual financial collapse. The relevant constitutional test is whether financial stability or credit is threatened.

6.2 Procedure and Approval

  • The President issues the proclamation under Article 360.
  • It must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months.
  • If the Lok Sabha is dissolved or is dissolved during the approval period, the Constitution provides a special procedure involving approval by the Rajya Sabha and subsequent approval by the newly constituted Lok Sabha.
  • Once approved, the Constitution does not prescribe a fixed maximum duration or periodic six-monthly renewal requirement.
  • The President may revoke the proclamation at any time.

6.3 Effects of Financial Emergency

During a Financial Emergency, the Union may exercise significant supervisory powers over State financial arrangements.

  • The Union may give directions to States to observe specified canons of financial propriety.
  • Directions may include requirements concerning reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving in connection with the affairs of a State.
  • The President may issue directions for reduction of salaries and allowances of persons serving in connection with the affairs of the Union, including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • The President may require Money Bills or other financial Bills passed by State Legislatures to be reserved for consideration after being presented to the Governor.

6.4 Status in India

No Financial Emergency under Article 360 has been proclaimed in India so far. The provision remains part of the constitutional framework for exceptional financial conditions.

8. Historical Use of National Emergency

India has experienced three National Emergency proclamations:

Period Ground Historical context
1962 External aggression Proclaimed during the conflict with China; continued until 1968.
1971 External aggression Proclaimed in the context of the conflict with Pakistan; continued until 1977.
1975 Internal disturbance Proclaimed on 25 June 1975 and revoked in March 1977.

The 1971 and 1975 proclamations overlapped. The Emergency of 1975 is particularly significant because of restrictions on civil liberties, detention without ordinary safeguards, press censorship and concentration of executive power.

The experience demonstrated the need for stronger parliamentary oversight, a narrower constitutional trigger, protection of core rights and safeguards against arbitrary use of emergency authority.

9. The 44th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1978

The 44th Amendment introduced major safeguards in response to the experience of the 1975 Emergency.

  • Replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for National Emergency.
  • Required written communication of the Union Cabinet's decision to the President.
  • Introduced the special majority requirement for parliamentary approval and continuation of a National Emergency.
  • Reduced the initial parliamentary approval period from two months to one month.
  • Provided a mechanism for a special sitting of the Lok Sabha to consider disapproval.
  • Restricted the operation of Article 358 to emergencies declared on grounds of war or external aggression.
  • Protected Articles 20 and 21 from suspension under Article 359.
  • Strengthened the conditions governing extension of President's Rule beyond one year.
  • Restored important safeguards concerning the normal tenure of elected legislatures.

The Amendment is a central constitutional safeguard against misuse of emergency powers and an important example of constitutional correction through the amendment process.

10. Emergency Provisions and Constitutional Federalism

India's federal structure ordinarily distributes legislative, executive and financial authority between the Union and States. Emergency provisions temporarily alter this distribution in response to exceptional circumstances.

  • National Emergency: Enables greater Union control over legislative and executive matters and permits Parliament to legislate on State List subjects under Article 250.
  • President's Rule: Temporarily shifts specified State executive and legislative functions to the Union.
  • Financial Emergency: Enables the Union to issue directions affecting State financial management.

These powers reveal the centralising features of Indian federalism. Their legitimate use depends on necessity, proportionality, constitutional compliance and effective institutional checks.

The misuse of Article 356 can weaken elected State governments and democratic federalism. The safeguards developed in S.R. Bommai are therefore significant for preserving the constitutional balance between the Union and States.

11. Emergency Powers and Fundamental Rights

Emergency provisions create a constitutional tension between collective security and individual liberty. The Constitution does not treat every emergency as authority to suspend all rights or eliminate judicial scrutiny.

  • Article 358 has a specific and limited operation concerning Article 19.
  • Article 359 requires a Presidential order specifying the rights whose judicial enforcement is affected.
  • Articles 20 and 21 remain protected from an Article 359 order.
  • Judicial review of the constitutional validity of emergency action remains important.
  • Restrictions on liberty must remain within the constitutional text and applicable legal safeguards.

In ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976), the majority adopted an approach that denied habeas corpus relief in the circumstances of the 1975 Emergency. The judgment was subsequently expressly overruled by the Supreme Court in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017).

12. Constitutional Issues and Challenges

  • Concentration of power: Emergency powers may significantly strengthen the Union executive and reduce ordinary institutional checks.
  • Risk to civil liberties: Restrictions on rights and remedies may affect personal liberty, freedom of expression and democratic participation.
  • Federal imbalance: Excessive reliance on Article 356 can undermine elected State governments and the federal distribution of authority.
  • Political misuse: Constitutional provisions may be invoked for partisan or political objectives rather than genuine constitutional necessity.
  • Institutional oversight: Effective parliamentary scrutiny, judicial review and transparent decision-making are essential.
  • Financial autonomy: Financial directions under Article 360 could affect the fiscal autonomy of States and the functioning of constitutional institutions.
  • Rights-security balance: Exceptional security requirements must be reconciled with constitutional liberty and non-derogable protections.

13. Reforms and Way Forward

  • Use emergency powers only when ordinary constitutional mechanisms are demonstrably inadequate.
  • Ensure written, reasoned and constitutionally relevant material supports emergency decisions.
  • Strengthen parliamentary debate and meaningful scrutiny of proclamations and extensions.
  • Follow the floor-test principle when determining whether a State government retains legislative confidence.
  • Respect judicial review and the safeguards laid down in S.R. Bommai.
  • Preserve Articles 20 and 21 and the constitutional limits on restrictions affecting Fundamental Rights.
  • Maintain transparency in the use of emergency authority and provide appropriate institutional accountability.
  • Protect the federal balance and ensure that exceptional powers do not become instruments of routine political administration.

15. Important Distinctions

Concepts Distinction
National Emergency and President's Rule Article 352 responds to threats to national security; Article 356 addresses failure of constitutional machinery in a State.
Article 358 and Article 359 Article 358 concerns Article 19 in specified emergencies; Article 359 concerns the right to move courts for enforcement of specified rights.
Internal disturbance and armed rebellion Internal disturbance was the earlier ground under Article 352; the 44th Amendment replaced it with the narrower term armed rebellion.
Suspension of a legislature and dissolution Suspension keeps the Assembly in abeyance; dissolution brings its existence to an end and requires a new election to constitute another Assembly.
Article 355 and Article 356 Article 355 states the Union's constitutional duty; Article 356 provides a specific mechanism for dealing with failure of constitutional machinery.
Financial Emergency and fiscal stress Ordinary fiscal difficulties do not by themselves constitute a Financial Emergency; Article 360 requires a threat to financial stability or credit.

16. Integrated Revision Framework

Analyse emergency provisions through six connected questions:

  1. Trigger: What constitutional condition permits the proclamation?
  2. Authority: Who issues it, and what advice or material is required?
  3. Approval: What parliamentary majority and time limit apply?
  4. Effects: How are federal powers, legislatures, finances and rights affected?
  5. Safeguards: What parliamentary, judicial and constitutional checks operate?
  6. Constitutional impact: What are the implications for democracy, liberty, federalism and accountability?
