1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

A constitutional amendment is a formal alteration of the Constitution through the procedure prescribed by the Constitution itself. It may add, modify or repeal constitutional provisions. Amendment enables a Constitution to respond to changing social, political, economic and institutional circumstances without abandoning constitutional continuity.

The Indian Constitution combines stability with adaptability. It is neither completely rigid nor completely flexible. Its amendment framework seeks to reconcile democratic change, federal participation and protection of foundational constitutional principles.

Constitutional amendment must be distinguished from ordinary legislation. Parliament exercises ordinary legislative power when it makes laws within the legislative fields assigned by the Constitution. When it changes the constitutional text through Article 368, it exercises constituent power.

2. Historical Background and Constitutional Design

The framers recognised that a Constitution intended to endure could not be made incapable of change. At the same time, they did not want fundamental constitutional arrangements to be altered through the same procedure as an ordinary law.

The Constitution therefore distributes amendment procedures across different provisions. Some changes can be made by a simple parliamentary majority; most formal amendments require a special majority under Article 368; and amendments affecting specified federal features additionally require ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

Article 368 is located in Part XX of the Constitution. Its present structure reflects, in particular, changes introduced by the Constitution (Twenty-fourth Amendment) Act, 1971, and the constitutional limits subsequently developed by the Supreme Court.

3. Article 368: Scope of the Amending Power

Article 368(1) authorises Parliament, in exercise of its constituent power, to amend the Constitution by way of addition, variation or repeal, subject to the procedure laid down in Article 368.

Article 368(2) prescribes the legislative process and identifies categories of amendments requiring State ratification. Article 368(3) provides that Article 13 does not apply to amendments made under Article 368.

Clauses (4) and (5), inserted by the Forty-second Amendment, attempted to exclude judicial review of constitutional amendments and declare Parliament's amending power unlimited. The Supreme Court invalidated these provisions in Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), reaffirming that limited amending power and judicial review are integral to the constitutional scheme.

Article 368 confers a wide constituent power, but it does not authorise Parliament to destroy the Constitution's basic structure.

4. Three Methods of Constitutional Change

4.1 Changes by simple parliamentary majority

Certain constitutional changes are expressly placed outside the procedure of Article 368. They are generally made through ordinary legislation passed by a simple majority of members present and voting, subject to the relevant constitutional provision.

Examples include:

  • Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing States under Articles 2, 3 and 4.
  • Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council under Article 169, following the prescribed resolution of the State Legislative Assembly.
  • Creation of certain legislative or council arrangements for Union Territories under Article 239A.
  • Changes to citizenship law under Parliament's legislative authority, including Article 11, through ordinary legislation.
  • Changes to the number of judges of the Supreme Court under Article 124(1), through a law made by Parliament.

Article 4 expressly provides that laws made under Articles 2 and 3 may amend the First and Fourth Schedules and contain supplemental, incidental and consequential provisions. Such changes are not deemed constitutional amendments for the purposes of Article 368.

4.2 Amendments by special majority of Parliament

Most constitutional amendments follow Article 368(2). The Bill must be passed separately in each House of Parliament by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of the members of that House present and voting.

Both requirements must be satisfied. The first is often described as an absolute majority of the House's total membership; the second is a two-thirds majority of members present and voting.

Examples include amendments to provisions concerning Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles, parliamentary institutions and other constitutional matters that do not fall within the State-ratification categories.

4.3 Amendments by special majority plus State ratification

Where an amendment affects specified federal features listed in the proviso to Article 368(2), it must also be ratified by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States.

Ratification is required before the Bill is presented to the President for assent. The Constitution does not prescribe a special majority within each State Legislature for this resolution; the applicable legislative procedure governs its passage.

State ratification is an additional federal safeguard. It is not a substitute for the special majority required in both Houses of Parliament.

5. Matters Requiring State Ratification

The proviso to Article 368(2) requires State ratification when an amendment seeks to make a change in the following matters:

  1. Election of the President: Articles 54 and 55.
  2. Union and State executive powers: Articles 73 and 162.
  3. Union Territories with legislatures: Article 241.
  4. Goods and Services Tax Council: Article 279A.
  5. Union judiciary: Chapter IV of Part V, relating to the Supreme Court.
  6. High Courts: Chapter V of Part VI.
  7. Distribution of legislative powers: Chapter I of Part XI.
  8. Seventh Schedule: Any of the Union, State or Concurrent Lists.
  9. Representation of States in Parliament.
  10. Article 368 itself: The constitutional amendment procedure.

The reference to Article 279A was added by the Constitution (One Hundred and First Amendment) Act, 2016, which introduced the GST framework.

The requirement is ratification by at least half of the States, not necessarily all States. Parliament's special-majority requirement remains applicable in both Houses.

6. Procedure for a Constitutional Amendment

  1. Introduction: A Constitution Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament. It may be introduced by a Minister or a private member.
  2. No State initiation: A State Legislature cannot introduce a Bill under Article 368. It may, however, initiate a request or resolution seeking constitutional change, which does not itself amend the Constitution.
  3. Passage in the first House: The Bill must secure the prescribed special majority in the House in which it is considered.
  4. Passage in the second House: The other House must independently pass the Bill by the same special majority. The Houses cannot substitute a joint sitting for the separate constitutional majorities.
  5. State ratification, where required: At least half of the State Legislatures must ratify the Bill before it is presented to the President.
  6. Presidential assent: The Bill is presented to the President, who is constitutionally required to give assent.
  7. Constitutional amendment: On assent, the Constitution stands amended in accordance with the terms of the Act.

There is no provision for a joint sitting under Article 108 to resolve disagreement between the Houses on a Constitution Amendment Bill. Failure to secure the required majority in either House means that the Bill cannot be passed.

Unlike an ordinary Bill, a Constitution Amendment Bill does not require prior recommendation of the President for introduction. The President's role at the final stage is governed by the express wording of Article 368(2).

8. Limits on the Amending Power: Judicial Evolution

8.1 Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

The Supreme Court upheld the First Constitutional Amendment. It held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not 'law' within the meaning of Article 13 and could amend Fundamental Rights.

8.2 Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

The Court reaffirmed the position in Shankari Prasad. However, separate opinions raised concerns about whether Parliament's amending power could extend without limit to every constitutional feature.

8.3 I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967)

The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights and treated a constitutional amendment as 'law' for Article 13 purposes. The ruling was prospective in operation and intensified the constitutional debate over Parliament's amending authority.

8.4 Twenty-fourth Amendment Act, 1971

Parliament responded by expressly providing that it exercised constituent power under Article 368, that Article 13 did not apply to amendments under that Article, and that the President was required to assent to a duly passed Constitution Amendment Bill.

8.5 Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

A thirteen-judge Bench of the Supreme Court evolved the Basic Structure doctrine. The Court held that Parliament could amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but could not alter or destroy its basic structure.

The judgment reconciled the need for constitutional change with the preservation of the Constitution's identity. It rejected both an unlimited amending power and a complete exclusion of Parliament's authority over Fundamental Rights.

8.6 Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Court invalidated a constitutional provision that sought to immunise the election of a particular constitutional office-holder from judicial scrutiny. The judgment applied basic constitutional principles, including democracy, rule of law and judicial review.

8.7 Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

The Supreme Court invalidated clauses (4) and (5) inserted into Article 368 by the Forty-second Amendment. It held that limited amending power and judicial review are part of the basic structure. It also emphasised the harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

8.8 Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Court applied the basic-structure doctrine to constitutional amendments and distinguished laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before and after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment.

8.9 I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

A nine-judge Bench held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule through constitutional amendments made after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review if they damage or destroy the basic structure. Placement in the Ninth Schedule does not create absolute immunity.

8.10 Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015)

The Supreme Court invalidated the Ninety-ninth Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission framework. The majority held that the arrangement damaged the basic structure, particularly judicial independence.

9. Basic Structure and Constitutional Amendment

The Basic Structure doctrine is the substantive limitation on Parliament's constituent power. It is not an exhaustive list written into Article 368; its application has developed through constitutional adjudication.

Features recognised by the Supreme Court in different cases include:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution.
  • Republican and democratic form of government.
  • Secular character of the Constitution.
  • Federal character of the Constitution.
  • Separation of powers.
  • Judicial review and independence of the judiciary.
  • Rule of law.
  • Free and fair elections.
  • Unity and integrity of India.
  • Limited amending power of Parliament.
  • Harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

The list is illustrative, not exhaustive. Whether a particular amendment violates the basic structure depends on its substance, effect and constitutional context. Not every alteration of a recognised feature automatically destroys the basic structure.

The doctrine does not make the Constitution unamendable. It preserves the Constitution's essential identity while allowing Parliament to respond to new circumstances.

11. Parliament's Amending Power and Federalism

India's amendment procedure reflects a federal arrangement with a strong Union. Parliament has the primary role in initiating and passing constitutional amendments, while State Legislatures participate when the amendment affects specified federal features.

State ratification protects the federal distribution of powers, State representation and important institutional arrangements. However, the Constitution does not require every State to ratify an amendment, nor does it give each State an individual veto.

The requirement of ratification by at least half of the States attempts to balance national constitutional change with the interests of the constituent units.

Federal participation must be understood alongside the legislative distribution in the Seventh Schedule, the role of the Rajya Sabha and the constitutional position of State institutions.

12. Constitutional Amendment and Judicial Review

Judicial review ensures that constitutional amendments comply with both procedural requirements and substantive constitutional limitations.

Courts may examine whether:

  • The amendment was passed by the constitutionally required majority in each House.
  • State ratification was obtained where the proviso to Article 368(2) required it.
  • The amendment violates the Basic Structure doctrine.

Judicial review does not mean that courts replace Parliament's policy judgment with their own. The central question is whether the amendment remains within the constitutional limits of the constituent power.

The relationship between Parliament and the judiciary is therefore one of constitutional checks and balances rather than the unrestricted supremacy of either institution.

13. Contemporary Issues and Reform Debates

  • Constitutional stability and flexibility: Excessive rigidity can obstruct necessary reform, while excessive ease of amendment may weaken constitutional safeguards.
  • Federal consultation: Amendments affecting States benefit from transparent consultation and meaningful deliberation with State Legislatures.
  • Parliamentary scrutiny: Constitution Amendment Bills raise long-term institutional questions and merit careful legislative examination and public debate.
  • Constitutional morality: Formal compliance with Article 368 does not by itself establish the substantive constitutional validity of an amendment.
  • Judicial review: The Basic Structure doctrine protects constitutional identity, but its application requires reasoned judicial analysis.
  • Representation and inclusion: Amendments concerning representation, equality and social justice must be evaluated in light of constitutional values and their practical effects.

14. Way Forward

  • Maintain rigorous legislative scrutiny of constitutional amendment proposals.
  • Promote consultation with State Governments and Legislatures when federal interests are involved.
  • Publish clear explanatory material on the objectives, effects and constitutional implications of proposed amendments.
  • Preserve the distinction between ordinary legislative power and constituent power.
  • Respect the Basic Structure doctrine while recognising Parliament's legitimate authority to adapt the Constitution.
  • Encourage informed public discussion on changes that affect democratic institutions, rights and federal relations.

16. Integrated Revision Framework

For a complete understanding of constitutional amendment, connect the following dimensions:

  1. Constitutional text: Article 368 and provisions that permit changes outside Article 368.
  2. Procedure: Introduction, separate passage in both Houses, special majority, State ratification where required and Presidential assent.
  3. Federalism: The matters requiring State ratification and the role of State Legislatures.
  4. Judicial interpretation: The progression from Shankari Prasad and Golak Nath to Kesavananda Bharati, Minerva Mills and I.R. Coelho.
  5. Constitutional values: The relationship between amendment, democracy, rule of law, rights, federalism and judicial independence.
  6. Institutional balance: Parliament's authority to reform the Constitution and the judiciary's responsibility to enforce constitutional limitations.

A strong constitutional analysis distinguishes the validity of the amendment procedure from the substantive question of whether the amendment damages the Basic Structure.
