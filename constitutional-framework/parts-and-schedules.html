1. Understanding the Structure of the Constitution

The Constitution of India is organised into Parts and Schedules. Parts group provisions dealing with broad constitutional subjects, while Schedules provide supplementary details, classifications, forms, lists and institutional arrangements.

This structure should be understood as an integrated framework rather than as an isolated collection of provisions. For example, the distribution of legislative powers is explained through Articles in Part XI and the Seventh Schedule; the administration of Scheduled Areas involves Part X and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules.

  • Parts: Organise constitutional provisions by subject.
  • Articles: Contain the operative constitutional provisions.
  • Schedules: Supplement and particularise provisions referred to in the constitutional text.

2. Original and Present Structure

At commencement on 26 January 1950, the Constitution contained 395 Articles arranged in 22 Parts and 8 Schedules. Subsequent amendments inserted new Parts and Schedules, modified provisions and repealed certain Parts.

The original numbering and present structure should not be confused. In particular, the repeal of a Part does not necessarily result in renumbering all subsequent Parts. New Parts have also been inserted using lettered Roman numerals.

The Constitution is commonly described as having 25 operative Parts and 12 Schedules. This count reflects the present arrangement, including inserted Parts and excluding the repealed Part VII.

4. Important Inserted and Repealed Parts

Part IVA: Fundamental Duties

  • Inserted by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976.
  • Introduced Article 51A, initially containing ten Fundamental Duties.
  • The Constitution (Eighty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2002 added clause (k), concerning the duty of a parent or guardian to provide educational opportunities to children between six and fourteen years.
  • Fundamental Duties are generally non-justiciable by themselves, but may inform legislation and judicial interpretation.

Part IX: Panchayats

  • Inserted by the Constitution (Seventy-third Amendment) Act, 1992.
  • Provides constitutional recognition to Panchayati Raj institutions.
  • Deals with Gram Sabhas, constitution and composition of Panchayats, reservations, duration, elections and State Finance Commissions.
  • The Eleventh Schedule lists 29 subjects that may be devolved to Panchayats by State legislatures.

Part IXA: Municipalities

  • Inserted by the Constitution (Seventy-fourth Amendment) Act, 1992.
  • Provides a constitutional framework for urban local bodies.
  • Recognises Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils and Municipal Corporations according to the constitutional framework.
  • The Twelfth Schedule lists 18 functions that may be entrusted to municipalities by State legislatures.

Part IXB: Co-operative Societies

  • Inserted by the Constitution (Ninety-seventh Amendment) Act, 2011.
  • Addresses democratic functioning, autonomy and professional management of co-operative societies.
  • In Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021), the Supreme Court held the amendment invalid for want of the required State ratification insofar as it concerned co-operative societies operating within a State. The provisions continue to apply to multi-State co-operative societies and Union Territories.

Part XIVA: Tribunals

  • Inserted by the Constitution (Forty-second Amendment) Act, 1976.
  • Article 323A concerns administrative tribunals, while Article 323B concerns tribunals for other specified matters.
  • Tribunals remain subject to constitutional judicial review by the High Courts and Supreme Court within the framework laid down by the judiciary.

Part VII: Repealed

  • Originally dealt with States in Part B of the First Schedule.
  • Repealed by the Constitution (Seventh Amendment) Act, 1956, as part of the reorganisation of States and the restructuring of the constitutional classification of States.
  • Its repeal did not lead to the renumbering of the remaining Parts.

5. Schedules of the Constitution: Complete Overview

The Constitution originally contained eight Schedules. Four additional Schedules were subsequently inserted. The twelve Schedules perform different functions: some distribute legislative subjects, some establish territorial arrangements, and others provide safeguards, forms or institutional details.

6. Important Schedules: Deeper Understanding

First Schedule: Territorial Organisation

The First Schedule identifies the States and Union Territories and describes their territories. It is closely connected with Articles 1–4, which establish India as a Union of States and empower Parliament to admit, establish and reorganise States.

Changes to the First Schedule may follow legislation under Article 3. A law made under Articles 2 or 3 may also make supplemental, incidental and consequential changes to the First and Fourth Schedules under Article 4.

Second and Third Schedules: Constitutional Offices and Oaths

The Second Schedule specifies certain emoluments, allowances and privileges associated with constitutional offices. The Third Schedule prescribes oath and affirmation forms for specified office-holders and legislators.

These Schedules support institutional independence, constitutional responsibility and the formal assumption of public office. The exact provisions applicable to each office should be read with the relevant Article.

Fourth Schedule: Rajya Sabha Representation

The Fourth Schedule deals with allocation of seats in the Council of States. The constitutional scheme does not provide equal representation to every State irrespective of population. Representation is broadly population-based, subject to the constitutional allocation.

It should be read with Article 80, which establishes the composition of the Rajya Sabha and the method of representation.

Fifth and Sixth Schedules: Tribal Governance

Both Schedules provide special arrangements for tribal communities, but their institutional designs differ.

  • Fifth Schedule: Applies to Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in States other than the tribal areas covered by the Sixth Schedule. It provides for the role of the Governor, Tribes Advisory Councils and special powers concerning the application of laws in Scheduled Areas.
  • Sixth Schedule: Provides autonomous district and regional councils in specified tribal areas of Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. These councils have legislative, administrative and, in specified matters, judicial and financial powers.

The distinction reflects different constitutional approaches to tribal self-governance, local autonomy and protection of customary institutions.

Seventh Schedule: Legislative Distribution

The Seventh Schedule contains three lists:

  • Union List: Subjects on which Parliament has exclusive legislative competence under the ordinary distribution of powers.
  • State List: Subjects ordinarily within the legislative competence of State legislatures.
  • Concurrent List: Subjects on which both Parliament and State legislatures may legislate, subject to constitutional rules governing repugnancy and priority.

Article 246 establishes the distribution of legislative powers with reference to these lists. Article 248 and Entry 97 of the Union List deal with residuary legislative powers. Articles 249, 250, 252 and 253 provide important circumstances in which Parliament may legislate on matters otherwise within the State List or exercise wider legislative competence.

Eighth Schedule: Languages

The Eighth Schedule currently recognises 22 languages. Its constitutional significance must be distinguished from the status of Hindi as the official language of the Union under Article 343 and the constitutional provisions concerning the use of English.

Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule does not, by itself, make a language an official language of the Union or of every State. The Schedule is relevant to the constitutional framework for language development and the provisions in Articles 344 and 351.

Ninth Schedule: Protection of Certain Laws

The Ninth Schedule was inserted by the Constitution (First Amendment) Act, 1951. It was initially intended to protect specified laws, particularly land reform legislation, from challenges under certain Fundamental Rights provisions through Article 31B.

Its protection is not absolute. In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested against the Basic Structure doctrine where they damage or destroy the basic structure through their impact on Fundamental Rights.

Tenth Schedule: Anti-Defection

The Tenth Schedule was inserted by the Constitution (Fifty-second Amendment) Act, 1985. It specifies circumstances in which Members of Parliament and State legislatures may be disqualified on grounds of defection.

The Constitution (Ninety-first Amendment) Act, 2003 removed the earlier protection for a split by one-third of a legislative party. A merger exception remains subject to the conditions in the Schedule, including the requirement concerning at least two-thirds of the legislature party.

The presiding officer of the House decides disqualification questions under the Schedule. In Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutional validity of the anti-defection framework while recognising judicial review of the presiding officer's decision on limited grounds.

Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules: Local Government

The Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules were inserted through the Seventy-third and Seventy-fourth Constitutional Amendments respectively. They provide indicative subject lists for rural and urban local government.

These lists do not automatically transfer every listed function to local bodies. The constitutional framework leaves the devolution of powers, authority and responsibilities to State legislatures through laws, subject to the relevant constitutional provisions.

9. Important Judicial Decisions and Constitutional Principles

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

The Supreme Court propounded the Basic Structure doctrine. Parliament's power to amend the Constitution under Article 368 does not extend to destroying its basic structure. This principle is relevant to amendments affecting Parts and Schedules.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

The Supreme Court distinguished laws placed in the Ninth Schedule before and after 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment. The ruling is an important step in understanding judicial scrutiny of Ninth Schedule laws.

Kihoto Hollohan v. Zachillhu (1992)

The Supreme Court upheld the anti-defection framework in the Tenth Schedule, while recognising judicial review of decisions made by the presiding officer on limited grounds.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are open to judicial review if their impact damages or destroys the Basic Structure of the Constitution.

Union of India v. Rajendra N. Shah (2021)

The Supreme Court invalidated the Ninety-seventh Amendment insofar as it related to co-operative societies operating within a State, because the required ratification by half the States had not been obtained. The amendment's provisions concerning multi-State co-operative societies and Union Territories were not invalidated on that ground.

Part VII and Part VIII Part VII is repealed; Part VIII continues to deal with Union Territories.
Fifth and Sixth Schedules The Fifth Schedule provides a framework for Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in specified States; the Sixth Schedule provides autonomous councils in specified tribal areas of four North-Eastern States.
Seventh and Eighth Schedules The Seventh distributes legislative subjects; the Eighth lists recognised languages.
Ninth and Tenth Schedules The Ninth concerns specified laws and constitutional protection; the Tenth concerns disqualification on grounds of defection.

Recognised language and official language Inclusion in the Eighth Schedule does not automatically confer official-language status at the Union or State level.

Ninth Schedule and immunity from judicial review Ninth Schedule inclusion does not provide absolute immunity from judicial review, particularly for post-24 April 1973 insertions that damage the Basic Structure.
Part IXB and State co-operative societies Following the 2021 Supreme Court judgment, Part IXB does not apply to co-operative societies operating solely within a State in the manner originally envisaged by the amendment.

11. Constitutional Significance and Critical Assessment

The Parts and Schedules together provide the organisational framework through which constitutional principles are translated into institutions, powers and procedures.

Advantages of the structure

  • Clarity: Subject-based organisation makes constitutional provisions easier to locate and interpret.
  • Institutional coordination: Related provisions across Parts and Schedules connect executive, legislative, judicial and administrative institutions.
  • Federal balance: The Seventh Schedule and special territorial arrangements accommodate the distribution of powers and India's regional diversity.
  • Social justice: Provisions concerning Scheduled Areas, representation, local government and recognised languages support constitutional commitments to inclusion.
  • Adaptability: The amendment process allows the constitutional framework to respond to changing institutional and social requirements.

Challenges and concerns

  • Frequent amendments can make the constitutional structure more complex and require careful attention to the current text.
  • Overlapping provisions across Parts and Schedules may require judicial interpretation.
  • Formal constitutional recognition of local government does not guarantee effective devolution of functions, funds and functionaries.
  • Special protections and institutional autonomy must be balanced with democratic accountability and constitutional rights.
  • Constitutional amendments remain subject to procedural requirements and the Basic Structure limitation.

12. Integrated Revision Framework

For effective revision, classify the Parts and Schedules by constitutional function rather than memorising them only as separate lists.

  • Territory and membership: Parts I and II; First and Fourth Schedules.
  • Rights and constitutional philosophy: Parts III, IV and IVA.
  • Institutions of government: Parts V, VI, VIII, XIV, XIVA and XV; Second and Third Schedules.
  • Federalism and legislative powers: Parts XI and XII; Seventh Schedule.
  • Tribal and local self-governance: Parts IX, IXA and X; Fifth, Sixth, Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules.
  • Representation and accountability: Part XVI and the Tenth Schedule.
  • Language and national integration: Part XVII and the Eighth Schedule.
  • Constitutional continuity and change: Parts XVIII, XIX, XX, XXI and XXII; Ninth Schedule and relevant amendment provisions.

For factual recall, remember the subject and Article range of each Part, the subject of each Schedule and the amendment that introduced important insertions. For conceptual understanding, connect each Schedule to its enabling Article, the relevant Part and the constitutional objective it serves.
