1. Meaning and Constitutional Philosophy

Fundamental Rights are constitutionally guaranteed rights contained in Part III of the Constitution (Articles 12–35). They protect individual liberty, equality and dignity, limit arbitrary State action and establish conditions necessary for democratic government.

They are called fundamental because they are essential to constitutional democracy and are enforceable through courts. They are not, however, unlimited. The Constitution permits carefully defined restrictions in specified circumstances, subject to constitutional safeguards and judicial review.

Fundamental Rights reflect several constitutional commitments:

  • Individual liberty: Protection against arbitrary interference with life, personal freedom and expression.
  • Equality: Prohibition of discriminatory State action and removal of specified forms of social inequality.
  • Human dignity: Protection of life, personal autonomy and conditions necessary for dignified existence.
  • Pluralism: Protection of religious, cultural and educational rights.
  • Constitutional accountability: Judicial remedies against violations of guaranteed rights.

2. Classification of Fundamental Rights

The Constitution originally recognised seven broad categories of Fundamental Rights. The Right to Property was removed from Part III by the Forty-fourth Amendment Act, 1978. The present framework is generally classified into six categories.

Category Articles Core protection
Right to Equality 14–18 Equality before law, non-discrimination, equality of opportunity, abolition of untouchability and titles.
Right to Freedom 19–22 Specified freedoms, protection in criminal matters, life and personal liberty, education and safeguards against arrest and detention.
Right against Exploitation 23–24 Prohibition of human trafficking, forced labour and specified forms of child labour.
Right to Freedom of Religion 25–28 Freedom of conscience, religious practice and management of religious affairs, subject to constitutional limitations.
Cultural and Educational Rights 29–30 Protection of cultural interests and minority educational institutions.
Right to Constitutional Remedies 32 Access to the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

Articles 31 and 31D have been omitted. Articles 31A, 31B and 31C remain important provisions concerning the relationship between certain laws and Fundamental Rights.

3. Article 12: Definition of the State

Article 12 defines the term "State" for the purposes of Part III. It includes:

  • The Government and Parliament of India.
  • The Government and Legislature of each State.
  • All local authorities within the territory of India.
  • Other authorities within the territory of India or under the control of the Government of India.

The definition is significant because many Fundamental Rights operate as limitations on State action. Determining whether an entity qualifies as "State" can therefore determine whether its actions are directly challengeable under relevant Fundamental Rights provisions.

Meaning of "other authorities"

The Supreme Court has interpreted "other authorities" broadly. Government ownership or funding alone is not always conclusive. The inquiry considers the nature and degree of governmental control, the functions performed, the origin of the body's authority and its institutional relationship with government.

  • Rajasthan State Electricity Board v. Mohan Lal (1967): Adopted a broad interpretation of "other authorities".
  • Sukhdev Singh v. Bhagatram (1975): Recognised statutory corporations as authorities within Article 12.
  • R.D. Shetty v. International Airport Authority of India (1979): Developed indicators for identifying instrumentalities or agencies of government.
  • Ajay Hasia v. Khalid Mujib (1981): Explained that the form of incorporation is not decisive; the substance of governmental control and institutional character matters.
  • Pradeep Kumar Biswas v. Indian Institute of Chemical Biology (2002): Emphasised whether the body is financially, functionally and administratively dominated by or under the control of government, with such control being particular to and pervasive over the body.

Private bodies are not automatically "State" under Article 12. Nevertheless, constitutional remedies may be available against certain private bodies performing public duties, depending on the right invoked and the nature of the action.

4. Article 13: Laws Inconsistent with Fundamental Rights

Article 13 establishes constitutional supremacy over ordinary law in the field of Fundamental Rights. It provides that laws inconsistent with or in derogation of Fundamental Rights are void to the extent of the inconsistency.

  • Article 13(1): Pre-Constitution laws inconsistent with Fundamental Rights become void to the extent of inconsistency after commencement of the Constitution.
  • Article 13(2): The State shall not make laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights; laws violating this prohibition are void to the extent of contravention.
  • Article 13(3): Defines "law" broadly to include ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs and usages having the force of law.
  • Article 13(4): Excludes constitutional amendments made under Article 368 from the operation of Article 13.

Although constitutional amendments are excluded from Article 13 by clause (4), they remain subject to the Basic Structure doctrine developed by the Supreme Court.

Important doctrines

  • Doctrine of severability: If an unconstitutional part of a law can be separated from the valid remainder, only the offending part need be invalidated.
  • Doctrine of eclipse: A pre-Constitution law inconsistent with a Fundamental Right may become inoperative to the extent of inconsistency rather than being erased for all purposes. Its operation may revive if the constitutional inconsistency is removed, subject to the legal context.
  • Prospective overruling: A court may declare a legal position while limiting the retrospective effect of its ruling. The doctrine was applied by the Supreme Court in Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967).

5. Right to Equality: Articles 14–18

Article 14: Equality before Law and Equal Protection of Laws

Article 14 guarantees equality before the law and equal protection of the laws within the territory of India. It is available to every person, not merely citizens.

  • Equality before law: A negative concept associated with the absence of arbitrary special privilege and equal subjection to ordinary law.
  • Equal protection of laws: A positive concept requiring equal treatment of persons similarly situated and permitting reasonable classification.

Article 14 does not prohibit every classification. A classification is constitutionally permissible when it satisfies two requirements:

  1. It is based on an intelligible differentia distinguishing persons or things grouped together from those left out.
  2. The differentia has a rational nexus with the objective sought to be achieved by the law.

Modern Article 14 jurisprudence also prohibits manifest arbitrariness in State action and legislation.

Important decisions:

  • State of West Bengal v. Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952): Examined the constitutional limits on arbitrary classification.
  • E.P. Royappa v. State of Tamil Nadu (1974): Connected equality with the prohibition of arbitrariness.
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Strengthened the requirement that State action affecting liberty must satisfy fairness and non-arbitrariness.
  • Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): Recognised manifest arbitrariness as a ground for invalidating legislation under Article 14.

Article 15: Prohibition of Discrimination

Article 15 prohibits specified forms of discrimination against citizens on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth.

  • Article 15(1): Prohibits the State from discriminating against citizens on the listed grounds.
  • Article 15(2): Prohibits specified forms of disability, restriction or condition concerning access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment and certain public facilities. It also addresses access to wells, tanks, bathing ghats, roads and places of public resort maintained wholly or partly by State funds or dedicated to public use.
  • Article 15(3): Permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4): Permits special provisions for the advancement of socially and educationally backward classes of citizens and for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 15(5): Enables special provisions for admission of socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes to educational institutions, including private aided and unaided institutions, subject to the exclusion of minority educational institutions protected by Article 30(1).
  • Article 15(6): Enables special provisions for economically weaker sections, including reservation in educational admissions, subject to the constitutional framework and the exclusion specified for minority educational institutions under Article 30(1).

Article 15(4) and 15(5) provide constitutional bases for specified affirmative action measures. Article 15(6), inserted by the One Hundred and Third Amendment, provides for EWS-related measures.

Article 16: Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

Article 16 guarantees equality of opportunity to citizens in matters relating to employment or appointment to an office under the State.

  • Article 16(1): Guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment.
  • Article 16(2): Prohibits discrimination on specified grounds, including religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth and residence.
  • Article 16(3): Allows Parliament to prescribe residence requirements for certain public employment.
  • Article 16(4): Permits reservation in appointments or posts for backward classes not adequately represented in State services, as constitutionally understood.
  • Article 16(4A): Permits reservation in promotion, with consequential seniority, for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes under specified conditions.
  • Article 16(4B): Allows unfilled reserved vacancies to be treated as a separate class for determining the ceiling on reservations in a subsequent year, subject to the constitutional provision.
  • Article 16(5): Preserves certain religious or denominational qualifications for offices connected with religious institutions.
  • Article 16(6): Enables reservation in appointments or posts for economically weaker sections, subject to the constitutional ceiling and conditions.

Article 16(4) is generally treated as an enabling provision, not an unconditional constitutional command to provide reservation in every service or post.

Article 17: Abolition of Untouchability

Article 17 abolishes untouchability and forbids its practice in any form. Enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable in accordance with law.

Parliament has enacted laws to give effect to this provision, including:

  • The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955.
  • The Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

Article 17 is significant because it addresses a deeply rooted social practice and is not confined to prohibiting discriminatory action by the State alone.

Article 18: Abolition of Titles

  • The State shall not confer titles, except military and academic distinctions.
  • Citizens of India shall not accept titles from foreign States.
  • Specified constitutional office-holders and persons holding offices of profit or trust under the State are subject to restrictions on accepting foreign titles, presents, emoluments or offices from foreign States without the President's consent.

National awards such as the Bharat Ratna and Padma awards are not titles under Article 18, provided they are not used as prefixes or suffixes to the recipient's name. This principle was affirmed in Balaji Raghavan v. Union of India (1996).

6. Right to Freedom: Articles 19–22

Article 19: Six Freedoms

Article 19 guarantees six freedoms to citizens of India. The original right to acquire, hold and dispose of property under Article 19(1)(f) was removed by the Forty-fourth Amendment Act, 1978.

  1. Freedom of speech and expression — Article 19(1)(a).
  2. Freedom to assemble peaceably and without arms — Article 19(1)(b).
  3. Freedom to form associations, unions or co-operative societies — Article 19(1)(c).
  4. Freedom to move freely throughout the territory of India — Article 19(1)(d).
  5. Freedom to reside and settle in any part of the territory of India — Article 19(1)(e).
  6. Freedom to practise any profession, or to carry on any occupation, trade or business — Article 19(1)(g).

These freedoms are not absolute. The Constitution specifies grounds on which reasonable restrictions may be imposed by law.

Freedom Restriction provision Permitted grounds
Speech and expression Article 19(2) Sovereignty and integrity of India; security of the State; friendly relations with foreign States; public order; decency or morality; contempt of court; defamation; and incitement to an offence.
Peaceful assembly Article 19(3) Sovereignty and integrity of India; public order.
Associations, unions and co-operative societies Article 19(4) Sovereignty and integrity of India; public order; morality.
Movement and residence Article 19(5) Interests of the general public; protection of the interests of any Scheduled Tribe.
Profession, occupation, trade or business Article 19(6) Interests of the general public; professional or technical qualifications; State monopoly in specified activities.

Freedom of Speech and Expression

Article 19(1)(a) protects the ability to express opinions and communicate ideas. Judicial interpretation has recognised dimensions such as freedom of the press, artistic expression, the right to receive information and certain forms of commercial expression.

  • Romesh Thappar v. State of Madras (1950): Emphasised the importance of freedom of political discussion and the press.
  • Bennett Coleman & Co. v. Union of India (1973): Recognised the relationship between press freedom and the right to circulate information.
  • Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015): Struck down Section 66A of the Information Technology Act, 2000 for violating Article 19(1)(a), and explained the distinction between discussion, advocacy and incitement.
  • Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020): Recognised that freedom of speech and expression and freedom to practise a profession through the internet receive constitutional protection; restrictions on internet services must satisfy constitutional requirements.

Article 20: Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

Article 20 provides three safeguards to persons accused or convicted of offences:

  • Article 20(1): Protection against ex post facto criminal laws. A person cannot be convicted for an act that was not an offence when committed, nor subjected to a greater penalty than the one applicable at the time of commission.
  • Article 20(2): Protection against double jeopardy. No person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.
  • Article 20(3): Protection against self-incrimination. No person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against themselves.

Article 20 protections are available to persons, not only citizens. The protection against self-incrimination concerns compelled testimonial evidence; its application to physical evidence and investigative procedures must be assessed in light of judicial interpretation.

Article 21: Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

Article 21 provides that no person shall be deprived of life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.

In Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978), the Supreme Court held that the procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty must be just, fair and reasonable, and not arbitrary. It also developed an integrated reading of Articles 14, 19 and 21.

Judicial interpretation has expanded Article 21 to include several dimensions of dignified life, subject to the facts and legal context of each case.

  • Right to live with human dignity.
  • Right to privacy.
  • Right to livelihood, within the limits recognised by law.
  • Right to a speedy trial.
  • Right to legal aid in appropriate circumstances.
  • Right to a clean and healthy environment.
  • Protection against custodial violence and arbitrary State action.
  • Right to die with dignity in the limited context of recognised end-of-life safeguards.

Important decisions:

  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): Recognised the importance of speedy trial.
  • Olga Tellis v. Bombay Municipal Corporation (1985): Recognised livelihood as an aspect of the right to life, while not establishing an unrestricted right to occupy public property.
  • Unni Krishnan v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993): Contributed to the constitutional development of the right to education, later expressly addressed by Article 21A.
  • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017): Recognised privacy as a Fundamental Right protected by the Constitution.
  • Common Cause v. Union of India (2018): Recognised the right to die with dignity in the context of passive euthanasia and advance medical directives, subject to safeguards.

Article 21A: Right to Education

Article 21A, inserted by the Eighty-sixth Amendment Act, 2002, requires the State to provide free and compulsory education to all children between six and fourteen years of age in such manner as the State may, by law, determine.

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 provides the principal statutory framework for implementing this right.

The constitutional education framework should be read with:

  • Article 21A: Free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen.
  • Article 45: Directive Principle concerning early childhood care and education for children below six years.
  • Article 51A(k): Fundamental Duty of a parent or guardian to provide opportunities for education to a child or ward between six and fourteen years.

Article 22: Protection against Arrest and Detention

Article 22 provides safeguards concerning arrest and detention. Its protections differ between ordinary arrest and preventive detention.

Safeguards for a person arrested under ordinary criminal law include:

  • Being informed, as soon as may be, of the grounds of arrest.
  • The right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of choice.
  • Production before the nearest magistrate within twenty-four hours of arrest, excluding the time necessary for the journey.
  • No detention beyond the twenty-four-hour period without the authority of a magistrate.

These safeguards do not apply in the same manner to enemy aliens or persons detained under a law providing for preventive detention, as specified in Article 22.

Preventive detention: The Constitution permits preventive detention subject to safeguards, including communication of grounds and an opportunity to make a representation. The constitutional text also provides for review by an Advisory Board in specified circumstances and contains rules concerning detention periods and parliamentary legislation.

Preventive detention is distinct from punitive detention: it is intended to prevent anticipated harmful conduct rather than punish a person for an offence already committed. Its use raises serious concerns regarding liberty, procedural fairness and executive accountability.

7. Right against Exploitation: Articles 23–24

Article 23: Prohibition of Human Trafficking and Forced Labour

Article 23 prohibits traffic in human beings, begar and other similar forms of forced labour. Contravention is an offence punishable in accordance with law.

The prohibition applies against the State and private individuals. The State may impose compulsory service for public purposes, but in doing so it cannot discriminate only on grounds of religion, race, caste or class.

In People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982), the Supreme Court held that payment below the statutory minimum wage may amount to forced labour under Article 23 where the labour is extracted through economic compulsion.

Article 24: Prohibition of Child Labour in Specified Employment

Article 24 prohibits the employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines or any other hazardous employment.

The constitutional protection is supplemented by the Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended, and other child-protection laws.

Article 24 should be read with Article 21A, Article 39(e) and (f), and Article 45 to understand the broader constitutional commitment to child welfare and education.

8. Right to Freedom of Religion: Articles 25–28

Article 25: Freedom of Conscience and Free Profession, Practice and Propagation of Religion

Article 25 protects freedom of conscience and the right freely to profess, practise and propagate religion. It is available to all persons and is subject to public order, morality, health and the other provisions of Part III.

The State may regulate secular activities associated with religious practice and enact laws for social welfare and reform. Article 25 also contains specific constitutional explanations concerning the wearing and carrying of kirpans and the inclusion of Sikhs within the reference to Hindus for the limited purpose of opening Hindu religious institutions of a public character to all classes and sections.

Article 26: Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

Every religious denomination or section thereof has the right, subject to public order, morality and health, to:

  • Establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.
  • Manage its own affairs in matters of religion.
  • Own and acquire movable and immovable property.
  • Administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27: Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of a Particular Religion

No person shall be compelled to pay taxes whose proceeds are specifically appropriated for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

Article 28: Religious Instruction in Educational Institutions

  • No religious instruction shall be provided in an educational institution wholly maintained out of State funds.
  • This prohibition does not apply to an institution administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction.
  • A person attending an educational institution recognised by the State or receiving State aid cannot be required to take part in religious instruction or worship without consent; for a minor, the guardian's consent is required.

Secularism and the State

Indian secularism does not require the State to be indifferent to every religious practice. The Constitution protects religious freedom while permitting regulation of secular activities, social reform and measures necessary to maintain public order, morality and health.

In S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994), the Supreme Court affirmed secularism as a basic feature of the Constitution.

9. Cultural and Educational Rights: Articles 29–30

Article 29: Protection of Interests of Minorities

Article 29(1) gives any section of citizens residing in the territory of India or any part thereof, having a distinct language, script or culture of its own, the right to conserve it.

Article 29(2) prohibits denial of admission to any citizen into an educational institution maintained by the State or receiving aid out of State funds on grounds only of religion, race, caste or language.

Article 29 is not confined exclusively to religious or linguistic minorities. Its protection under clause (1) extends to any qualifying section of citizens.

Article 30: Rights of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions

All religious and linguistic minorities have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.

  • Article 30(1) protects the institutional autonomy of religious and linguistic minorities.
  • Article 30(1A) protects the right to compensation where property of a minority educational institution is compulsorily acquired, subject to the constitutional conditions.
  • Article 30(2) prohibits discrimination in granting aid to educational institutions on the ground that an institution is under the management of a religious or linguistic minority.

The right to administer does not mean a right to maladminister. Reasonable regulations concerning academic standards, qualifications, public order and educational excellence may be permissible if they do not destroy the institution's minority character or essential autonomy.

Important decisions:

  • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Addressed the relationship between Fundamental Rights and communal allocation in educational admissions and led to the First Amendment's insertion of Article 15(4).
  • St. Xavier's College v. State of Gujarat (1974): Examined minority educational autonomy and permissible regulation.
  • T.M.A. Pai Foundation v. State of Karnataka (2002): Explained the scope of minority educational rights and institutional autonomy.
  • P.A. Inamdar v. State of Maharashtra (2005): Considered State regulation and admissions in private unaided professional educational institutions.

10. Article 32: Right to Constitutional Remedies

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including writs in the nature of habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, quo warranto and certiorari.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the "heart and soul" of the Constitution because rights would be ineffective without remedies for their enforcement.

Five Constitutional Writs

Writ Meaning and purpose Typical use
Habeas Corpus "To have the body"; requires production of a detained person and examination of the legality of detention. Unlawful detention or deprivation of personal liberty.
Mandamus "We command"; directs a public authority to perform a public or legal duty. Failure or refusal to perform a legally required public duty.
Prohibition Prevents a subordinate court or tribunal from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction. Excess or absence of jurisdiction in proceedings that are still pending.
Certiorari Quashes an order or decision of a subordinate court, tribunal or authority where legal grounds for intervention exist. Jurisdictional error, violation of natural justice or an error of law apparent on the record, within the applicable doctrine.
Quo Warranto "By what authority"; questions the legal authority of a person to hold a public office. Occupation of a substantive public office contrary to legal requirements.

Article 32 and Article 226: Key Differences

Basis Article 32 Article 226
Issuing authority Supreme Court High Courts
Purpose Enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Enforcement of Fundamental Rights and for any other purpose.
Nature of right The right to move the Supreme Court under Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right. Constitutional power of the High Court; not itself a Fundamental Right.
Territorial reach Supreme Court exercises jurisdiction under the Constitution. High Court jurisdiction is governed by territorial limits and the cause-of-action rule in Article 226(2).
Scope Limited to enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Wider subject matter, including other legal rights.

Article 32 is an important constitutional guarantee, but the Supreme Court's jurisdiction is exercised according to established procedural and judicial principles. Article 226 gives High Courts a wider remedial jurisdiction, though relief remains subject to judicial discretion and recognised limitations.

11. Articles 31A, 31B and 31C: Relationship with Certain Laws

Article 31A

Article 31A protects specified categories of laws from challenge on the grounds of inconsistency with Articles 14 and 19, subject to the conditions and exceptions contained in the provision. It principally concerns matters such as acquisition of estates, taking over management of property, amalgamation of corporations and specified modifications of rights in certain circumstances.

The protection is not an unrestricted immunity for every law described as a reform measure. The law must fall within the constitutional categories and satisfy the applicable conditions.

Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule

Article 31B gives protection to laws specified in the Ninth Schedule from being deemed void on the ground that they are inconsistent with or take away or abridge rights conferred by Part III, subject to the Basic Structure doctrine as interpreted by the Supreme Court.

The Ninth Schedule was inserted by the First Amendment Act, 1951. Its protection is not absolute, particularly for laws inserted after the Basic Structure decision in 1973.

Article 31C

Article 31C concerns laws giving effect to specified Directive Principles. Its present valid protection is connected with laws implementing Article 39(b) and Article 39(c), subject to the judicial interpretation of the provision.

The Forty-second Amendment attempted to extend Article 31C protection to all Directive Principles. In Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980), the Supreme Court invalidated that extension. The balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles is part of the Constitution's basic structure.

13. Fundamental Rights During Emergencies

The Constitution provides special rules concerning Fundamental Rights during a Proclamation of Emergency.

Article 358: Suspension of Article 19

Article 358 deals with the effect of a Proclamation of Emergency on Article 19. Following the Forty-fourth Amendment, the automatic operation of Article 358 is limited to an Emergency declared on the grounds of war or external aggression. It does not apply to an Emergency declared solely on the ground of armed rebellion.

The constitutional protection also includes conditions concerning laws and executive action claiming the benefit of Article 358.

Article 359: Suspension of the Right to Move Courts for Specified Rights

Under Article 359, the President may, by order, suspend the right to move any court for enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights during the Emergency, subject to constitutional conditions.

Articles 20 and 21 cannot be included in such an order following the Forty-fourth Amendment.

Important distinction

  • Article 358 concerns Article 19 and operates in the constitutionally specified Emergency circumstances.
  • Article 359 concerns the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights through courts under a Presidential order.
  • Neither provision should be understood as an unlimited suspension of the entire Fundamental Rights chapter.

The Forty-fourth Amendment strengthened safeguards after the Emergency experience of 1975–1977, particularly by protecting Articles 20 and 21 from suspension of the right to move courts under Article 359.

14. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles

Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy are complementary parts of the constitutional framework. Fundamental Rights are generally enforceable through courts, while Directive Principles are non-justiciable but fundamental in the governance of the country under Article 37.

Basis Fundamental Rights Directive Principles
Constitutional location Part III Part IV
Articles 12–35 36–51
Enforceability Generally enforceable through courts. Not enforceable by courts under Article 37.
Primary emphasis Individual liberty, equality and protection against specified forms of State action. Social and economic transformation and welfare-oriented governance.
Role of the State Often restrains arbitrary State action and also imposes positive obligations in specified contexts. Guides the State in law-making and policy.

Constitutional interpretation has moved away from treating the two Parts as inherently antagonistic.

  • State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Initially emphasised the primacy of Fundamental Rights in the event of direct conflict, leading to constitutional amendment.
  • Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973): Established the Basic Structure doctrine and examined the constitutional relationship between rights and reform measures.
  • Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980): Held that harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles form part of the Basic Structure.

15. Fundamental Rights and Fundamental Duties

Fundamental Rights protect individual freedoms and constitutional entitlements. Fundamental Duties under Article 51A identify responsibilities expected of citizens. They are distinct but complementary aspects of constitutional citizenship.

  • Rights protect individuals against specified forms of State interference and provide conditions for dignified life.
  • Duties encourage respect for constitutional values, public property, the environment and the spirit of common citizenship.
  • Fundamental Duties are not generally directly enforceable in the same manner as Fundamental Rights, but may inform legislation and judicial interpretation.

The relationship should not be used to suggest that enjoyment of Fundamental Rights is generally conditional upon performance of Fundamental Duties.

17. Right to Property: From Fundamental Right to Constitutional Right

The original Constitution recognised the right to property as a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31. The Forty-fourth Amendment Act, 1978 removed these provisions from Part III and inserted Article 300A in Part XII.

Article 300A provides that no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. Consequently:

  • Right to property is no longer a Fundamental Right.
  • It remains a constitutional right under Article 300A.
  • It is not among the rights protected by Article 32 as a Fundamental Right, although appropriate legal and constitutional remedies may be available, including under Article 226.
  • Deprivation of property must have authority of law and satisfy applicable constitutional requirements.

The change reflected the constitutional effort to reconcile individual property interests with land reform, redistribution and social justice.

18. Contemporary Issues and Challenges

  • Freedom of expression: Balancing speech with public order, national security, dignity and protection against incitement, while preventing disproportionate restrictions.
  • Privacy and digital rights: Protecting personal data and autonomy in an environment of digital services, surveillance and data processing.
  • Preventive detention: Ensuring that security-related detention powers are exercised with procedural safeguards and effective review.
  • Equality and affirmative action: Reconciling formal equality, substantive equality, representation and constitutional limits on classification.
  • Religious freedom: Balancing individual conscience, denominational rights, public order and social reform.
  • Minority educational autonomy: Reconciling institutional autonomy with educational standards, transparency and regulation.
  • Access to justice: Addressing legal aid, delay, affordability and barriers to effective constitutional remedies.
  • Rights and technology: Applying constitutional safeguards to internet restrictions, algorithmic decisions and emerging forms of State power.

These issues require analysis of the relevant constitutional text, judicial doctrine, legitimate State objectives, proportionality and available remedies rather than a simplistic assumption that every right is absolute.

19. Integrated Revision Framework

For systematic revision, organise Fundamental Rights around five questions:

  1. Who is protected? Distinguish rights available to citizens from those available to all persons.
  2. Against whom is the right enforceable? Examine Article 12, the nature of State action and the limited circumstances in which rights have horizontal application.
  3. What is the scope of the right? Read the text of the relevant Article alongside judicial interpretation.
  4. What restrictions are permitted? Identify the constitutional grounds, procedural requirements and standards of judicial review.
  5. What remedy is available? Connect the right with Articles 32 and 226 and the relevant writ jurisdiction.

For factual recall, remember the Article ranges, categories of rights, restriction clauses, constitutional amendments and landmark judgments. For conceptual understanding, connect equality with affirmative action, liberty with due process, religion with secularism, and rights with constitutional remedies.
