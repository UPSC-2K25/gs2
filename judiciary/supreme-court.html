1. Constitutional Position

The Supreme Court of India is the apex judicial institution in the country. It is the final interpreter of the Constitution and the highest court of appeal. It protects Fundamental Rights, adjudicates disputes and exercises judicial review over legislative and executive action.

Part V, Chapter IV of the Constitution, Articles 124–147, deals with the Union Judiciary. The Supreme Court commenced functioning on 28 January 1950, replacing the Federal Court established under the Government of India Act, 1935.

Its constitutional roles include:

  • Guardian and interpreter of the Constitution.
  • Protector of Fundamental Rights.
  • Final court of appeal.
  • Adjudicator of specified federal disputes.
  • Institution exercising judicial review.
  • Authority whose declared law binds all courts in India under Article 141.

3. Composition and Strength

Article 124 provides for a Supreme Court consisting of the Chief Justice of India and such number of other judges as Parliament may prescribe by law.

The sanctioned strength is currently 34 judges, including the Chief Justice of India. Parliament has increased the strength over time through amendments to the Supreme Court (Number of Judges) Act, 1956.

The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed numerical strength. The statutory strength can be changed by Parliament.

4. Appointment of Judges

Under Article 124(2), judges of the Supreme Court are formally appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal after constitutionally prescribed consultation.

The collegium system, developed through judicial interpretation, governs the recommendation process for appointments and transfers.

Important stages in its evolution:

  • First Judges Case — S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): Initially gave primacy to the Executive in judicial appointments.
  • Second Judges Case — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (1993): Established judicial primacy and the collegium system.
  • Third Judges Case — Presidential Reference (1998): Clarified the composition and consultation process of the collegium.
  • Fourth Judges Case — Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India (2015): Invalidated the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the National Judicial Appointments Commission framework.

For Supreme Court appointments, the collegium consists of the Chief Justice of India and the four senior-most puisne judges of the Supreme Court. The recommendation process is governed by the applicable collegium procedure and Memorandum of Procedure.

5. Qualifications of Judges

Under Article 124(3), a person must:

  • Be a citizen of India; and
  • Have been a High Court judge for at least five years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years; or
  • Be, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.

The distinguished jurist qualification has not yet been used for an appointment to the Supreme Court.

6. Oath and Tenure

A Supreme Court judge takes the oath or affirmation prescribed in the Third Schedule before entering office. The oath includes upholding the Constitution and laws, performing duties without fear or favour, affection or ill-will, and maintaining the sovereignty and integrity of India.

A Supreme Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 65 years.

A judge may resign by writing addressed to the President. A judge may also be removed through the constitutional procedure under Article 124(4).

7. Removal of Judges

A Supreme Court judge may be removed only by an order of the President after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

The address must be presented to the President in the same session of Parliament. The permissible grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides the statutory procedure for investigation of the charges.

Broad stages include:

  1. A removal motion is given notice in either House.
  2. The Speaker or Chairman decides whether to admit it.
  3. If admitted, a three-member inquiry committee is constituted under the statutory procedure.
  4. The committee investigates the charges and submits its findings.
  5. If the committee finds the judge guilty of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, the motion is taken up for consideration by Parliament.
  6. Both Houses must pass the address with the constitutionally prescribed special majority in the same session.
  7. The President issues the removal order.

The constitutional process is commonly called impeachment in public discourse, although the Constitution uses the term removal for Supreme Court judges.

8. Independence of the Supreme Court

Judicial independence is essential to constitutional government. The Constitution provides safeguards against improper interference by the Legislature and Executive.

  • Security of tenure until the age of 65 years.
  • Removal only through the difficult constitutional procedure.
  • Salaries and allowances charged on the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Service conditions cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment, except as constitutionally permitted.
  • Restrictions on parliamentary discussion of judicial conduct under Article 121, except upon a removal motion.
  • Power to punish for contempt as a Court of Record under Article 129.
  • Separation of the judiciary from the executive as a constitutional objective under Article 50.
  • Authority to regulate its own procedure under Article 145.

10. Original Jurisdiction — Article 131

Article 131 gives the Supreme Court exclusive original jurisdiction in specified disputes involving legal rights between:

  • The Government of India and one or more States.
  • The Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other.
  • Two or more States.

The dispute must involve a question, whether of law or fact, on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

Article 131 does not extend to every dispute between governments. It contains constitutional exclusions, including disputes arising out of certain pre-Constitution treaties, agreements and similar instruments.

Parliament may also exclude the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in specified matters under the constitutional framework.

11. Writ Jurisdiction — Article 32

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described it as the heart and soul of the Constitution.

The Supreme Court may issue five types of writs:

  • Habeas Corpus: Secures release from unlawful detention.
  • Mandamus: Directs a public authority to perform a public or legal duty.
  • Prohibition: Prevents a lower court or tribunal from proceeding beyond its jurisdiction.
  • Certiorari: Quashes an order of a lower court or tribunal for jurisdictional or other recognised legal error.
  • Quo Warranto: Challenges the legal authority of a person to hold a public office.

Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right. Article 226 gives High Courts a wider writ jurisdiction, extending to Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.

12. Appellate Jurisdiction

The Supreme Court is the highest appellate court in India. Its appellate jurisdiction is exercised under Articles 132, 133 and 134, along with Article 136.

  • Article 132: Appeals involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, subject to the constitutional requirements.
  • Article 133: Civil appeals where the High Court certifies that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance requiring decision by the Supreme Court.
  • Article 134: Criminal appeals in constitutionally specified circumstances.
  • Article 134A: Certificate for appeal to the Supreme Court.
  • Article 136: Discretionary special leave to appeal from judgments, decrees, determinations, sentences or orders of courts and tribunals, subject to constitutional exclusions.

13. Special Leave Petition — Article 136

Article 136 empowers the Supreme Court, in its discretion, to grant special leave to appeal from a judgment, decree, determination, sentence or order passed by a court or tribunal in India.

It is an exceptional discretionary jurisdiction, not an ordinary appeal available as of right. The Court may intervene where substantial injustice, serious legal error or exceptional circumstances justify its intervention.

Article 136 excludes courts or tribunals constituted by or under laws relating to the Armed Forces.

14. Review and Curative Jurisdiction

Article 137 authorises the Supreme Court to review its judgments and orders, subject to laws made by Parliament and rules under Article 145.

Review is narrow and is not an opportunity for a routine rehearing merely because a party disagrees with the outcome.

In Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002), the Supreme Court recognised the curative petition as an exceptional remedy after dismissal of a review petition, to prevent abuse of process and cure a gross miscarriage of justice in narrowly defined circumstances.

15. Advisory Jurisdiction — Article 143

Article 143 empowers the President to refer questions of law or fact of public importance to the Supreme Court for its advisory opinion.

It contains two distinct provisions:

  • Article 143(1): The President may refer a question of law or fact of public importance. The Court may report its opinion after hearing as it considers appropriate.
  • Article 143(2): The President may refer specified disputes arising out of pre-Constitution treaties, agreements and similar instruments. The Court shall report its opinion after hearing as it considers appropriate.

The opinion under Article 143(1) is advisory and is not a judgment binding in the same manner as an ordinary adjudication. It nevertheless carries substantial constitutional and persuasive authority.

Examples include the In re Berubari Union (1960) and the Special Reference No. 1 of 1993 concerning judicial appointments.

16. Article 141 — Binding Nature of Law

Article 141 provides that the law declared by the Supreme Court is binding on all courts within the territory of India.

The binding force attaches to the legal principle forming the ratio decidendi of a decision, read in the context of the judgment. Not every observation or factual statement is automatically binding law.

This provision promotes legal uniformity and certainty throughout the judicial system.

17. Article 142 — Complete Justice

Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it.

It is an important constitutional power that enables the Court to provide effective remedies where ordinary legal procedures may be inadequate.

However, Article 142 is not a licence to disregard substantive law or exercise unrestricted legislative authority. Its use must remain consistent with the Constitution and the basic legal framework.

18. Court of Record — Article 129

Article 129 declares the Supreme Court to be a Court of Record with all the powers of such a court, including the power to punish for contempt of itself.

Its significance includes:

  • Judicial records are preserved as authoritative records.
  • Its records have evidentiary and precedential significance.
  • It possesses contempt jurisdiction to protect the administration of justice and the authority of the Court.

19. Seat of the Supreme Court — Article 130

Article 130 provides that the Supreme Court shall sit in Delhi or in such other place or places as the Chief Justice of India may, with the approval of the President, appoint from time to time.

The provision allows constitutional flexibility regarding the location of sittings. The principal seat remains in New Delhi.

20. Constitution Benches

Article 145(3) requires a minimum of five judges to hear a case involving a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, or a reference under Article 143.

Such benches are commonly called Constitution Benches. Larger benches may be constituted for important constitutional questions.

Their decisions are especially significant for constitutional interpretation and institutional development.

21. Public Interest Litigation

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) enables courts to consider genuine public causes and, in appropriate circumstances, relax traditional standing requirements.

It has facilitated access to justice for disadvantaged groups, prisoners, workers and persons unable to approach courts effectively.

PIL is not a separate constitutional jurisdiction. It operates through existing judicial powers, including Articles 32 and 226.

Important concerns include publicity-oriented litigation, private disputes disguised as public causes, judicial workload and the need to respect institutional boundaries.

22. Judicial Administration and Registry

Article 146 deals with officers and servants of the Supreme Court and its expenses.

The Registry supports the judicial institution through filing, listing, record management, procedural scrutiny and administrative functions. The Secretary-General and other Registry officials perform responsibilities under the applicable rules and administrative structure.

Article 145 empowers the Supreme Court to make rules regulating its practice and procedure, subject to the Constitution and applicable law.

23. Supreme Court and Federalism

The Supreme Court performs a major role in maintaining the constitutional distribution of powers between the Union and States.

  • Article 131 provides original jurisdiction in specified federal disputes.
  • Articles 245–255 regulate legislative relations.
  • Article 262 provides for adjudication of specified inter-State river water disputes and permits Parliament to exclude court jurisdiction in the prescribed manner.
  • Article 263 provides for the establishment of an Inter-State Council.
  • Judicial review safeguards constitutional limits on Union and State action.

25. Challenges Facing the Supreme Court

  • Case pendency: A large volume of pending cases affects timely justice.
  • Access barriers: Geographic, financial and linguistic barriers affect litigants.
  • Appointment delays: Delays in appointments and vacancies can affect judicial capacity.
  • Concentration of litigation: The central location of the Court creates access concerns for litigants from distant regions.
  • Constitutional case delays: Important constitutional questions may take substantial time to reach final adjudication.
  • Transparency: Appointment processes and institutional administration require public confidence.
  • Workload management: The mix of constitutional, civil, criminal and special leave matters creates competing demands.
  • Judicial accountability: Independence must be balanced with ethical conduct and institutional transparency.
  • Implementation of judgments: Effective justice depends on compliance by public authorities and other institutions.

26. Reform Priorities

  • Improve case management and listing systems.
  • Strengthen research and judicial support infrastructure.
  • Improve transparency and predictability in judicial appointments.
  • Address vacancies through timely and effective processes.
  • Expand technology-enabled access while preserving open justice.
  • Improve translation and multilingual access to judgments.
  • Consider institutional arrangements for regional access to the Supreme Court, consistent with Article 130 and constitutional requirements.
  • Strengthen alternative dispute resolution and reduce avoidable litigation.
  • Develop clear mechanisms for prioritising urgent constitutional and public importance cases.
  • Strengthen judicial ethics, accountability and public confidence.

27. Important Distinctions

  • Original vs appellate jurisdiction: Original jurisdiction begins a specified dispute before the Supreme Court; appellate jurisdiction reviews decisions of lower courts.
  • Article 32 vs Article 226: Article 32 is for enforcement of Fundamental Rights; Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Article 136 vs ordinary appeal: Special leave is discretionary and not an appeal available as of right.
  • Review vs curative petition: Review is under Article 137; curative jurisdiction is an exceptional judicially evolved remedy after review.
  • Article 141 vs Article 142: Article 141 concerns binding law declared by the Supreme Court; Article 142 concerns orders necessary for complete justice in a pending cause or matter.
  • Article 143 vs Article 131: Article 143 is advisory; Article 131 adjudicates specified federal disputes.
  • Impeachment vs removal: The Constitution prescribes a removal process for Supreme Court judges; impeachment is a common but technically imprecise description.

28. Integrated Understanding

The Supreme Court is central to constitutional governance because it combines final appellate authority, constitutional interpretation, Fundamental Rights enforcement and federal dispute resolution.

Its legitimacy depends on independence, impartiality, accessibility, institutional accountability and timely justice. The Court must protect constitutional supremacy while respecting the legitimate functions of the Legislature and Executive.
