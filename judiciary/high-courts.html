1. Constitutional Position

High Courts are the principal constitutional courts at the State level. They form an essential part of India's integrated judicial system and exercise civil, criminal, constitutional and supervisory jurisdiction.

Part VI, Chapter V of the Constitution, Articles 214–231, deals with High Courts in the States. A High Court is a Court of Record under Article 215 and has powers to issue writs under Article 226.

Their constitutional functions include:

  • Enforcement of Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Judicial review of State legislation and executive action.
  • Superintendence over courts and tribunals within their territorial jurisdiction, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Appellate and original adjudication under the Constitution and applicable laws.
  • Protection of the rule of law and constitutional governance at the State level.

3. Establishment and Organisation

Article 214 provides that there shall be a High Court for each State. However, Parliament may establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and Union Territories under Article 231.

Article 216 provides that every High Court shall consist of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time deem necessary to appoint.

The Constitution does not prescribe a fixed numerical strength for each High Court. The sanctioned strength is determined through the applicable institutional and administrative process.

High Courts exercise jurisdiction over their respective territories, which may include one or more States or Union Territories.

4. Appointment of High Court Judges

Under Article 217, every High Court judge is appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal after consultation with the Chief Justice of India, the Governor of the State and, in the case of appointment of a judge other than the Chief Justice, the Chief Justice of that High Court.

The collegium system, developed through judicial interpretation, governs recommendations for appointments and transfers.

  • For appointment of a High Court judge, the proposal is initiated by the Chief Justice of the concerned High Court in consultation with the two senior-most puisne judges of that High Court.
  • The recommendation is considered through the Union Government and the Supreme Court collegium process.
  • The Supreme Court collegium for High Court appointments consists of the Chief Justice of India and the two senior-most puisne judges of the Supreme Court.
  • The President makes the formal appointment under Article 217.

The Governor is consulted as constitutionally required, but the collegium system gives primacy to the judiciary in the recommendation process.

5. Qualifications of High Court Judges

Article 217(2) requires a person to:

  • Be a citizen of India; and
  • Have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court, or of two or more such courts in succession, for at least ten years.

The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age for appointment as a High Court judge.

6. Oath, Tenure and Retirement

Under Article 219, a High Court judge takes the prescribed oath or affirmation before the Governor of the State or a person appointed by the Governor.

A High Court judge holds office until attaining the age of 62 years.

A judge may resign by writing addressed to the President. A judge may also be removed through the same constitutional process applicable to a Supreme Court judge, as provided by Article 218 read with Article 124(4) and Article 124(5).

7. Removal of High Court Judges

A High Court judge may be removed by the President after an address by each House of Parliament, supported by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

The address must be presented to the President in the same session of Parliament. The grounds are proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides the statutory framework for investigation of charges.

The process is deliberately difficult to protect judicial independence while preserving a constitutional mechanism for accountability.

8. Independence of High Courts

The Constitution provides safeguards to protect High Court judges from improper influence by the Executive and Legislature.

  • Security of tenure until the age of 62 years.
  • Removal only through the special parliamentary procedure.
  • Salaries and allowances charged on the Consolidated Fund of the State, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Service conditions cannot be varied to a judge's disadvantage after appointment, except as constitutionally permitted.
  • Restrictions on parliamentary discussion of judicial conduct under Article 121, except upon a removal motion.
  • Contempt powers as a Court of Record under Article 215.
  • Control over subordinate courts under Article 235.
  • Administrative autonomy concerning officers and servants under Article 229.

9. Writ Jurisdiction — Article 226

Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue directions, orders or writs, including habeas corpus, mandamus, prohibition, certiorari and quo warranto.

The jurisdiction may be exercised for:

  • Enforcement of Fundamental Rights; and
  • Enforcement of any other purpose, including other legal rights.

This makes Article 226 wider in subject matter than Article 32, which is specifically available for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

A High Court may exercise jurisdiction where the cause of action arises wholly or in part within its territorial jurisdiction, even if the seat of the authority concerned is outside that territory, subject to Article 226(2).

11. Appellate Jurisdiction

High Courts hear appeals from subordinate courts and tribunals under the Constitution and applicable legislation. The exact scope depends on the relevant procedural and substantive laws.

  • Civil appeals: Appeals from civil courts under applicable civil procedure and special statutes.
  • Criminal appeals: Appeals against specified convictions, acquittals and sentences under criminal procedure law.
  • Constitutional matters: Cases involving constitutional interpretation and judicial review.
  • Statutory appeals: Appeals conferred by specific enactments.

High Courts also exercise appellate jurisdiction over specified decisions of tribunals where the statutory framework and constitutional requirements permit.

12. Original Jurisdiction

High Courts exercise original jurisdiction in matters assigned to them by the Constitution or legislation. Its extent differs across High Courts.

Examples include:

  • Writ petitions under Article 226.
  • Election petitions under applicable election laws.
  • Specified civil matters where original jurisdiction is conferred by law.
  • Company, insolvency, testamentary or admiralty matters where jurisdiction is vested by legislation or preserved under applicable law.

Some High Courts have historically exercised ordinary original civil jurisdiction in specified territorial and subject-matter limits.

13. Superintendence — Article 227

Article 227 gives every High Court superintendence over all courts and tribunals throughout the territories in relation to which it exercises jurisdiction, subject to constitutional exclusions.

Its purposes include:

  • Ensuring that subordinate courts and tribunals act within their jurisdiction.
  • Maintaining standards of judicial administration.
  • Correcting jurisdictional errors and serious procedural irregularities.
  • Promoting consistency in the administration of justice.

The power is supervisory, not a general appellate jurisdiction. It is exercised with judicial restraint.

14. Control over Subordinate Courts — Article 235

Article 235 vests control over district courts and courts subordinate thereto in the High Court.

This control includes important aspects of posting, promotion and leave of judicial officers belonging to the State judicial service, subject to applicable constitutional provisions and service rules.

The provision supports judicial independence at the district and subordinate levels by limiting executive interference in judicial administration.

15. Transfer of Cases — Article 228

Article 228 provides that where a High Court is satisfied that a case pending in a subordinate court involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution, the determination of which is necessary for disposal of the case, it shall withdraw the case.

The High Court may dispose of the entire case itself or determine the constitutional question and return the case to the subordinate court with its judgment.

16. Common High Courts and Union Territories

Article 231 permits Parliament to establish a common High Court for two or more States or for two or more States and Union Territories.

Article 230 enables Parliament to extend or exclude the jurisdiction of a High Court to or from a Union Territory, subject to the constitutional framework.

A common High Court promotes shared judicial infrastructure and may serve multiple territorial jurisdictions without changing the constitutional status of the States concerned.

17. Additional, Acting and Retired Judges

  • Article 223: The President may appoint a judge of the High Court to perform the duties of the Chief Justice when the office is vacant or the Chief Justice is unable to perform duties.
  • Article 224: Additional judges may be appointed for a temporary increase in business or arrears, subject to constitutional limits. Acting judges may be appointed in specified circumstances.
  • Article 224A: The Chief Justice of a High Court may, with the previous consent of the President, request a retired judge of that or another High Court to sit and act as a judge of the High Court.

These provisions provide flexibility in managing vacancies, temporary workload and judicial arrears.

18. High Courts and Judicial Review

High Courts play a major role in constitutional judicial review. They may examine the legality and constitutionality of State laws, delegated legislation and executive action.

Article 226 is a major instrument of this review. Article 227 and the High Courts' constitutional position also support supervision of the judicial and tribunal system.

Judicial review by constitutional courts is part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution, as recognised in L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997).

19. High Courts and Tribunals

Tribunals may be established under legislation and constitutional provisions, including Articles 323A and 323B.

In L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review of High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32 forms part of the Basic Structure. Tribunal decisions are therefore subject to scrutiny by the jurisdictional High Court, ordinarily through a Division Bench.

Tribunals may function as courts of first instance in their specialised fields, but they cannot replace the constitutional role of High Courts in judicial review.

20. High Court and Subordinate Judiciary

High Courts occupy a central position in the administration of justice within each State. Their constitutional responsibilities include:

  • Administrative control over subordinate courts under Article 235.
  • Superintendence under Article 227.
  • Appellate scrutiny of subordinate court decisions.
  • Judicial review of administrative and judicial action within their jurisdiction.
  • Participation in the constitutional process governing district judge appointments under Article 233.
  • Consultation in appointments and postings under Article 234 and applicable service rules.

22. Challenges Facing High Courts

  • Vacancies: Unfilled judicial posts affect disposal capacity.
  • Pendency: Heavy caseloads contribute to delay and uncertainty.
  • Uneven distribution: Workload and infrastructure vary across High Courts.
  • Access to justice: Distance, litigation costs, language and legal awareness create barriers.
  • Appointment delays: Delays in the appointment process can weaken institutional capacity.
  • Infrastructure: Courtrooms, digital facilities, staff and case-management systems may be inadequate.
  • Procedural complexity: Adjournments and lengthy procedures can increase litigation time and cost.
  • Tribunal coordination: Fragmented jurisdiction and appeals may create additional litigation.
  • Administrative autonomy: Maintaining effective control over subordinate courts requires adequate institutional capacity.

23. Reform Priorities

  • Fill judicial vacancies through timely and transparent processes.
  • Strengthen court infrastructure, staffing and digital case-management systems.
  • Improve judicial research and administrative support.
  • Promote alternative dispute resolution and mediation to reduce avoidable litigation.
  • Expand e-filing, virtual hearings and access to digital records while safeguarding open justice.
  • Improve translation of judgments and multilingual legal services.
  • Strengthen district court infrastructure and judicial services.
  • Improve case allocation and prioritisation for urgent and constitutionally significant matters.
  • Ensure accountability and transparency while protecting judicial independence.
  • Improve coordination between High Courts, tribunals and subordinate courts.

24. Important Distinctions

  • Article 226 vs Article 227: Article 226 provides writ jurisdiction; Article 227 provides superintendence over subordinate courts and tribunals within the constitutional framework.
  • Article 227 vs Article 235: Article 227 concerns judicial superintendence; Article 235 vests control over district and subordinate courts in the High Court.
  • Article 32 vs Article 226: Article 32 is a Fundamental Right for enforcement of Fundamental Rights; Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Additional vs acting judge: Article 224 provides for both, but they address different temporary institutional requirements.
  • Common High Court vs separate High Courts: Article 231 enables a shared High Court for multiple States or States and Union Territories.
  • Original vs appellate jurisdiction: Original jurisdiction hears matters at first instance; appellate jurisdiction reviews decisions of lower courts.

25. Integrated Understanding

High Courts are indispensable to India's constitutional democracy. Their writ jurisdiction protects rights, their appellate jurisdiction ensures judicial correction, and their supervisory and administrative powers support the integrity of the subordinate judiciary.

Their effectiveness depends on independence, timely appointments, adequate infrastructure, accessibility and institutional accountability. Strengthening High Courts also strengthens the entire justice-delivery system, especially at the district and local levels.
