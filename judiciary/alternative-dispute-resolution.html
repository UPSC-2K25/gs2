1. Meaning and Significance

Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) refers to processes through which disputes are resolved outside conventional adjudication by courts, or through court-connected settlement mechanisms.

ADR includes negotiation, mediation, conciliation, arbitration and Lok Adalats. These processes differ in the role of the neutral third party, the degree of party control and the legal effect of the outcome.

  • Reduces the burden on courts.
  • Can provide faster and less expensive dispute resolution.
  • Encourages consensual settlement and preservation of relationships.
  • Allows procedures suited to the nature of the dispute.
  • Improves access to justice for individuals and businesses.

3. Negotiation

Negotiation is a process in which parties communicate directly to reach a mutually acceptable settlement without requiring a neutral third-party decision-maker.

  • Voluntary and flexible.
  • Parties retain control over the process and outcome.
  • Can preserve commercial, family or institutional relationships.
  • May be formal or informal and can occur before or during litigation.

Its effectiveness depends on informed participation, bargaining capacity and willingness to compromise. Unequal bargaining power may affect the fairness of the outcome.

4. Mediation

Mediation is a structured process in which a neutral mediator assists parties in reaching a mutually acceptable settlement. The mediator facilitates communication but does not ordinarily impose a binding decision on the merits.

Key characteristics include:

  • Voluntary participation, subject to applicable statutory requirements and court-referred processes.
  • Party control over the settlement outcome.
  • Confidentiality subject to statutory exceptions.
  • Neutral facilitation and informed decision-making.
  • Possibility of preserving ongoing relationships.

5. Mediation Act, 2023

The Mediation Act, 2023 establishes a statutory framework for mediation in India. It seeks to promote institutional and community mediation, facilitate enforcement of mediated settlement agreements and support online mediation.

Its framework addresses:

  • Pre-litigation mediation and voluntary mediation.
  • Court and tribunal referral to mediation in appropriate cases.
  • Institutional mediation and mediator accreditation-related arrangements.
  • Mediated settlement agreements and their enforcement, subject to statutory requirements.
  • Confidentiality and privilege relating to mediation communications, subject to exceptions.
  • Online mediation and community mediation.
  • The Mediation Council of India and institutional development.

The Act contains exclusions and qualifications for specified disputes. Its provisions must be read with commencement notifications, rules and amendments applicable at the relevant time.

6. Court-Annexed and Institutional Mediation

Court-annexed mediation operates through mediation centres associated with courts. Institutional mediation is administered by an organisation that provides rules, facilities and administrative support.

These arrangements can improve quality and consistency through trained mediators, clear procedures and appropriate referral systems.

Important institutional considerations include mediator competence, neutrality, confidentiality, accessibility, settlement recording and follow-up mechanisms.

7. Conciliation

Conciliation is a consensual dispute-resolution process in which a conciliator assists parties in reaching an amicable settlement. Under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, conciliation has a distinct statutory framework.

The conciliator may take a more active role in suggesting settlement terms than a mediator ordinarily does, while the parties retain control over whether to accept a settlement.

Under the statutory framework, a settlement agreement reached through conciliation has the status and effect specified by the Act, including treatment as an arbitral award on agreed terms for the purposes of the relevant provisions.

8. Arbitration

Arbitration is a private adjudicatory process in which parties submit a dispute to one or more arbitrators and receive an award. Unlike mediation and negotiation, the arbitrator determines the dispute rather than merely facilitating agreement.

  • Generally based on an arbitration agreement.
  • Can be domestic or international commercial arbitration.
  • May be institutional or ad hoc.
  • Allows party autonomy in procedural matters within statutory limits.
  • Arbitral awards are binding and enforceable under the governing law, subject to limited statutory challenge grounds.

9. Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996

The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 consolidates the law relating to domestic arbitration, international commercial arbitration and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards, and provides a framework for conciliation.

Important features include:

  • Recognition of arbitration agreements.
  • Appointment and jurisdiction of arbitral tribunals.
  • Interim measures by courts and arbitral tribunals under the Act.
  • Conduct of arbitral proceedings and equal treatment of parties.
  • Making and form of arbitral awards.
  • Challenge to awards under Section 34 on limited statutory grounds.
  • Enforcement of domestic awards under Section 36.
  • Recognition and enforcement of specified foreign awards under the applicable parts of the Act.

10. Court Intervention in Arbitration

The arbitration framework seeks to minimise unnecessary judicial intervention while preserving judicial supervision on defined grounds.

  • Section 5 limits judicial intervention in matters governed by Part I, except where the Act provides otherwise.
  • Section 8 addresses referral of parties to arbitration where its requirements are met.
  • Section 9 provides for specified interim measures by courts.
  • Section 11 deals with appointment of arbitrators in specified circumstances.
  • Section 17 provides interim measures by arbitral tribunals.
  • Section 34 provides limited grounds for setting aside an award.
  • Section 36 deals with enforcement of awards.

Judicial supervision protects legality and fairness but should not become a routine rehearing of the merits.

11. International Commercial Arbitration

International commercial arbitration provides a neutral forum for cross-border commercial disputes. It is important for international trade, investment and contractual confidence.

India's legal framework recognises international commercial arbitration and provides for enforcement of certain foreign awards under the New York Convention and Geneva Convention frameworks incorporated in the 1996 Act.

Predictable enforcement, judicial restraint, impartial arbitrator selection and efficient proceedings influence a jurisdiction's attractiveness as an arbitration seat.

12. Lok Adalats

Lok Adalats are statutory dispute-settlement forums established under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987. They seek to resolve disputes through compromise and settlement.

  • They may take up pending cases and disputes at the pre-litigation stage, subject to statutory conditions.
  • They use conciliatory methods rather than deciding contested disputes on merits.
  • An award of a Lok Adalat is deemed to be a decree of a civil court and is final and binding under the Act.
  • No appeal lies against a Lok Adalat award under the ordinary statutory framework.
  • Where settlement is not reached, the pending case returns to the court or the parties may pursue an appropriate remedy according to law.

13. Permanent Lok Adalats

Permanent Lok Adalats are established under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 for disputes relating to public utility services, as defined by the Act.

They have a two-stage role:

  • First, they attempt conciliation between the parties.
  • If settlement fails, they may decide the dispute on merits within their statutory jurisdiction, subject to the statutory restrictions.

This distinguishes Permanent Lok Adalats from ordinary Lok Adalats, which depend on compromise or settlement and do not decide the dispute on merits when parties fail to agree.

14. Legal Services Authorities and Access to ADR

The Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987 establishes a national and State-level framework for legal services and Lok Adalats.

  • National Legal Services Authority (NALSA).
  • State Legal Services Authorities.
  • District Legal Services Authorities.
  • Taluk Legal Services Committees.
  • Supreme Court Legal Services Committee and High Court Legal Services Committees.

These institutions support legal aid, awareness, advice and access to settlement mechanisms for eligible persons.

15. Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure

Section 89 of the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 enables courts to refer suitable disputes for settlement outside the court through mechanisms identified in the provision, including arbitration, conciliation, judicial settlement including settlement through Lok Adalat, and mediation.

The provision reflects the legislative objective of encouraging settlement where the nature of the dispute and the parties' circumstances make ADR appropriate.

In Afcons Infrastructure Ltd. v. Cherian Varkey Construction Co. (2010), the Supreme Court explained the operation of Section 89 and the suitability of different ADR processes for different categories of disputes.

16. Pre-Institution Mediation in Commercial Disputes

Section 12A of the Commercial Courts Act, 2015 provides for pre-institution mediation in specified commercial disputes where urgent interim relief is not contemplated.

The framework seeks to encourage settlement before parties enter full-scale commercial litigation, reducing cost and judicial workload.

In Patil Automation Pvt. Ltd. v. Rakheja Engineers Pvt. Ltd. (2022), the Supreme Court treated compliance with Section 12A as mandatory for suits to which it applies, subject to the judgment's directions and legal framework.

18. Disputes Suitable for ADR

ADR may be particularly suitable for:

  • Commercial and contractual disputes.
  • Construction and infrastructure disputes.
  • Consumer disputes where settlement is appropriate.
  • Family and matrimonial disputes, subject to legal safeguards and the nature of the relief sought.
  • Neighbourhood and community disputes.
  • Specified public utility service disputes.
  • Cross-border commercial disputes.

Not every dispute is suitable for private settlement. Disputes involving serious criminal offences, certain public-law questions, rights of third parties or matters expressly excluded by law may require adjudication through the prescribed legal process.

19. Advantages of ADR

  • Efficiency: Can resolve disputes faster than conventional litigation.
  • Cost reduction: May reduce legal and procedural expenses.
  • Party autonomy: Parties can shape the process and settlement.
  • Confidentiality: Some ADR mechanisms provide greater confidentiality than open court proceedings, subject to law.
  • Expertise: Parties may select neutral professionals with relevant technical knowledge in arbitration.
  • Relationship preservation: Consensual processes can reduce adversarial conflict.
  • Access: Local and community mechanisms may make dispute resolution more accessible.

20. Limitations and Challenges

  • Unequal bargaining power: Weaker parties may accept unfair settlements.
  • Awareness gaps: Citizens and businesses may lack knowledge of available mechanisms.
  • Quality variation: Mediator and arbitrator competence may differ.
  • Cost of arbitration: Complex arbitration can become expensive.
  • Enforcement delays: Challenges and enforcement proceedings can reduce the speed advantage.
  • Consent concerns: Formal consent may not always reflect genuine bargaining freedom.
  • Confidentiality concerns: Excessive secrecy may limit transparency in disputes involving public interest.
  • Limited institutional capacity: Infrastructure and trained professionals remain unevenly distributed.
  • Unsuitable referrals: Matters requiring authoritative public adjudication may be inappropriately referred to settlement processes.

21. Reform Priorities

  • Strengthen mediator training, accreditation and professional standards.
  • Improve public awareness of ADR options and legal aid.
  • Expand accessible mediation centres across districts.
  • Promote institutional arbitration with transparent rules and fee structures.
  • Improve enforcement of settlements and arbitral awards.
  • Protect weaker parties through informed consent and safeguards against coercion.
  • Use online dispute resolution where it improves access without excluding digitally disadvantaged groups.
  • Improve coordination between courts, tribunals, legal services authorities and ADR institutions.
  • Collect reliable data on settlement rates, time, costs and user experience.

23. Important Distinctions

  • Mediation vs arbitration: Mediation facilitates agreement; arbitration results in an adjudicatory award.
  • Conciliation vs mediation: Both are consensual, but the statutory conciliation framework permits a more active settlement role for the conciliator.
  • Lok Adalat vs Permanent Lok Adalat: Ordinary Lok Adalat relies on settlement; Permanent Lok Adalat may decide specified public utility disputes on merits after conciliation fails.
  • Adjudication vs settlement: Adjudication determines rights through a decision; settlement depends on agreement or a statutory settlement process.
  • Institutional vs ad hoc arbitration: Institutional arbitration is administered under an institution's rules; ad hoc arbitration is organised by the parties and tribunal without that institutional administration.

24. Integrated Understanding

ADR complements the formal justice system by providing suitable alternatives to conventional litigation. It can reduce pendency, improve access and support consensual dispute resolution.

Its success depends on informed participation, impartial neutrals, enforceable outcomes, institutional quality and safeguards for vulnerable parties. ADR should strengthen the justice system, not become a means of denying access to courts or avoiding public accountability.
