1. Meaning and Rationale

Tribunals are adjudicatory bodies established by the Constitution or legislation to decide specified categories of disputes. They provide specialised forums outside the ordinary court hierarchy, although their decisions remain subject to constitutional judicial review.

Their growth reflects the need for expertise, procedural flexibility and faster resolution in technically complex or high-volume areas such as taxation, public employment, environmental regulation and service matters.

  • Specialised knowledge and expertise.
  • Reduction of the workload of ordinary courts.
  • Accessible and comparatively flexible procedures.
  • Consistent adjudication in specialised fields.
  • Institutional mechanisms for regulatory and administrative disputes.

2. Constitutional Framework

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 inserted Part XIV-A, comprising Articles 323A and 323B, to provide a constitutional basis for tribunals.

3. Administrative Tribunals — Article 323A

Article 323A empowers Parliament to provide by law for administrative tribunals dealing with disputes concerning recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, States, local or other authorities and government-controlled corporations, as specified by law.

The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 provides the principal statutory framework.

  • Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT) deals with covered service matters concerning Union services and other notified categories.
  • State Administrative Tribunals may be established for specified State service matters.
  • Joint Administrative Tribunals may be established for two or more States under the statutory framework.

4. Central Administrative Tribunal

The CAT was established in 1985 under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985. It adjudicates covered disputes concerning recruitment and service conditions of persons within its statutory jurisdiction.

Its jurisdiction includes matters such as appointment, seniority, promotion, pay, disciplinary action, pension and other service conditions, subject to statutory exclusions and the governing law.

  • It is a specialised adjudicatory forum, not a department of the executive deciding service grievances administratively.
  • It follows its statutory procedure and principles of natural justice.
  • It has powers relating to summoning witnesses, receiving evidence and reviewing its decisions within the applicable legal framework.
  • Its decisions are subject to judicial review by the jurisdictional High Court.

5. Tribunals under Article 323B

Article 323B permits the appropriate legislature to provide for tribunals in relation to specified subjects. Unlike Article 323A, which concerns Parliament's power over administrative tribunals, Article 323B contemplates legislation by the appropriate legislature within the constitutional scheme.

The subjects listed in Article 323B include:

  • Taxation.
  • Foreign exchange, import and export.
  • Industrial and labour disputes.
  • Land reforms and related matters.
  • Ceiling on urban property.
  • Elections to specified legislatures, subject to constitutional provisions.
  • Production, procurement, supply and distribution of foodstuffs and other specified goods.
  • Rent, tenancy and related matters.

The precise jurisdiction of any tribunal depends on its enabling statute. Article 323B is not itself a complete code establishing every such tribunal.

7. National Green Tribunal

The National Green Tribunal was established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 for effective and expeditious disposal of specified environmental cases.

  • Exercises jurisdiction over civil cases involving a substantial question relating to the environment arising from the implementation of laws listed in the Act.
  • Hears appeals against specified decisions, directions and orders under scheduled environmental legislation.
  • Applies principles including sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter-pays principle.
  • May grant relief and compensation and order restitution of damaged property and the environment within its statutory powers.

Its significance lies in specialised environmental adjudication, but effective enforcement depends on scientific capacity, institutional resources and compliance with its orders.

8. National Company Law Tribunal

The NCLT was constituted under the Companies Act, 2013. It performs adjudicatory functions assigned under company law and other applicable legislation, including important functions under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016.

  • Company-law disputes and statutory applications.
  • Corporate insolvency resolution processes under the IBC.
  • Liquidation and other matters assigned by legislation.
  • Specified mergers, arrangements and restructuring matters.

The NCLAT hears appeals within its statutory jurisdiction. Further appeals to the Supreme Court are governed by the relevant statutory provisions and the questions of law specified there.

9. Armed Forces Tribunal

The Armed Forces Tribunal was established under the Armed Forces Tribunal Act, 2007. It adjudicates specified disputes relating to service matters and appeals arising from court-martial decisions concerning persons subject to the relevant service laws.

It seeks to provide specialised adjudication of military service disputes while recognising the distinct constitutional and statutory framework governing the armed forces.

10. Tribunal Composition and Expertise

Tribunals may include judicial members and members with technical, administrative or domain expertise. The appropriate composition depends on the subject and the enabling statute.

Sound institutional design requires:

  • Judicial members with adequate legal and adjudicatory experience.
  • Technical members selected for relevant subject expertise.
  • Transparent and merit-based appointments.
  • Secure tenure and fair service conditions.
  • Clear allocation of functions between adjudication and administration.

11. Independence of Tribunals

Tribunals exercise adjudicatory power and must be independent of the authorities whose decisions they review. Their independence is relevant to the rule of law and the constitutional separation of adjudication from executive influence.

Key safeguards include:

  • Independent and transparent selection processes.
  • Reasonable and secure tenure.
  • Protection against arbitrary removal.
  • Appropriate remuneration and service conditions.
  • Independent infrastructure and administrative support.
  • Fair allocation of cases and publication of reasoned decisions.

12. Judicial Review of Tribunal Decisions

In L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court held that judicial review under Articles 32 and 226/227 forms part of the Basic Structure of the Constitution.

The Court held that tribunals may perform a supplementary role, but cannot replace the constitutional jurisdiction of High Courts and the Supreme Court.

  • Tribunal decisions are subject to scrutiny by the jurisdictional High Court under Articles 226 and 227 in the framework laid down by the judgment.
  • Judicial review is not eliminated by a statutory exclusion clause.
  • Specialised adjudication must remain consistent with constitutional supremacy.

13. Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021

The Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 made changes to the framework governing specified tribunals, including provisions relating to their composition, qualifications, appointments, tenure and service conditions.

Its provisions and the wider tribunal reform framework have been subject to constitutional scrutiny. In Madras Bar Association v. Union of India (2021), the Supreme Court examined provisions of the 2021 legislation and invalidated specified provisions that did not meet constitutional requirements.

Tribunal reform must therefore be assessed against judicial independence, separation of powers and the constitutional standards developed by the Supreme Court.

15. Challenges Facing Tribunals

  • Vacancies: Delayed appointments reduce capacity and increase pendency.
  • Executive influence: Dependence on ministries for appointments, finance or infrastructure may affect perceived independence.
  • Short tenure: Inadequate tenure can discourage experienced candidates and affect continuity.
  • Fragmented administration: Different ministries may exercise administrative control over tribunals, creating uneven standards.
  • Infrastructure gaps: Limited benches, staff and digital facilities restrict access.
  • Overlapping jurisdiction: Complex appellate routes can increase litigation.
  • Delay: Tribunals may reproduce the pendency problems they were intended to reduce.
  • Uneven expertise: Appointment and training practices may not ensure adequate technical competence.
  • Compliance: Effective relief depends on implementation of tribunal orders.

16. Reform Priorities

  • Establish transparent, independent and merit-based appointment procedures.
  • Provide adequate tenure and secure service conditions consistent with constitutional standards.
  • Ensure financial and administrative independence.
  • Fill vacancies promptly and maintain sufficient benches.
  • Standardise infrastructure, registry systems and case-management practices.
  • Clarify appellate routes and reduce unnecessary jurisdictional overlap.
  • Publish reasoned decisions and accessible performance data.
  • Provide continuing training in law, technology and relevant technical disciplines.
  • Strengthen compliance mechanisms and periodic institutional evaluation.

18. Integrated Understanding

Tribunals are an important component of India's adjudicatory architecture. They can improve specialised decision-making and reduce pressure on ordinary courts, but only when they possess independence, expertise, adequate resources and clear jurisdiction.

Tribunalisation cannot be a substitute for constitutional courts. The constitutional role of the Supreme Court and High Courts, particularly their judicial-review jurisdiction, remains indispensable.
