1. Meaning and Constitutional Foundation

Jurisdiction refers to the legal authority of a court to hear, determine and decide a dispute. It defines the subject matter, territory, parties and procedural circumstances within which a court may exercise its judicial power.

Constitutional remedies are legal mechanisms through which individuals and institutions seek judicial protection against violations of constitutional and legal rights.

The Indian Constitution establishes the jurisdiction of the Supreme Court and High Courts, protects access to constitutional remedies and provides judicial supervision over the administration of justice.

2. Types of Jurisdiction

  • Original jurisdiction: Authority to hear a matter at the first instance.
  • Appellate jurisdiction: Authority to examine decisions of lower courts or tribunals.
  • Writ jurisdiction: Authority to issue constitutional writs for enforcement of rights and other lawful purposes.
  • Advisory jurisdiction: Authority to provide an opinion on a question referred by the President under Article 143.
  • Review jurisdiction: Authority to reconsider a judgment within the limited grounds recognised by law.
  • Curative jurisdiction: An exceptional, narrowly confined power developed to prevent a gross miscarriage of justice.
  • Supervisory jurisdiction: Authority to supervise subordinate courts and tribunals within constitutional limits.

3. Supreme Court: Constitutional Position

Article 124 establishes the Supreme Court of India. It is the apex judicial institution and exercises jurisdiction conferred by the Constitution and parliamentary legislation.

Its jurisdiction includes original, appellate, advisory, writ, review and other powers.

The Supreme Court's decisions are binding on all courts within India under Article 141. Article 144 requires all civil and judicial authorities to act in aid of the Supreme Court.

4. Original Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court — Article 131

Article 131 confers exclusive original jurisdiction on the Supreme Court in specified federal disputes involving the Union and States.

It covers disputes between:

  • The Government of India and one or more States.
  • The Government of India and one or more States on one side and one or more other States on the other.
  • Two or more States.

The dispute must involve a question, whether of law or fact, on which the existence or extent of a legal right depends.

Article 131 does not automatically cover every political disagreement or every dispute involving a State. Its scope is defined by the constitutional requirements and judicial interpretation.

The jurisdiction is subject to constitutional exclusions, including the relevant provisions concerning certain pre-Constitution treaties, agreements and instruments.

5. Writ Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court — Article 32

Article 32 guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

The Supreme Court may issue directions, orders or writs, including the five traditional writs, for enforcement of rights conferred by Part III.

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 as the Constitution's heart and soul because it makes Fundamental Rights judicially enforceable.

Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right. However, the scope of the remedy remains tied to enforcement of Fundamental Rights.

6. High Court Writ Jurisdiction — Article 226

Article 226 empowers every High Court to issue directions, orders or writs to any person or authority, including in appropriate cases any government, within its territorial jurisdiction.

High Courts may issue writs:

  • For enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • For enforcement of other legal rights and for other purposes within the scope of Article 226.

Article 226 is therefore wider in subject matter than Article 32, although its exercise is governed by judicial principles, territorial limits and discretionary considerations.

Article 226(2) permits a High Court to exercise jurisdiction where the cause of action arises wholly or in part within its territorial jurisdiction, even if the seat of the authority is outside that territory, subject to the constitutional conditions.

7. The Five Constitutional Writs

Articles 32 and 226 recognise the power to issue five traditional writs. Each serves a distinct constitutional purpose.

Habeas Corpus

Meaning: "You may have the body."

It protects personal liberty by requiring the authority or person detaining an individual to justify the legality of detention.

  • Can be sought against unlawful detention by public authorities.
  • May also be issued against private persons in appropriate cases of unlawful custody.
  • It is an important remedy against arbitrary detention and violations of personal liberty.

Mandamus

Meaning: "We command."

It directs a public authority or other legally amenable body to perform a public or statutory duty that it has failed or refused to perform.

  • Requires an existing legal duty.
  • Generally does not compel performance of a purely private duty.
  • Cannot ordinarily dictate the manner of exercising lawful discretion, although it may require the authority to exercise that discretion lawfully.

Prohibition

It is issued by a superior court to a lower court or tribunal to prevent it from continuing proceedings beyond its jurisdiction or in violation of the law.

  • Generally preventive in nature.
  • Intervenes before the lower forum completes the proceeding.
  • Protects jurisdictional boundaries and lawful procedure.

Certiorari

It is issued by a superior court to quash an order or decision of a lower court, tribunal or other body where recognised grounds exist.

  • Generally corrective in nature.
  • May be used where the decision-maker exceeded jurisdiction or acted without jurisdiction.
  • May address errors of law apparent on the face of the record and violations of principles of natural justice, subject to applicable judicial standards.

Quo Warranto

Meaning: "By what authority?"

It questions the legal authority by which a person holds a substantive public office.

  • Protects the legality of appointments to public offices.
  • Can be sought by a person without demonstrating the same personal injury required in many other writ proceedings.
  • Requires that the office be public and substantive and that the appointment be legally challengeable.

9. Appellate Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court exercises appellate jurisdiction in constitutional, civil and criminal matters.

Constitutional Appeals — Article 132

An appeal may lie from a judgment, decree or final order of a High Court where the case involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution and the High Court certifies the case as fit for appeal.

Civil Appeals — Article 133

Appeals may lie from specified High Court judgments in civil proceedings where the constitutional conditions are satisfied, including certification that the case involves a substantial question of law of general importance requiring decision by the Supreme Court.

Criminal Appeals — Article 134

The Constitution provides specified routes for criminal appeals, including certain cases involving reversal of acquittal and imposition of death sentence, and cases certified by the High Court as fit for appeal.

Certificate for Appeal — Article 134A

Article 134A provides the procedure for consideration of a certificate for appeal under Articles 132, 133 and 134.

10. Special Leave Petition — Article 136

Article 136 empowers the Supreme Court, in its discretion, to grant special leave to appeal from judgments, decrees, determinations, sentences or orders passed by courts or tribunals in India.

It is an extraordinary discretionary jurisdiction and is not an ordinary right of appeal.

  • It can be invoked in civil, criminal and other appropriate proceedings.
  • The Court may refuse leave where no substantial reason for intervention exists.
  • It is not available against a judgment or order of a court or tribunal constituted by or under a law relating to the Armed Forces.
  • The Court generally uses it to address substantial injustice, serious legal error or exceptional circumstances.

11. Advisory Jurisdiction — Article 143

Article 143 enables the President to refer certain questions to the Supreme Court for its opinion.

  • Article 143(1) concerns a question of law or fact of public importance that has arisen or is likely to arise and is considered expedient to refer.
  • Article 143(2) concerns disputes arising out of specified pre-Constitution treaties, agreements, covenants or similar instruments, subject to its terms.

Under Article 143(1), the Supreme Court may decline to answer a reference. Its opinion is advisory rather than an ordinary binding adjudication between litigating parties.

Advisory opinions carry significant constitutional authority, although their legal character differs from judgments delivered in contentious proceedings.

12. Review and Curative Jurisdiction

Review — Article 137

Article 137 empowers the Supreme Court to review its judgments or orders, subject to laws made by Parliament and rules framed under Article 145.

Review is limited and is not a routine opportunity to reargue a case simply because a party disagrees with the outcome.

Curative Petition

The Supreme Court developed the curative petition in Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra (2002). It is an exceptional remedy intended to prevent a gross miscarriage of justice after dismissal of a review petition.

Its use is subject to strict judicial safeguards and narrowly defined grounds.

13. Supervisory Jurisdiction of High Courts — Article 227

Article 227 gives every High Court superintendence over courts and tribunals within its territorial jurisdiction, subject to constitutional exclusions.

It supports:

  • Supervision over subordinate judicial institutions.
  • Correction of serious jurisdictional errors.
  • Maintenance of judicial discipline and lawful procedure.
  • Protection of the integrity of the subordinate court system.

It is not an unrestricted appellate power and should not ordinarily be used to substitute the High Court's view for every decision of a subordinate court.

14. Transfer of Cases — Articles 139A and 228

Article 139A enables the Supreme Court to transfer certain cases involving the same or substantially the same questions of law of general importance pending before the Supreme Court and one or more High Courts, and to withdraw cases from High Courts in the circumstances specified by the provision.

Article 228 empowers a High Court to withdraw a case pending before a subordinate court if it involves a substantial question of law as to the interpretation of the Constitution and the High Court considers that determination necessary for disposal of the case.

15. Disputes Involving Elections and Constitutional Offices

The Constitution and parliamentary legislation assign jurisdiction over certain categories of disputes to designated judicial forums.

  • Article 329(b) restricts ordinary judicial interference in electoral processes except through an election petition in the manner provided by law.
  • Article 71 assigns disputes concerning the election of the President and Vice-President to the Supreme Court.
  • Article 323A and Article 323B provide for tribunals in specified fields, subject to constitutional judicial review.
  • Service, tax, administrative and other statutory disputes may be assigned to specialised forums, subject to constitutional limitations.

16. Jurisdiction and the Doctrine of Alternative Remedy

Courts may decline to exercise discretionary writ jurisdiction where an effective alternative statutory remedy is available.

This is a rule of judicial restraint rather than an absolute constitutional bar.

Commonly recognised exceptions include:

  • Violation of Fundamental Rights.
  • Violation of principles of natural justice.
  • Proceedings or orders wholly without jurisdiction.
  • Challenge to the vires or constitutional validity of legislation.

The existence of an alternative remedy does not automatically extinguish the constitutional jurisdiction of a High Court under Article 226.

17. Jurisdiction and Territorial Limits

Courts must act within their constitutionally and statutorily defined territorial and subject-matter limits.

  • The Supreme Court exercises jurisdiction across India within the constitutional framework.
  • High Court writ jurisdiction is governed by Article 226(2), including the place where the cause of action wholly or partly arises.
  • Subordinate courts exercise jurisdiction assigned by procedural and substantive laws.
  • Tribunals exercise jurisdiction within the limits of their enabling statutes.

Jurisdictional limits prevent arbitrary exercise of judicial power and promote orderly administration of justice.

18. Public Interest Litigation

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) is a judicially developed method of relaxing traditional rules of standing in appropriate cases involving public injury or rights of disadvantaged groups.

It has expanded access to constitutional remedies for persons who may be unable to approach courts individually.

PIL is not a separate constitutional jurisdiction. It operates through existing constitutional powers, particularly Articles 32 and 226.

Safeguards against publicity-oriented or private-interest litigation are necessary to protect judicial time and institutional credibility.

19. Limits on Constitutional Remedies

Constitutional remedies are powerful but operate within legal and constitutional constraints.

  • Jurisdictional requirements must be satisfied.
  • Writ relief is governed by judicial discretion and equitable principles.
  • Courts may consider delay, laches and availability of alternative remedies.
  • Relief must be consistent with constitutional and statutory limits.
  • Judicial review does not ordinarily authorise courts to take over policy-making or day-to-day administration.
  • Emergency provisions and statutory restrictions may affect the availability of particular remedies, subject to constitutional safeguards.

21. Challenges in the Exercise of Jurisdiction

  • Case pendency: Delays weaken the practical value of constitutional remedies.
  • Overlapping jurisdiction: Multiple forums may create procedural complexity and inconsistent approaches.
  • Access barriers: Cost, distance, language and legal awareness limit access to higher courts.
  • Forum selection: Uncertainty about the proper court can cause delay and additional expense.
  • Misuse of PIL: Private or publicity-driven litigation can divert judicial resources.
  • Tribunal dependence: Institutional design and administrative control may affect tribunal independence.
  • Implementation: Judicial orders may be ineffective without timely administrative compliance.

22. Reform Priorities

  • Improve judicial capacity and fill vacancies.
  • Strengthen legal aid and awareness of constitutional remedies.
  • Clarify jurisdictional rules and reduce unnecessary procedural complexity.
  • Improve the independence and competence of tribunals.
  • Promote mediation and other suitable dispute-resolution mechanisms.
  • Use technology to improve filing, case tracking and access to judgments.
  • Ensure that PIL remains accessible to genuine public causes while discouraging abuse.
  • Improve compliance with judicial orders through lawful institutional coordination.

24. Important Distinctions

  • Article 32 vs Article 226: Article 32 enforces Fundamental Rights; Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights and other legal purposes.
  • Original vs appellate jurisdiction: Original jurisdiction hears a matter at first instance; appellate jurisdiction reviews a decision of another forum.
  • Prohibition vs certiorari: Prohibition prevents a lower forum from proceeding beyond its jurisdiction; certiorari quashes an order on recognised grounds.
  • Mandamus vs quo warranto: Mandamus compels performance of a public duty; quo warranto questions authority to hold a public office.
  • Review vs appeal: Review is a limited reconsideration by the same court; appeal is a statutory or constitutional process before an appellate forum.
  • Advisory vs contentious jurisdiction: Advisory jurisdiction involves a Presidential reference; contentious jurisdiction determines disputes between parties.
  • Article 226 vs Article 227: Article 226 is writ jurisdiction; Article 227 is supervisory jurisdiction.

25. Integrated Understanding

Judicial jurisdiction and constitutional remedies make constitutional limitations enforceable. The Supreme Court protects constitutional rights and resolves specified federal disputes, while High Courts provide a broad and accessible constitutional remedy within their territorial jurisdiction.

The effectiveness of these powers depends not only on constitutional text but also on judicial independence, institutional capacity, access to legal assistance, timely adjudication and compliance with judicial decisions.
