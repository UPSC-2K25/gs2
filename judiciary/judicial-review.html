1. Meaning and Constitutional Significance

Judicial review is the power of constitutional courts to examine the constitutionality and legality of legislative enactments, executive actions and, within the applicable constitutional framework, other exercises of public power.

Where an action violates the Constitution or exceeds legal authority, courts may grant appropriate relief, including declaring a provision unconstitutional, quashing an unlawful order or issuing constitutional directions.

Its principal objectives are:

  • Upholding constitutional supremacy.
  • Protecting Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Maintaining the federal distribution of powers.
  • Preventing arbitrary and ultra vires State action.
  • Preserving the rule of law and constitutional limitations.

2. Constitutional Foundations of Judicial Review

The Constitution does not use one single provision to establish the entire system of judicial review. Its foundations are distributed across provisions dealing with rights, judicial remedies, legislative competence and constitutional courts.

3. Constitutional Supremacy and Judicial Review

India follows constitutional supremacy rather than unlimited parliamentary sovereignty. All organs of the State derive authority from the Constitution and must function within its limitations.

Parliament possesses extensive legislative and constitutional amendment powers, but these are not unlimited. Courts may examine whether legislation or constitutional amendments violate enforceable constitutional limitations.

Judicial review therefore acts as a mechanism for maintaining constitutional government, rather than as a general power to substitute judicial preferences for legislative policy.

4. Judicial Review of Legislation

Courts may examine legislation on several constitutional grounds.

  • Violation of Fundamental Rights: A law may be invalid where it violates applicable provisions of Part III.
  • Lack of legislative competence: A legislature cannot enact laws beyond its constitutional subject-matter authority.
  • Violation of constitutional procedure: A law may be challenged where a mandatory constitutional requirement has not been followed.
  • Repugnancy: A State law may be invalid to the extent of repugnancy with a valid Union law in the Concurrent List, subject to Article 254.
  • Basic Structure violation: A constitutional amendment may be invalidated if it damages or destroys the Constitution's Basic Structure.

5. Article 13 and Fundamental Rights Review

Article 13 provides a central textual foundation for judicial review of laws that violate Fundamental Rights.

  • Article 13(1) addresses pre-Constitution laws to the extent that they are inconsistent with Part III.
  • Article 13(2) prohibits the State from making laws that take away or abridge Fundamental Rights and declares such laws void to the extent of contravention.
  • Article 13(3) defines the terms "law" and "laws in force" for the purposes of the provision.
  • Article 13(4) states that Article 13 does not apply to constitutional amendments made under Article 368, following the 24th Amendment. Such amendments remain subject to the Basic Structure doctrine.

The doctrine of severability permits courts, where appropriate, to invalidate only the unconstitutional part of a law if it can be separated from the valid remainder.

6. Judicial Review of Executive Action

Executive authorities must act within the Constitution, statutes and principles of administrative law.

Courts may review executive action on grounds including:

  • Lack or excess of jurisdiction.
  • Violation of Fundamental Rights.
  • Failure to follow mandatory legal procedure.
  • Arbitrariness, mala fides or improper purpose.
  • Violation of principles of natural justice.
  • Relevant considerations being ignored or irrelevant considerations being relied upon.
  • Unreasonableness or proportionality where the applicable legal standard requires such scrutiny.

Judicial review generally examines the legality of administrative action rather than replacing the authority's lawful policy choice with the court's preferred alternative.

7. Judicial Review of Constitutional Amendments

Article 368 empowers Parliament to amend the Constitution through the prescribed procedure. However, the power is limited by the Basic Structure doctrine.

In Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973), the Supreme Court held that Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights, but cannot damage or destroy its Basic Structure.

Courts may therefore examine whether a constitutional amendment:

  • Was passed in accordance with Article 368.
  • Received State ratification where the proviso to Article 368(2) requires it.
  • Damages or destroys an essential feature of the Constitution.

8. The Basic Structure Doctrine

The Basic Structure doctrine is a judicially evolved limitation on Parliament's power to amend the Constitution.

The Supreme Court has recognised several features as part of the Basic Structure in different judgments. These include:

  • Supremacy of the Constitution.
  • Republican and democratic form of government.
  • Secular character of the Constitution.
  • Federal character of the Constitution.
  • Separation of powers.
  • Judicial review.
  • Independence of the judiciary.
  • Rule of law.
  • Free and fair elections.
  • Parliamentary system of government.
  • Unity and integrity of India.
  • Limited amending power of Parliament.
  • Balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.

The list is illustrative, not a closed catalogue. Whether a particular feature forms part of the Basic Structure and whether an amendment damages it are questions determined in the context of the challenged amendment.

9. Judicial Review and Federalism

India has a constitutionally divided legislative field. Judicial review helps ensure that the Union and States exercise their respective powers within the constitutional distribution.

  • Articles 245 and 246 define the territorial and subject-matter scope of legislative power.
  • The Seventh Schedule distributes subjects across the Union, State and Concurrent Lists.
  • Article 254 addresses inconsistency between Union and State laws in the Concurrent List.
  • Article 131 provides an original forum for specified disputes between the Union and States or among States.

Judicial review is therefore important for preserving the federal balance and resolving constitutional disputes through law.

10. Judicial Review and Fundamental Rights

Judicial review ensures that State action remains consistent with the rights guaranteed under Part III.

  • Article 14 protects equality before law and equal protection of laws.
  • Article 19 protects specified freedoms of citizens, subject to constitutionally permitted restrictions.
  • Article 21 protects life and personal liberty through a fair, just and reasonable procedure.
  • Articles 25–28 protect religious freedom within constitutional limits.
  • Articles 29–30 protect specified cultural and educational rights.
  • Article 32 provides a direct constitutional remedy before the Supreme Court.

Judicial review also supports the harmonious interpretation of Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles of State Policy.

11. Judicial Review of Presidential and Governor's Action

Constitutional offices are not outside judicial scrutiny merely because they involve high constitutional authorities.

Courts may examine constitutional action where the Constitution permits judicial scrutiny, including specified exercises of discretion, constitutional powers and satisfaction-based decisions.

  • Article 361 provides specified personal immunities to the President and Governors during their term of office.
  • Article 361 does not make the validity of governmental action immune from judicial review.
  • Article 356 proclamations are subject to judicial review under the principles laid down in S.R. Bommai.
  • Exercise of powers under Articles 200 and 201 must be understood within the constitutional framework and relevant judicial interpretation.

12. Judicial Review of Ordinances

Ordinances issued under Articles 123 and 213 have the force of law, but are subject to constitutional limitations.

Courts may examine whether the constitutional conditions for promulgation were satisfied and whether the ordinance violates Fundamental Rights or other constitutional requirements.

The ordinance power is not a parallel or unrestricted legislative process. It is an exceptional law-making mechanism subject to constitutional safeguards and legislative control.

13. Judicial Review of Tribunals

Tribunals may be created to provide specialised adjudication, but their decisions and institutional design remain subject to constitutional limitations.

In L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997), the Supreme Court held that the power of judicial review of High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32 forms part of the Basic Structure.

Decisions of tribunals are therefore subject to scrutiny by the jurisdictional High Court division bench in accordance with the judgment's framework.

Tribunal specialisation cannot be used to eliminate the constitutional role of the higher judiciary.

14. Judicial Review and Elections

Free and fair elections are an essential feature of constitutional democracy.

Article 329(b) provides that an election to either House of Parliament or to a House of a State Legislature may be called in question only through an election petition presented in the manner provided by law.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 provides the statutory framework for election petitions and adjudication by designated High Courts.

The constitutional restriction is designed to avoid interruption of the electoral process, while preserving a legal mechanism for challenging election results.

15. Judicial Review and the Ninth Schedule

Article 31B protects laws placed in the Ninth Schedule from being void merely on the ground of inconsistency with Fundamental Rights, subject to the constitutional interpretation developed by the Supreme Court.

In I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007), the Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be challenged if their impact damages or destroys the Basic Structure.

The judgment prevents the Ninth Schedule from becoming a device for completely insulating legislation from constitutional scrutiny.

16. Judicial Review and the Separation of Powers

Judicial review is a constitutional check on legislative and executive power, but it must operate consistently with separation of powers.

  • Legislatures make laws within constitutional competence.
  • The Executive implements laws and conducts administration.
  • The Judiciary interprets law and adjudicates disputes.

The Indian Constitution permits functional overlap and checks and balances. Judicial review ensures constitutional compliance without granting courts a general mandate to govern or legislate.

17. Limitations on Judicial Review

Judicial review is powerful but not unlimited. Courts must respect constitutional boundaries, institutional competence and applicable standards of review.

  • Standing and maintainability: A petitioner must satisfy the applicable requirements, subject to public interest litigation principles.
  • Jurisdiction: Courts must act within their constitutional and statutory authority.
  • Political questions: Courts generally avoid substituting themselves for political decision-makers on matters constitutionally committed to them, while retaining power to examine legality and constitutional compliance.
  • Policy restraint: Courts do not ordinarily replace lawful policy choices with their own preferences.
  • Separation of powers: Judicial directions must remain within the judicial function.
  • Procedural limits: Remedies are governed by rules of procedure, limitation, maintainability and judicial discretion.

18. Judicial Review and Judicial Activism

Judicial review is a constitutional power to examine the legality or constitutionality of State action.

Judicial activism describes a more interventionist approach to the exercise of judicial power, often involving expansive remedies, rights-oriented interpretation or directions addressing governance failures.

The two concepts are related but not identical. Judicial review may be exercised with restraint, while activism concerns the approach and intensity of judicial intervention.

20. Criticisms and Challenges

  • Judicial overreach: Excessive intervention may blur institutional boundaries.
  • Democratic concerns: Unelected judges may be criticised for invalidating decisions made by elected legislatures.
  • Policy complexity: Courts may lack the expertise and information required to manage complex policy programmes.
  • Implementation gaps: Judicial directions may be difficult to implement without administrative capacity and resources.
  • Delay and pendency: Prolonged litigation weakens the effectiveness of constitutional remedies.
  • Inconsistent standards: Different approaches to similar constitutional questions can create uncertainty.
  • Public confidence: Perceptions of selective intervention or inconsistent reasoning may affect institutional legitimacy.

21. Strengthening Judicial Review

  • Maintain judicial independence and institutional integrity.
  • Provide clear, reasoned and consistent constitutional judgments.
  • Respect the constitutional roles of the Legislature and Executive.
  • Use proportionate and appropriately tailored remedies.
  • Strengthen access to justice and legal aid.
  • Improve judicial capacity, research support and court administration.
  • Promote timely compliance with judicial decisions.
  • Develop transparent and principled standards for review of administrative and legislative action.

22. Important Distinctions

  • Judicial review vs judicial activism: Review is the constitutional power; activism describes an interventionist approach to its exercise.
  • Constitutional supremacy vs parliamentary sovereignty: In India, all State institutions are subject to the Constitution.
  • Judicial review vs judicial appeal: Review examines legality or constitutionality; an appeal reconsiders a decision under the applicable appellate framework.
  • Article 32 vs Article 226: Article 32 enforces Fundamental Rights; Article 226 extends to Fundamental Rights and other legal purposes.
  • Legislative competence vs Fundamental Rights review: The former examines whether the legislature had authority to enact a law; the latter examines whether the law violates protected rights.
  • Basic Structure review vs ordinary legislation review: Basic Structure review examines constitutional amendments; ordinary legislation is reviewed on grounds such as rights violations and lack of competence.

23. Integrated Understanding

Judicial review is an essential mechanism for maintaining constitutional supremacy. It protects Fundamental Rights, preserves federalism, enforces legislative competence and prevents arbitrary exercise of public power.

Its legitimacy depends on judicial independence, sound constitutional reasoning, institutional restraint and effective remedies. A strong system of judicial review must protect the Constitution without displacing the democratic responsibilities of other constitutional institutions.
