1. Meaning and Constitutional Context

Judicial activism refers to an approach in which courts interpret and apply the Constitution proactively to protect rights, address constitutional violations and provide effective remedies where ordinary institutional processes have failed.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL) is a judicially developed procedure that relaxes traditional rules of standing in appropriate cases involving public injury, collective rights or disadvantaged groups.

Judicial activism and PIL are related but not identical. Activism concerns the approach adopted by a court; PIL concerns access to judicial remedies in public-interest matters.

2. Constitutional Foundations

The Constitution does not expressly use the term "judicial activism" or establish PIL as a separate jurisdiction. These practices have developed through judicial interpretation of existing constitutional powers.

3. Evolution of Public Interest Litigation in India

Traditional litigation generally required a person to establish a direct personal injury before approaching a court. This could exclude prisoners, bonded labourers, children, informal workers and other disadvantaged groups who lacked the resources or capacity to litigate.

From the late 1970s and 1980s, the Supreme Court relaxed procedural rules in appropriate cases to make constitutional remedies more accessible.

  • Relaxation of locus standi in cases involving public injury.
  • Acceptance of letters and informal communications as writ petitions in suitable circumstances.
  • Appointment of commissions and fact-finding bodies.
  • Use of continuing mandamus to monitor compliance with judicial directions.
  • Expansion of Article 21 jurisprudence to address dignity, livelihood, health, environment and access to justice.

4. Relaxation of Locus Standi

Locus standi refers to the legal standing of a person to approach a court.

In appropriate public-interest cases, courts have permitted a public-spirited individual or organisation to approach the court on behalf of persons who cannot effectively assert their rights themselves.

This approach does not eliminate all standing requirements. Courts still examine the bona fides of the petitioner, the public importance of the issue and the suitability of judicial intervention.

5. Epistolary Jurisdiction

Epistolary jurisdiction refers to the practice of treating letters or informal communications as petitions in appropriate cases.

It developed to overcome procedural barriers faced by vulnerable persons who could not prepare formal pleadings or approach courts through conventional legal processes.

The practice is exceptional and must be balanced against procedural fairness, verification of facts and the rights of affected parties.

6. Continuing Mandamus and Court-Monitored Proceedings

Continuing mandamus is a remedial technique through which a court retains jurisdiction over a matter and periodically examines whether its directions are being implemented.

It has been used in complex matters involving environmental protection, institutional accountability and investigation of serious public concerns.

Its use may be justified where a one-time order is unlikely to secure compliance. However, prolonged judicial monitoring can raise concerns about institutional capacity, separation of powers and administrative responsibility.

7. Judicial Activism in Fundamental Rights Protection

Judicial interpretation has expanded the practical protection available under Fundamental Rights, particularly Article 21.

  • Protection of dignity and humane treatment.
  • Access to legal aid and fair trial.
  • Protection against arbitrary detention.
  • Recognition of environmental dimensions of life and health.
  • Protection of livelihood and shelter within the constitutional framework.
  • Safeguards for prisoners, children, workers and other vulnerable groups.

Such developments have strengthened the transformative potential of the Constitution, while requiring courts to remain attentive to statutory limits and institutional roles.

8. Judicial Activism and Environmental Protection

Public interest litigation has played a significant role in environmental adjudication.

Courts have used constitutional provisions, environmental legislation and principles of environmental law to address pollution, ecological degradation and failures of regulatory enforcement.

  • Article 21 has been interpreted in connection with a clean and healthy environment.
  • Article 48A directs the State to protect and improve the environment.
  • Article 51A(g) places a fundamental duty on citizens to protect the natural environment.
  • The precautionary principle, polluter pays principle and public trust doctrine have informed environmental decisions.

Judicial intervention can strengthen enforcement, but long-term environmental governance requires effective regulators, scientific capacity, public participation and administrative action.

9. Judicial Activism and Governance

Courts have intervened where administrative inaction, corruption, failure to investigate or non-compliance with statutory obligations threatened constitutional rights or public interest.

Such intervention may improve accountability, but courts are not general administrative supervisors. Their role is to enforce legal duties and constitutional standards rather than routinely manage government departments.

10. Judicial Activism and Electoral Democracy

Judicial intervention has contributed to transparency and accountability in electoral processes.

  • Disclosure of relevant criminal antecedents, assets and educational qualifications of candidates under applicable law and judicial directions.
  • Protection of electoral integrity and constitutional limitations.
  • Judicial examination of election-related disputes through constitutionally prescribed procedures.

Article 329(b) and election legislation continue to govern the manner in which elections may be challenged.

11. Judicial Activism and Social Justice

PIL has been used to address structural problems affecting groups with limited access to legal and political institutions.

  • Bonded labour and exploitative working conditions.
  • Prison conditions and custodial safeguards.
  • Child labour and protection of children.
  • Rights of pavement dwellers and persons facing displacement.
  • Access to legal aid and speedy trial.
  • Rights and dignity of persons with disabilities and other marginalised groups.

Judicial remedies can address specific violations, but durable social justice also requires legislation, public services, budgets and effective implementation.

12. Article 142 and Complete Justice

Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to pass such decree or make such order as is necessary for doing complete justice in any cause or matter pending before it.

This power enables the Court to craft effective remedies where ordinary procedural mechanisms are insufficient.

Article 142 is not an unlimited legislative or executive power. It must be exercised consistently with substantive constitutional and statutory provisions and cannot be used to disregard the Constitution.

14. Significance of Judicial Activism and PIL

  • Access to justice: Reduces procedural barriers for persons unable to approach courts individually.
  • Rights protection: Provides remedies where vulnerable groups face systematic violations.
  • Accountability: Can compel public authorities to perform legal duties.
  • Environmental protection: Provides a forum for addressing public environmental harms.
  • Constitutional enforcement: Helps translate constitutional guarantees into practical remedies.
  • Institutional correction: Can address failures where ordinary accountability mechanisms are ineffective.
  • Public participation: Enables civil society to raise issues of wider public concern.

15. Criticisms and Concerns

  • Judicial overreach: Courts may enter policy areas primarily assigned to elected institutions.
  • Separation of powers: Detailed judicial management of administration can blur institutional boundaries.
  • Democratic legitimacy: Courts are not directly elected and must respect the constitutional role of representative institutions.
  • Institutional competence: Courts may lack technical, financial and administrative expertise for complex policy decisions.
  • Unequal access: Litigation may be dominated by well-resourced petitioners and organisations.
  • Frivolous litigation: Publicity-oriented petitions can consume scarce judicial time.
  • Implementation difficulties: Judicial orders may not produce durable outcomes without administrative capacity.
  • Inconsistent intervention: Different levels of judicial intervention may create uncertainty in governance.

16. Misuse of Public Interest Litigation

PIL may be misused to settle private disputes, pursue political objectives, obtain publicity or obstruct lawful administrative action.

In State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010), the Supreme Court emphasised the need to verify the credentials of the petitioner, examine the correctness of the information and ensure that the litigation genuinely serves public interest.

Appropriate safeguards include:

  • Preliminary scrutiny of the petitioner's bona fides.
  • Verification of factual allegations.
  • Assessment of whether the matter genuinely affects public interest.
  • Costs or other measures against demonstrably abusive litigation, where justified.
  • Protection of genuine petitions brought on behalf of disadvantaged groups.

The distinction depends on the constitutional basis, nature of the remedy, legal duty involved and degree of institutional intrusion. Strong judicial intervention is not automatically overreach.

18. Relationship with Separation of Powers

The Indian Constitution follows functional separation with checks and balances rather than a rigid separation of all functions.

Courts may enforce legal duties, invalidate unconstitutional action and protect rights. However, policy formulation, budget allocation and day-to-day administration generally remain the responsibility of the Legislature and Executive within constitutional limits.

Effective judicial activism must therefore combine constitutional courage with institutional restraint.

19. Judicial Activism and Democratic Governance

Judicial intervention can strengthen democracy when it protects constitutional rights, electoral integrity, institutional independence and the rule of law.

However, courts cannot become substitutes for representative institutions. Durable governance improvements require democratic deliberation, accountable administration, effective legislation and citizen participation.

20. Reform Priorities

  • Develop clear and consistent standards for PIL admission and scrutiny.
  • Protect access to courts for disadvantaged groups and genuine public causes.
  • Use expert committees and evidence-based processes in technically complex matters.
  • Frame remedies that are legally grounded, proportionate and capable of implementation.
  • Monitor compliance where necessary without assuming routine executive functions.
  • Strengthen regulatory institutions and administrative accountability.
  • Improve legal aid, procedural accessibility and court capacity.
  • Promote judicial restraint where matters involve lawful policy choices and institutional competence.

21. Important Distinctions

  • Judicial activism vs judicial review: Review is the constitutional power to examine legality; activism describes a proactive approach to exercising judicial power.
  • PIL vs ordinary litigation: PIL concerns a genuine public cause and may relax traditional standing requirements; ordinary litigation generally concerns the parties' own legal interests.
  • Judicial activism vs judicial overreach: Activism can enforce constitutional duties; overreach occurs when intervention crosses legitimate institutional boundaries.
  • Article 32 vs Article 226: Both support constitutional remedies, but Article 226 has a wider subject-matter scope.
  • Continuing mandamus vs ordinary mandamus: Continuing mandamus involves retained judicial supervision of compliance; ordinary mandamus directs performance of a public duty.

22. Integrated Understanding

Judicial activism and PIL have widened access to constitutional justice and strengthened protection of rights, particularly for disadvantaged communities.

Their legitimacy depends on bona fide public interest, constitutional grounding, procedural fairness and institutional restraint. The objective is not judicial governance but effective enforcement of constitutional obligations within a democratic system.
