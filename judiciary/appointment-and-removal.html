1. Constitutional Framework

Judicial appointments and removal are central to the independence, legitimacy and accountability of the Indian judiciary. The Constitution establishes appointment procedures, qualifications, tenure, service safeguards and a special removal process for judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.

Key constitutional provisions include:

  • Article 124: Appointment and removal framework for Supreme Court judges.
  • Article 217: Appointment and conditions of office of High Court judges.
  • Article 218: Application of specified Supreme Court judge provisions to High Court judges.
  • Article 222: Transfer of High Court judges.
  • Articles 223–224A: Acting, additional and retired judges of High Courts.
  • Articles 125 and 221: Salaries and allowances.
  • Articles 121 and 211: Restrictions on discussion of judicial conduct in legislatures.
  • Article 50: Separation of the judiciary from the executive as a Directive Principle.

2. Constitutional Objectives of Judicial Appointments

The appointment framework must balance several constitutional objectives:

  • Judicial independence: Judges must be protected from improper political or executive influence.
  • Institutional competence: Appointments should ensure legal knowledge, judicial ability and professional integrity.
  • Constitutional balance: The process must respect the roles assigned to constitutional institutions.
  • Transparency: The process should command public confidence and permit meaningful scrutiny.
  • Diversity and representation: The judiciary should reflect the breadth of Indian society and legal experience.
  • Timeliness: Vacancies should be addressed without avoidable delay.

3. Appointment of Supreme Court Judges

Article 124(2) provides that every Supreme Court judge is appointed by the President by warrant under hand and seal after constitutionally prescribed consultation.

The Chief Justice of India is appointed by the President. By established convention, the senior-most judge of the Supreme Court considered fit to hold the office is appointed as Chief Justice of India.

Other Supreme Court judges are appointed through the collegium recommendation process, which emerged from judicial interpretation of the constitutional expression "consultation".

4. Appointment of High Court Judges

Article 217(1) provides for appointment of High Court judges by the President after consultation with:

  • The Chief Justice of India.
  • The Governor of the State.
  • The Chief Justice of the High Court concerned, in the case of appointment of a judge other than its Chief Justice.

The recommendation process is governed by the collegium system and the applicable Memorandum of Procedure.

The Governor's consultation is constitutionally prescribed. The judicial collegium, however, has primacy in the recommendation process under the law declared by the Supreme Court.

5. Evolution of the Collegium System

The collegium system was not expressly set out in the original text of the Constitution. It developed through judicial interpretation of the appointment provisions.

CaseYearSignificance
S.P. Gupta v. Union of India — First Judges Case 1981 Held that consultation did not mean concurrence and gave primacy to the Executive.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India — Second Judges Case 1993 Overruled the earlier approach and established judicial primacy and the collegium system.
In re Special Reference No. 1 of 1998 — Third Judges Case 1998 Clarified the collegium's composition and consultation process.
Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India — Fourth Judges Case 2015 Invalidated the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC framework.

6. Composition of the Collegium

The composition differs according to the appointment being considered.

  • Supreme Court appointments: The Chief Justice of India and the four senior-most puisne judges of the Supreme Court.
  • High Court appointments: The Chief Justice of India and the two senior-most puisne judges of the Supreme Court consider recommendations for High Court appointments.
  • High Court-level recommendation: The Chief Justice of the concerned High Court initiates the proposal in consultation with the two senior-most puisne judges of that High Court.

The collegium process is not a formal constitutional body created by a separate constitutional article. It is a judicially evolved institutional mechanism.

7. Memorandum of Procedure

The Memorandum of Procedure (MoP) provides the administrative framework for processing judicial appointment and transfer recommendations.

Broadly, the process involves:

  1. Initiation of a proposal by the appropriate judicial authority.
  2. Consideration of professional competence, integrity, experience and suitability.
  3. Consultation and evaluation through the relevant collegium.
  4. Processing by the Union Government, including the required background and intelligence inputs.
  5. Consideration of the recommendation by the Supreme Court collegium where applicable.
  6. Formal appointment by the President.

The MoP is an administrative framework operating within the constitutional and judicially interpreted appointment system. It does not independently replace constitutional provisions.

8. Reconsideration and Executive Role

The Union Government may return a collegium recommendation for reconsideration, giving reasons or relevant information.

If the collegium reiterates its recommendation in accordance with the applicable procedure, the established convention and judicial framework require the appointment to be processed accordingly.

This limited reconsideration mechanism allows institutional scrutiny while preserving the judicial primacy recognised by the Supreme Court.

9. Qualifications for Supreme Court Judges

Article 124(3) requires a person to be a citizen of India and satisfy one of the following:

  • Have been a judge of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least five years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years; or
  • Be, in the opinion of the President, a distinguished jurist.

10. Qualifications for High Court Judges

Article 217(2) requires a person to be a citizen of India and satisfy one of the following:

  • Have held a judicial office in the territory of India for at least ten years; or
  • Have been an advocate of a High Court or of two or more such courts in succession for at least ten years.

The Constitution does not prescribe a minimum age for appointment to either the Supreme Court or a High Court.

Judges may resign by writing addressed to the President. They may also be removed through the constitutional process on the grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity.

12. Transfer of High Court Judges

Article 222 empowers the President to transfer a judge from one High Court to another after consultation with the Chief Justice of India.

The transfer framework has been interpreted in the context of judicial independence and the collegium system.

Important principles include:

  • Transfer should serve a relevant public and institutional purpose.
  • The Chief Justice of India plays a central role in the consultation process.
  • The affected judge's views may be considered in accordance with the applicable procedure.
  • Transfers should not be used as a punitive device or as an instrument of improper influence.

13. National Judicial Appointments Commission

The 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014, introduced the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC) framework to replace the collegium system.

The proposed Commission consisted of:

  • The Chief Justice of India as Chairperson.
  • Two senior-most Supreme Court judges.
  • The Union Minister in charge of Law and Justice.
  • Two eminent persons selected by a committee comprising the Prime Minister, the Chief Justice of India and the Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha or the leader of the single largest opposition party, as specified by the amendment.

The NJAC Act, 2014, provided the statutory framework for the Commission.

In the Fourth Judges Case (2015), the Supreme Court struck down the 99th Constitutional Amendment and the NJAC Act as unconstitutional, holding that judicial independence is part of the Basic Structure.

14. Arguments Supporting the Collegium System

  • Protects judicial independence from direct political control.
  • Places substantial appointment responsibility with experienced judges.
  • Reduces the possibility of appointments being driven solely by political considerations.
  • Supports continuity in constitutional interpretation and judicial standards.
  • Reflects the judicial primacy recognised in the Judges Cases.

15. Criticisms of the Collegium System

  • Limited transparency: The criteria and deliberations are not always sufficiently visible.
  • Opacity: The process can be difficult for the public to understand and evaluate.
  • Accountability concerns: The system has limited external institutional scrutiny.
  • Representation: Diversity across gender, caste, region and professional background remains an important concern.
  • Perceived favouritism: Lack of clearly articulated criteria can create concerns about personal preferences and networks.
  • Vacancies and delay: Repeated exchanges between institutions can slow appointments.
  • Limited public participation: The system does not provide a structured public process for assessing institutional priorities.

16. Removal of Supreme Court and High Court Judges

The Constitution provides a common substantive removal framework for Supreme Court and High Court judges.

Articles 124(4) and 124(5), read with Article 218, govern removal of High Court judges.

The permissible grounds are:

  • Proved misbehaviour.
  • Incapacity.

The Constitution does not permit removal merely because of disagreement with a judgment or an unpopular judicial decision.

17. Parliamentary Removal Procedure

A judge can be removed only after each House of Parliament passes an address in the same session with the prescribed special majority.

  1. A motion is initiated in either House, subject to the statutory notice requirements.
  2. The Speaker or Chairman decides whether to admit the motion.
  3. If admitted, an inquiry committee is constituted under the Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968.
  4. The committee investigates the allegations and gives the judge an opportunity to respond.
  5. The committee submits its findings to the relevant parliamentary authority.
  6. If the committee finds the judge guilty of proved misbehaviour or incapacity, the motion proceeds for parliamentary consideration.
  7. Each House must pass the address with the required special majority.
  8. The President issues the order of removal.

18. Special Majority Requirement

Under Article 124(4), the address must be supported in each House by:

  • A majority of the total membership of that House; and
  • A majority of not less than two-thirds of members present and voting.

Both conditions must be satisfied. The address must be presented to the President in the same session of Parliament.

The requirement is intentionally stringent to prevent arbitrary removal and protect judicial independence.

19. Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968

Article 124(5) authorises Parliament to regulate by law the procedure for presentation of an address and for investigation and proof of misbehaviour or incapacity.

The Judges (Inquiry) Act, 1968 provides the statutory framework for the inquiry process.

It establishes the mechanism for considering a removal motion and constituting an inquiry committee to investigate the charges.

20. Resignation, Retirement and Removal Distinguished

  • Resignation: Voluntary relinquishment of office by writing addressed to the President.
  • Retirement: Cessation of office upon attaining the constitutionally prescribed age.
  • Removal: Compulsory termination of office through the special constitutional procedure.

Resignation or retirement does not amount to removal through the parliamentary process.

21. Historical Removal Proceedings

The high constitutional threshold for removal has meant that successful removal of a judge by parliamentary address has been extremely rare.

  • Justice V. Ramaswami: The removal motion was debated in the Lok Sabha in 1993 but did not secure the required majority.
  • Justice Soumitra Sen: The Rajya Sabha adopted a removal motion in 2011. He resigned before the Lok Sabha completed consideration.
  • Justice P.D. Dinakaran: Removal proceedings were initiated, but he resigned before the process was completed.

These proceedings illustrate the distinction between initiating an inquiry or parliamentary motion and completing constitutional removal.

22. Judicial Independence and Accountability

Judicial independence does not mean absence of accountability. It means that accountability must operate without compromising impartial adjudication or constitutional independence.

Relevant mechanisms include:

  • Constitutional removal procedure.
  • Judicial conduct standards and ethical principles.
  • In-house procedures for addressing complaints.
  • Contempt jurisdiction and protection of judicial proceedings.
  • Publicly available judgments and reasoned decisions.
  • Disclosure and institutional transparency measures, subject to legitimate confidentiality.

23. Reform Priorities

  • Publish clearer and more consistent criteria for judicial appointments.
  • Improve transparency in collegium recommendations and reasons, while protecting legitimate confidentiality.
  • Ensure timely processing of recommendations and appointments.
  • Promote diversity across gender, caste, region and professional background.
  • Strengthen institutional research and assessment of candidates.
  • Clarify the Memorandum of Procedure through consultation among constitutional stakeholders.
  • Maintain judicial independence while improving accountability.
  • Develop credible, fair and confidential procedures for addressing judicial misconduct.
  • Improve public understanding of the appointment and removal framework.

26. Important Distinctions

  • Appointment vs transfer: Appointment fills a judicial office; transfer changes the High Court in which a judge serves.
  • Consultation vs concurrence: The constitutional text uses consultation, but judicial interpretation has developed the collegium system with judicial primacy.
  • Collegium vs NJAC: The collegium is the judicially evolved appointment mechanism currently in operation; the NJAC framework was invalidated in 2015.
  • Eligibility vs suitability: Eligibility concerns constitutional qualifications; suitability involves assessment of professional and personal attributes.
  • Resignation vs removal: Resignation is voluntary; removal requires the constitutional parliamentary process.
  • Inquiry vs removal: An inquiry investigates allegations; constitutional removal requires the prescribed parliamentary addresses and Presidential order.

27. Integrated Understanding

The appointment and removal framework reflects a constitutional balance between independence and accountability. The collegium system seeks to protect judicial appointments from direct political control, while the constitutional removal process protects judges from arbitrary dismissal.

At the same time, opacity, delays, vacancies and representation concerns demonstrate the need for institutional reform. The objective should be a judiciary that is independent, competent, diverse, transparent and accountable within constitutional limits.
