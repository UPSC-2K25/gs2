1. Consumer protection as a governance responsibility

Consumer protection is an essential part of a fair and functioning market economy. Consumers often have less information, bargaining power and technical knowledge than manufacturers, service providers and large digital platforms. Defective products, misleading advertisements, unfair contracts, unsafe services and exploitative commercial practices can therefore produce serious economic and social harm.

Consumer protection is not limited to private disputes about refunds. It concerns public health, product safety, market transparency, fair competition, digital rights and access to justice. It also supports trust in markets and encourages responsible business conduct.

India's consumer protection framework combines statutory consumer rights, specialised adjudicatory commissions, a regulatory authority, product-liability provisions, mediation and rules governing e-commerce and unfair trade practices.

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 replaced the Consumer Protection Act, 1986. It modernised the framework to address e-commerce, product liability, misleading advertisements and the need for a central regulatory authority.

2. Constitutional foundations

Consumer protection is supported by the constitutional commitments to social and economic justice, public health and fair economic governance. The Constitution does not create a separate fundamental right called the right to consumer protection, but several provisions inform the legislative framework.

  • Article 14: Protects equality and guards against arbitrary State action in regulatory and licensing decisions.
  • Article 19(1)(g): Protects the freedom to practise a profession or carry on an occupation, trade or business, subject to reasonable restrictions under Article 19(6).
  • Article 21: Protects life and personal liberty; unsafe products, hazardous services and serious health risks may engage the wider constitutional protection of life and dignity.
  • Article 38: Directs the State to promote welfare and a social order informed by justice.
  • Article 39: Provides policy guidance concerning material resources, economic justice and protection against concentration of wealth and means of production.
  • Article 47: Directs the State to raise nutrition levels and improve public health.

Consumer protection therefore reflects a constitutional balance: markets should remain open to legitimate enterprise, but commercial freedom is subject to laws protecting consumers, public health and fair dealing.

3. Evolution of consumer protection legislation

The Consumer Protection Act, 1986 introduced a specialised, relatively inexpensive and speedy system for consumer dispute redressal. It recognised consumer rights and established District, State and National consumer fora.

Economic liberalisation, growth of services, complex supply chains and digital commerce exposed limitations in the older framework. Consumers increasingly purchased products online, relied on digital intermediaries and faced misleading endorsements, opaque pricing and cross-border sellers.

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 responded by creating the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA), recognising product liability, providing for mediation and strengthening provisions concerning misleading advertisements and unfair trade practices.

The new framework preserves the three-tier consumer dispute redressal system but uses the term Consumer Commissions. It also gives consumers greater flexibility in filing complaints and provides a statutory basis for action against certain forms of unfair market conduct.

4. Consumer Protection Act, 2019: objectives and scope

The Act seeks to protect consumer interests and establish authorities for timely and effective administration and settlement of consumer disputes. It applies to goods and services, subject to statutory definitions and exclusions.

The Act covers transactions involving offline and online purchases, electronic means, teleshopping, direct selling and multi-level marketing, subject to the applicable provisions and rules.

Its main institutional components are:

  • Central Consumer Protection Council, State Consumer Protection Councils and District Consumer Protection Councils.
  • Central Consumer Protection Authority.
  • District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions.
  • State Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions.
  • National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission.
  • Mediation cells attached to consumer commissions.

The Act also provides for product liability, offences relating to adulterated and spurious goods, regulation of misleading advertisements and rules for electronic commerce and direct selling.

5. Who is a consumer?

The Act defines a consumer as a person who buys goods or hires or avails services for consideration paid, promised, partly paid and partly promised, or under a system of deferred payment. It includes an approved user of goods and a beneficiary of services, subject to the statutory conditions.

The definition excludes a person who obtains goods for resale or for a commercial purpose. However, goods bought and used exclusively for earning a livelihood by means of self-employment are covered by the statutory exception.

Whether a transaction is for a commercial purpose depends on its facts and circumstances. A small entrepreneur personally operating a piece of equipment to earn a livelihood may fall within the self-employment exception, while a large commercial enterprise purchasing equipment for business operations may not.

The Act also recognises that a person may be a consumer of services. Banking, insurance, transport, telecommunications, housing construction and other services may fall within the framework where the statutory requirements are satisfied. Services rendered free of charge or under a contract of personal service are excluded under the definition of service.

6. Consumer rights

The Act recognises six principal consumer rights:

  1. Right to safety: Protection against marketing of goods and services that are hazardous to life and property.
  2. Right to be informed: Access to information about quality, quantity, potency, purity, standard and price so as to protect consumers against unfair trade practices.
  3. Right to choose: Access, wherever possible, to a variety of goods and services at competitive prices.
  4. Right to be heard: Assurance that consumer interests will receive due consideration at appropriate forums.
  5. Right to seek redressal: Access to remedies against unfair or restrictive trade practices and exploitation.
  6. Right to consumer awareness: The right to acquire knowledge and skills necessary to make informed consumer choices.

These rights are supported by the Consumer Protection Councils, the CCPA, consumer commissions and public awareness programmes. Their realisation also depends on accurate labelling, accessible information, effective standards and fair commercial practices.

7. Three-tier consumer dispute redressal system

The Act provides for District, State and National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions. These are quasi-judicial bodies established to decide consumer disputes and grant statutory remedies.

7.1 District Commission

A District Commission is established by the State Government in each district or as otherwise permitted by the Act. It is the first-level forum for consumer disputes falling within its pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction.

Under the Consumer Protection (Jurisdiction of the District Commission, the State Commission and the National Commission) Rules, 2021, the District Commission has pecuniary jurisdiction where the value of goods or services paid as consideration does not exceed fifty lakh rupees.

7.2 State Commission

A State Commission is established at the State level. It exercises original jurisdiction over complaints within the prescribed pecuniary range, hears appeals against orders of District Commissions and may exercise the statutory revisional jurisdiction.

Under the 2021 Jurisdiction Rules, the State Commission has pecuniary jurisdiction where the value of goods or services paid as consideration exceeds fifty lakh rupees but does not exceed two crore rupees.

7.3 National Commission

The National Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission (NCDRC) is the national-level consumer adjudicatory body. It exercises original jurisdiction over complaints exceeding the prescribed pecuniary threshold, hears appeals against orders of State Commissions and exercises revisional powers under the Act.

Under the 2021 Jurisdiction Rules, the National Commission has pecuniary jurisdiction where the value of goods or services paid as consideration exceeds two crore rupees.

The thresholds are based on the value of consideration paid for the goods or services, not simply the compensation amount claimed. The current rules should be checked when determining the correct forum because pecuniary limits have changed over time.

8. Territorial jurisdiction and place of filing

The Act provides consumer-friendly rules for territorial jurisdiction. A complaint may be instituted before the commission within whose jurisdiction the opposite party resides or carries on business, where the cause of action arises wholly or in part, or where the complainant resides or personally works for gain, subject to the statutory conditions.

This is a significant improvement for consumers who previously faced the burden of travelling to the seller's or service provider's location. It is particularly relevant to online purchases and services supplied by businesses operating across States.

Consumers should identify the appropriate commission by considering both pecuniary and territorial jurisdiction. Filing in the wrong forum may result in procedural delay and transfer or return of the complaint.

9. Filing a consumer complaint

A consumer complaint may be filed by a consumer, a recognised consumer association, one or more consumers with the same interest, the Central Government, the State Government, the CCPA or a legal heir or representative in the circumstances specified by the Act.

The Act permits electronic filing and provides for payment of the prescribed fee. The consumer should ordinarily preserve evidence such as invoices, payment records, warranties, correspondence, screenshots, product descriptions, service agreements and communications with the seller or service provider.

A complaint should identify the parties, set out the relevant facts, specify the alleged defect, deficiency or unfair practice and state the relief sought. It should be supported by documents and a clear chronology.

The limitation period is ordinarily two years from the date on which the cause of action arises. A commission may entertain a complaint after this period if sufficient cause for delay is shown and reasons are recorded in accordance with the Act.

Consumers should distinguish a consumer complaint from a general grievance submitted to a company, a sector regulator or the National Consumer Helpline. These channels may assist resolution but do not necessarily replace the statutory right to approach a Consumer Commission.

10. Remedies available to consumers

Where a commission finds that a complaint is established, it may grant relief permitted by the Act. Depending on the case, this may include:

  • Removal of defects in goods.
  • Replacement of defective goods with new goods of similar description free from defect.
  • Return of the price or charges paid by the complainant.
  • Compensation for loss or injury suffered due to negligence of the opposite party.
  • Compensation in appropriate cases for product liability.
  • Discontinuance of unfair or restrictive trade practices.
  • Stopping the sale of hazardous goods and withdrawal of hazardous goods from sale.
  • Stopping the manufacture of hazardous goods or services.
  • Corrective advertisement to neutralise the effect of misleading advertisements.
  • Payment of adequate costs to parties.

Relief must be connected to the statutory grounds and evidence. Consumer commissions are not general compensation tribunals for every form of economic loss or contractual disagreement.

11. Appeals and enforcement

The Act provides a hierarchy of appeals from District Commissions to State Commissions, from State Commissions to the National Commission and from the National Commission to the Supreme Court in the circumstances specified by the statute.

Appeals are subject to limitation periods and, where applicable, statutory deposit requirements. These conditions are intended to balance access to appellate review with the need to prevent dilatory litigation.

Orders of consumer commissions are enforceable under the Act. Failure to comply with an order may attract statutory consequences. The effectiveness of consumer justice depends not only on obtaining a favourable order but also on ensuring that refunds, replacements, compensation and corrective directions are implemented.

12. Central Consumer Protection Authority

12.1 Establishment and purpose

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 established the Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA). Unlike consumer commissions, which adjudicate individual disputes, the CCPA is a regulatory authority concerned with protection and enforcement of consumer rights as a class.

Its mandate includes preventing unfair trade practices, addressing false or misleading advertisements and protecting consumers from violations of consumer rights. It can act in matters affecting consumers generally rather than merely deciding an individual claim for compensation.

12.2 Powers and functions

The CCPA may inquire or investigate violations of consumer rights, unfair trade practices and misleading advertisements. It may act on complaints, references and suo motu information within its statutory mandate.

Its powers include directing recall of dangerous or unsafe goods, withdrawal of unsafe services, reimbursement of prices paid by consumers and discontinuance of practices prejudicial to consumer interests. It may issue directions and impose penalties in relation to misleading advertisements in accordance with the Act.

The CCPA may also refer matters to other regulators or authorities where their statutory jurisdiction is engaged. Coordination is particularly important where consumer harm overlaps with food safety, financial services, telecommunications, medicines or competition issues.

12.3 Misleading advertisements

The Act provides a framework for action against advertisements that falsely describe products or services, give false guarantees, convey misleading representations or deliberately conceal important information.

The CCPA may impose statutory penalties on manufacturers or endorsers for misleading advertisements, subject to the Act. It may also prohibit an endorser from making endorsements for a specified period in circumstances provided by law.

Endorsers are expected to exercise due diligence regarding the claims they make. The legal framework is particularly important in the context of celebrity endorsements, influencer marketing, online reviews and health-related product claims.

13. Consumer Protection Councils

The Act provides for Central, State and District Consumer Protection Councils. These are advisory bodies intended to promote and protect consumer rights.

The Central Council advises on the promotion and protection of consumer rights at the national level. State and District Councils perform corresponding advisory roles within their jurisdictions.

Consumer Protection Councils should not be confused with Consumer Commissions. Councils are advisory bodies concerned with policy and awareness; commissions are quasi-judicial bodies that decide consumer disputes.

The distinction between policy advice, regulatory enforcement and adjudication is central to understanding the institutional structure under the 2019 Act.

14. Product liability

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 introduced a statutory product-liability framework. A product manufacturer, product service provider or product seller may be liable to compensate for harm caused by a defective product or deficient service in the circumstances set out by the Act.

Product liability recognises that harm may arise at different stages of the supply chain. A manufacturer may be responsible for manufacturing defects, design defects, deviation from manufacturing specifications, failure to conform to an express warranty or inadequate instructions or warnings, subject to statutory conditions.

A product service provider may be liable where the service relating to the product is deficient or negligent in the manner specified by law. A product seller may also be liable in specified circumstances, including where it exercises substantial control, makes an independent warranty or alters or modifies the product in a manner that causes harm.

The Act contains defences and exclusions. Liability must therefore be assessed by examining the statutory elements, the nature of the product, the causal connection with the harm and the conduct of the parties.

Product liability is important for consumer safety because it creates incentives for manufacturers and sellers to invest in quality control, accurate warnings, traceability and responsible after-sales support.

15. Mediation in consumer disputes

The 2019 Act provides for mediation as an alternative dispute-resolution mechanism. A consumer commission may refer a dispute to mediation where it appears that there is a possibility of settlement and the parties consent in accordance with the statutory framework.

Mediation can reduce litigation costs, preserve commercial relationships and produce practical settlements. It is especially useful in disputes involving refunds, repairs, service deficiencies and other matters where parties can reach a mutually acceptable outcome.

Mediation is not suitable for every dispute. Cases involving serious public safety concerns, systemic unfair trade practices or issues requiring authoritative legal determination may require formal adjudication or regulatory action.

Mediation cells must operate with trained mediators, impartial procedures, confidentiality and clear safeguards against coercion. A settlement should reflect informed consent rather than pressure on a consumer to accept an inadequate remedy.

16. E-commerce and digital consumer protection

Digital commerce has expanded consumer choice but has also created new risks. Consumers may not know the identity of the seller, the source of a product, the total price, the applicable return policy or the method for resolving complaints.

The Consumer Protection (E-Commerce) Rules, 2020 impose obligations on e-commerce entities and sellers operating through digital platforms. The rules address disclosure of information, grievance redressal, unfair trade practices and specified responsibilities of marketplace and inventory-based entities.

Important areas of concern include:

  • Accurate disclosure of seller identity and contact information.
  • Clear information about price, payment, delivery, return, refund and warranty conditions.
  • Prevention of misleading product descriptions and manipulated consumer reviews.
  • Transparent treatment of cancellation charges and refund requests.
  • Accessible grievance officers and time-bound responses.
  • Prevention of unfair discrimination or manipulation of consumer choice.
  • Protection against counterfeit, unsafe or misrepresented products.

Digital platforms may act as marketplaces rather than direct sellers, but their role in shaping visibility, information and transactions creates important consumer-protection responsibilities under applicable law.

17. Consumer protection and competition policy

Consumer protection and competition law are related but distinct. Consumer law focuses on consumer rights, unfair trade practices, defective goods, deficient services and remedies. Competition law addresses anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominant position and combinations that may cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition.

A market with effective competition may offer consumers more choice, better prices and improved quality. However, competition alone does not prevent unsafe products, deceptive advertising or unfair contract terms.

The Competition Commission of India and the CCPA may therefore have complementary roles in cases involving digital markets, platform practices, market power and consumer harm. Coordination should avoid conflicting directions and gaps in enforcement.

18. Important judicial principles

Lucknow Development Authority v. M.K. Gupta (1994): The Supreme Court interpreted consumer protection broadly in the context of public authorities providing services and recognised the importance of compensation for harassment and deficiency in service.

Indian Medical Association v. V.P. Shantha (1995): The Court considered the application of consumer law to medical services and clarified the circumstances in which services rendered by medical practitioners fall within the statutory framework.

Spring Meadows Hospital v. Harjol Ahluwalia (1998): The Court addressed liability for medical negligence and the rights of beneficiaries of services under consumer law.

National Seeds Corporation Ltd. v. M. Madhusudhan Reddy (2012): The Supreme Court considered remedies available to farmers who purchased defective seeds and emphasised the importance of consumer remedies in agricultural contexts.

Imperia Structures Ltd. v. Anil Patni (2020): The Court considered the relationship between consumer remedies and other statutory forums in the context of real-estate disputes. The availability of a remedy under another statute does not necessarily extinguish the consumer remedy unless the law so provides.

These decisions illustrate that consumer law is intended to provide an accessible additional remedy and should be interpreted in a manner that gives practical effect to consumer rights, subject to statutory limits.

19. Challenges in consumer dispute redressal

19.1 Backlog and delay

Consumer commissions may face large caseloads, vacancies and procedural delays. The promise of inexpensive and speedy justice is weakened when cases remain pending for long periods.

19.2 Unequal bargaining power

Large businesses may have greater legal and financial resources than individual consumers. Complex documentation, technical evidence and repeated proceedings can discourage consumers from pursuing legitimate claims.

19.3 Digital asymmetry

Consumers may lack access to platform records, algorithmic decisions, seller information and transaction data. Effective redressal requires transparent disclosure and preservation of relevant digital evidence.

19.4 Product safety and counterfeit goods

Complex supply chains make it difficult to identify responsible manufacturers, importers, sellers and intermediaries. Product traceability, testing and recall mechanisms are essential.

19.5 Awareness and access

Rural consumers, elderly persons, persons with disabilities and people with limited digital literacy may be unaware of their rights or unable to navigate electronic filing systems.

19.6 Enforcement of orders

Even a successful complaint may not provide meaningful relief if refunds, replacements or compensation are not implemented promptly.

19.7 Misleading advertising and influencer marketing

Social media endorsements, disguised advertisements, fabricated reviews and exaggerated health or financial claims can influence consumers rapidly. Monitoring and enforcement require technical expertise and cooperation with digital platforms.

20. Reform agenda

  • Fill vacancies in District, State and National Commissions and provide adequate support staff.
  • Improve digital filing and case-management systems while retaining accessible offline options.
  • Strengthen enforcement of commission orders and monitor compliance.
  • Improve consumer awareness through schools, local bodies, community organisations and public information campaigns.
  • Develop stronger product-testing, traceability and recall mechanisms.
  • Ensure that e-commerce platforms provide transparent seller information and effective grievance redressal.
  • Strengthen action against misleading advertisements and deceptive influencer endorsements.
  • Expand mediation for suitable disputes without undermining consumer choice or access to adjudication.
  • Improve coordination between the CCPA, sector regulators, standards authorities and competition authorities.
  • Publish accessible data on disposal, pendency, compliance and consumer outcomes.
  • Provide multilingual and disability-accessible complaint systems.
  • Promote fair contract terms and clear disclosure of fees, risks and cancellation conditions.

21. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 reflects the changing nature of markets and the need to protect consumers in both physical and digital transactions. It strengthens the institutional framework through the CCPA, product liability, mediation and e-commerce regulation while retaining the three-tier consumer dispute redressal system.

The principal challenge is implementation. Consumer commissions require adequate capacity, consumers need accessible procedures, and regulatory authorities must respond to new forms of digital market conduct. Product safety and misleading advertising require coordinated enforcement rather than reliance only on individual complaints.

Consumer protection should be viewed as an element of good governance and social justice. A fair market requires informed consumers, responsible businesses, effective regulators and accessible remedies. The objective is not to restrict legitimate enterprise but to ensure that commercial freedom operates consistently with safety, transparency and accountability.

23. Key terms

Consumer: A person who buys goods or hires or avails services for consideration, subject to statutory inclusions and exclusions.

Defect: A fault, imperfection or shortcoming in the quality, quantity, potency, purity or standard of goods, as defined by law.

Deficiency: A fault, imperfection, shortcoming or inadequacy in the quality, nature or manner of performance of a service.

Unfair trade practice: A commercial practice involving specified unfair or deceptive methods, representations or conduct under the Act.

Product liability: Legal responsibility of a manufacturer, product service provider or product seller to compensate for harm caused by a defective product or deficient product-related service in the circumstances specified by law.

CCPA: Central Consumer Protection Authority, the regulatory authority established under the 2019 Act.
