1. Meaning and Democratic Significance

Information commissions are statutory institutions established under the Right to Information Act, 2005 to safeguard the effective exercise of the right to information and promote transparency and accountability in public authorities.

The right to information enables citizens to scrutinise government decisions, examine the use of public resources, identify administrative failures and participate meaningfully in democratic governance. Information commissions provide an independent statutory forum when access is denied or the obligations under the Act are not fulfilled.

The Central Information Commission (CIC) and State Information Commissions (SICs) are not constitutional bodies. They derive their authority from the Right to Information Act, 2005 and must act within the powers and procedures prescribed by that law.

2. Constitutional Foundations

The Constitution does not expressly mention a general right to information in a separate article. The Supreme Court has, however, recognised access to information as an important dimension of freedom of speech and expression under Article 19(1)(a).

  • Article 19(1)(a): Freedom of speech and expression supports the citizen's ability to receive information necessary for informed participation in public affairs.
  • Article 14: Transparency helps prevent arbitrary decision-making and supports equality before the law.
  • Article 21: Access to information can be important where disclosure is necessary to protect life, liberty or dignity, subject to the statutory framework.
  • Article 32 and Article 226: Constitutional remedies remain available against unlawful State action, including violations of legal and fundamental rights.
  • Article 51A: The duties of citizens, including promoting harmony and developing scientific temper, are supported by informed public participation.

The RTI Act converts the constitutional value of informed citizenship into a detailed statutory regime. It creates enforceable duties for public authorities, establishes procedures for requests and appeals, and provides penalties for specified failures.

3. Right to Information Act, 2005: Framework

The Right to Information Act, 2005 replaced the earlier Freedom of Information Act, 2002 and created a practical framework for citizens to obtain information held by or under the control of public authorities.

The Act applies to public authorities established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by legislation, or by government notification or order, and includes bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by government and non-government organisations substantially financed directly or indirectly by public funds.

Important provisions include:

  • Section 3: Confers the right to information on all citizens.
  • Section 4: Requires proactive disclosure and maintenance of records by public authorities.
  • Sections 5–7: Provide for Public Information Officers, requests and disposal of applications.
  • Sections 8–11: Deal with exemptions, severability and third-party information.
  • Sections 12–14: Establish and regulate the Central Information Commission.
  • Sections 15–17: Establish and regulate State Information Commissions.
  • Sections 18–20: Provide for complaints, appeals and penalties.
  • Section 22: Gives the Act overriding effect over inconsistent provisions of the Official Secrets Act, 1923 and other laws, to the extent of inconsistency.
  • Section 24: Provides exemptions for specified intelligence and security organisations, subject to statutory exceptions.

4. Central Information Commission: Composition

Section 12 provides for the constitution of the CIC by the Central Government through notification in the Official Gazette.

The Commission consists of:

  • A Chief Information Commissioner.
  • Such number of Central Information Commissioners as may be considered necessary, subject to the statutory maximum of ten.

The Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee consisting of:

  1. The Prime Minister, who is the Chairperson of the committee.
  2. The Leader of Opposition in the Lok Sabha.
  3. A Union Cabinet Minister nominated by the Prime Minister.

The Act provides that where the Leader of Opposition has not been recognised, the leader of the single largest group in opposition in the Lok Sabha is treated as the Leader of Opposition for this purpose.

Members must be persons of eminence in public life with wide knowledge and experience in fields such as law, science and technology, social service, management, journalism, mass media or administration and governance. They must satisfy the statutory disqualifications concerning legislative membership, office of profit, political-party association, business and profession.

5. State Information Commissions

Section 15 provides for the constitution of a State Information Commission by the State Government through notification in the Official Gazette.

A State Information Commission consists of:

  • A State Chief Information Commissioner.
  • Such number of State Information Commissioners as may be considered necessary, subject to the statutory maximum of ten.

The Governor appoints the State Chief Information Commissioner and State Information Commissioners on the recommendation of a committee consisting of:

  1. The Chief Minister, who is the Chairperson.
  2. The Leader of Opposition in the Legislative Assembly.
  3. A Cabinet Minister nominated by the Chief Minister.

Where a State has a Legislative Council, the statutory provisions also provide for the role of the Chairman and Leader of Opposition in the Council in the specified circumstances.

The eligibility and disqualification framework broadly parallels that for the CIC, with the relevant State-level offices and restrictions.

6. Tenure, Service Conditions and Removal

The Right to Information (Amendment) Act, 2019 changed the framework governing tenure, salaries and service conditions of the Chief Information Commissioner and Information Commissioners at both Union and State levels.

Under the amended Section 13 and Section 16, the term of office and conditions of service are prescribed by the Central Government through rules. The Right to Information Rules, 2019 prescribe a tenure of three years for the Chief Information Commissioner, Information Commissioners, State Chief Information Commissioners and State Information Commissioners, subject to the statutory age ceiling of sixty-five years.

The 2019 amendment removed the earlier statutory linkage of salary and service conditions to those of specified constitutional or senior public offices. This has generated debate about whether executive control over service conditions can affect the perceived independence of information commissions.

The Act provides a protected removal procedure. The President may remove the Chief Information Commissioner or an Information Commissioner on grounds and through the procedure prescribed in Section 14. For proved misbehaviour or incapacity, the President must refer the matter to the Supreme Court for an inquiry; removal follows the Court's report in accordance with the Act. The statute also lists other grounds such as insolvency, conviction involving moral turpitude, paid employment outside official duties, infirmity, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court and abuse of office.

For State Information Commissioners, Section 17 provides the corresponding removal framework, with the Governor exercising the statutory role and the Supreme Court inquiry safeguard applying to the specified grounds.

7. Powers and Functions of Information Commissions

Section 18 authorises the CIC and SICs to receive and inquire into complaints concerning the operation of the Act. Complaints may arise where:

  • A person is unable to submit an information request because no Public Information Officer has been appointed.
  • An application or appeal is refused at the receiving stage.
  • Access to information has been denied.
  • No response is received within the prescribed time.
  • An unreasonable fee is demanded.
  • Information supplied is incomplete, misleading or false.
  • Another matter relating to obtaining access to records gives rise to a complaint under the Act.

While conducting an inquiry, the Commission has powers of a civil court in specified matters, including summoning persons, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records and examining witnesses.

Under Section 19, the Commission decides second appeals against decisions of the first appellate authority. It may require a public authority to take steps necessary to secure compliance with the Act, including providing access to information, appointing Public Information Officers, publishing specified information, improving record-management practices, training officials and submitting compliance reports.

The Commission may also award compensation to a complainant for loss or detriment suffered, impose statutory penalties on a Public Information Officer in appropriate cases and recommend disciplinary action where the statutory conditions are satisfied.

8. Complaint and Appeal: Important Distinction

The complaint mechanism under Section 18 and the appeal mechanism under Section 19 serve related but distinct purposes.

  • Section 18 complaint: Enables the Commission to examine specified failures in the RTI process, such as refusal to accept an application, absence of a Public Information Officer or denial of access.
  • Section 19 first appeal: Lies before an officer senior in rank to the Public Information Officer within the same public authority, ordinarily within thirty days of the decision or expiry of the response period. Delay may be condoned for sufficient cause.
  • Section 19 second appeal: Lies before the CIC or SIC, as appropriate, ordinarily within ninety days of the decision on the first appeal or the date on which it should have been made. Delay may be condoned for sufficient cause.

In Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur (2011), the Supreme Court distinguished the complaint jurisdiction under Section 18 from the appellate jurisdiction under Section 19. The Court held that an order directing disclosure of information is to be made through the appellate mechanism under Section 19, rather than through a complaint inquiry under Section 18 alone.

This distinction is important for applicants: a person seeking disclosure after denial should ordinarily use the appeal route, while a complaint addresses the specified failures in the statutory process.

9. Penalties and Accountability of Public Information Officers

Section 20 authorises the Information Commission to impose a penalty on a Public Information Officer where, without reasonable cause, the officer has:

  • Refused to receive an application for information.
  • Failed to furnish information within the prescribed time.
  • Malafidely denied a request for information.
  • Knowingly given incorrect, incomplete or misleading information.
  • Destroyed information that was the subject of a request.
  • Obstructed the furnishing of information in any manner.

The penalty is Rs. 250 for each day of delay, subject to a maximum of Rs. 25,000. Before imposing a penalty, the officer must be given a reasonable opportunity of being heard. The burden of proving that the officer acted reasonably and diligently lies on the officer in the circumstances specified by the Act.

The Commission may also recommend disciplinary action under the applicable service rules where the statutory conditions are met. Penalty provisions are intended to deter deliberate obstruction, but they must be applied fairly and with attention to whether the officer had reasonable cause.

10. Exemptions and the Balance with Other Rights

The right to information is not absolute. Section 8 protects specified categories of information, including matters affecting national security, parliamentary privilege, commercial confidence, fiduciary relationships, information received in confidence from foreign governments, safety of individuals, investigation and prosecution, cabinet papers and certain personal information, subject to the statutory language and applicable overrides.

Section 8(2) contains a public-interest override: notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act or the exemptions in Section 8(1), access may be allowed where public interest in disclosure outweighs the harm to the protected interests. Section 10 permits severance, enabling disclosure of the non-exempt portion of a record where the exempt portion can be separated.

Section 11 establishes a procedure for consultation where information relating to or supplied by a third party is treated as confidential. It does not create an independent exemption; the Commission or Public Information Officer must still apply the relevant statutory provisions.

Section 24 exempts specified intelligence and security organisations from the Act, but the statutory exception concerning allegations of corruption and human rights violations must be noted. For information relating to alleged human rights violations, the Act provides a special approval and time framework.

11. RTI and the Right to Privacy: The DPDP Act Position

Transparency must be balanced with privacy and protection of personal data. The RTI Act itself contains exemptions relating to personal information, and the Supreme Court has recognised privacy as a fundamental right under Article 21 and Part III of the Constitution.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 provides for an amendment to Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act. The substituted clause refers to information which relates to personal information. However, the amendment's commencement must be stated accurately.

As at 15 November 2026, Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act, which contains the amendment to Section 8(1)(j), has not yet come into force. The Central Government's commencement notification of 13 November 2025 schedules this provision for commencement eighteen months after publication. Therefore, it is incorrect to describe the substituted Section 8(1)(j) as already operative on this date. The existing RTI provision and its applicable public-interest framework continue to govern until the relevant amendment commences.

The broader policy challenge is to prevent disclosure of genuinely sensitive personal data while ensuring that privacy is not used as a blanket justification to conceal corruption, misuse of public office or information necessary for public accountability.

12. Landmark Judicial Decisions

  • State of U.P. v. Raj Narain (1975): Recognised the importance of the people's right to know about public acts of government, subject to legitimate claims of confidentiality and public interest.
  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981): Emphasised open government and treated disclosure as an important aspect of democratic accountability, while recognising limited grounds for confidentiality.
  • Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002): Recognised voters' right to know relevant information about electoral candidates as part of Article 19(1)(a).
  • People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2003): Reaffirmed the importance of voters' right to information in the electoral process.
  • Chief Information Commissioner v. State of Manipur (2011): Distinguished the complaint jurisdiction under Section 18 from the appellate jurisdiction under Section 19.
  • Girish Ramchandra Deshpande v. Central Information Commissioner (2012): Considered the protection of personal information under the RTI Act, while leaving disclosure to be assessed under the statutory public-interest framework.
  • Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (Retd.) v. Union of India (2017): Recognised privacy as a fundamental right, making the reconciliation of privacy and transparency an important constitutional issue.

13. Major Challenges Facing Information Commissions

  • Vacancies and pendency: Delays in appointments and inadequate staffing can create large backlogs and weaken the value of timely information.
  • Executive influence: Appointment procedures and government control over service conditions raise questions about the independence of commissions.
  • Weak proactive disclosure: Poor compliance with Section 4 forces citizens to file individual requests for information that should already be available.
  • Record-management failures: Incomplete, unindexed or poorly digitised records make disclosure difficult and costly.
  • Misuse of exemptions: Broad or mechanical invocation of exemptions can undermine the statutory presumption in favour of disclosure.
  • Threats to applicants: Citizens and whistle-blowers seeking sensitive information may face intimidation or violence.
  • Limited awareness: Marginalised groups may lack knowledge of application procedures, appeal rights and assistance mechanisms.
  • Digital divide: Online systems can improve efficiency but may exclude people without reliable internet access or digital literacy.
  • Weak compliance: Orders and recommendations may not always result in timely disclosure or systemic improvement.

14. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Ensure timely appointments and adequate staffing at the CIC and SICs.
  2. Strengthen transparency and merit-based selection while protecting institutional independence.
  3. Implement Section 4 proactive disclosure through updated websites, searchable databases and accessible public records.
  4. Improve record digitisation, indexing, retention and secure archival systems.
  5. Publish regular pendency, disposal, compliance and penalty data to enable public assessment.
  6. Protect RTI applicants and strengthen mechanisms for reporting threats and retaliation.
  7. Provide application and appeal assistance in regional languages and through local facilitation centres.
  8. Train Public Information Officers in exemptions, severability, third-party consultation and time-bound disposal.
  9. Ensure privacy exemptions are applied narrowly and with reasoned consideration of public interest.
  10. Strengthen compliance monitoring so that disclosure orders lead to actual access and improved administrative practice.

15. Prelims-Oriented Facts

  • The RTI Act was enacted in 2005 and replaced the Freedom of Information Act, 2002.
  • Only citizens have the right to seek information under Section 3.
  • The CIC is constituted by the Central Government; the SIC is constituted by the State Government.
  • The President appoints the Chief Information Commissioner and Central Information Commissioners; the Governor appoints the State counterparts.
  • The CIC and SIC can have a Chief Information Commissioner and up to ten Information Commissioners.
  • The first appeal is ordinarily made within thirty days; the second appeal is ordinarily made within ninety days, subject to statutory provisions for condonation of delay.
  • The penalty under Section 20 is Rs. 250 per day, subject to a maximum of Rs. 25,000.
  • Section 18 concerns complaints; Section 19 provides the appellate mechanism.
  • Section 22 gives the RTI Act overriding effect over inconsistent laws to the extent provided by the section.
  • Section 24 contains exemptions for specified intelligence and security organisations, subject to statutory exceptions.
  • The DPDP Act amendment to Section 8(1)(j) is not yet in force as at 15 November 2026.

16. Mains Perspective and Conclusion

Information commissions are essential to the accountability architecture of a democratic State. They transform the abstract right to know into a statutory process through which citizens can question administrative decisions, monitor public expenditure and expose institutional failures.

The effectiveness of the RTI framework depends not merely on the existence of commissions but on timely appointments, independent functioning, accessible procedures, proactive disclosure and compliance with orders. The balance between transparency and privacy must be maintained through careful application of statutory exemptions rather than routine secrecy.

Conclusion: A robust right to information regime strengthens participatory democracy and responsible government. Information commissions must be institutionally independent, adequately resourced and publicly accountable so that access to information becomes an effective instrument of citizen empowerment.
