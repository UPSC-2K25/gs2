1. Competition as a foundation of a fair market economy

Competition is a process through which enterprises seek to attract consumers by improving price, quality, innovation, choice and efficiency. It encourages firms to use resources productively, respond to consumer preferences and develop new products and services. Competitive markets can support productivity, investment and economic growth.

Competition cannot always be taken for granted. Enterprises may enter into cartels, abuse market power, exclude rivals, coordinate prices or use mergers to reduce competitive pressure. Such conduct can harm consumers, suppliers, workers, smaller enterprises and the wider economy.

Competition law therefore seeks to preserve the competitive process rather than protect every individual competitor from commercial failure. A firm is not prohibited merely because it is large, successful or efficient. Intervention is justified when conduct or combinations fall within the prohibitions and tests prescribed by law.

The Competition Commission of India (CCI) is the statutory authority responsible for enforcing India's competition law framework, promoting competition advocacy and addressing anti-competitive conduct and combinations.

2. Constitutional and economic foundations

The Constitution does not establish a separate competition regulator, but competition policy is connected with the constitutional vision of social and economic justice and the regulation of economic power.

  • Article 14: Equality before law and protection against arbitrary State action are relevant to fair and non-discriminatory regulatory decisions.
  • Article 19(1)(g): Protects the freedom to practise a profession or carry on an occupation, trade or business.
  • Article 19(6): Permits reasonable restrictions on that freedom in the interests specified by the Constitution.
  • Article 38: Directs the State to promote a social order informed by justice and to minimise inequalities.
  • Article 39(b): Directs the State to secure that ownership and control of material resources are distributed to subserve the common good.
  • Article 39(c): Directs the State to ensure that the operation of the economic system does not result in concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment.

Competition law is one instrument for addressing private economic power. It operates alongside sector regulation, public procurement rules, consumer protection, corporate law and policies supporting small enterprises.

3. Evolution of competition law in India

The Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1969 (MRTP Act) was India's earlier framework for addressing monopolistic, restrictive and unfair trade practices. It emerged in the context of concerns about concentration of economic power and restrictive business conduct.

Economic liberalisation in the 1990s changed the regulatory environment. Greater reliance on markets, private investment, global trade and competition required a modern framework that focused on competitive effects rather than treating size or dominance as inherently objectionable.

The Competition Act, 2002 was enacted to establish a modern competition regime. It was amended in 2007, and the CCI became operational for enforcement of the principal prohibitions in 2009. The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 introduced important changes to merger review, settlement, commitments, penalties and other enforcement mechanisms.

The CCI is a statutory body established under Section 7 of the Competition Act, 2002. It is not a constitutional body.

4. Composition and institutional structure

The Competition Act provides for a Commission consisting of a Chairperson and Members appointed by the Central Government. The statutory framework requires the Chairperson and Members to have specified qualifications, integrity and professional experience in fields such as economics, law, commerce, finance, industry, management and public affairs.

The CCI's multidisciplinary composition is important because competition cases often require economic analysis, market definition, assessment of market power, evaluation of business conduct and interpretation of legal obligations.

The Director General (DG) investigates matters referred for investigation under the Act. The DG's investigative role is distinct from the CCI's adjudicatory role. The DG collects evidence and submits an investigation report; the Commission independently considers the record and provides parties an opportunity to be heard.

The Competition Act, 2023 changed the statutory arrangement for appointment of the Director General, providing for appointment by the Commission with prior approval of the Central Government. The precise appointment and service framework should be read with the current Act and applicable rules.

5. Core objectives and functions of the CCI

The CCI's statutory mandate is to prevent practices having an adverse effect on competition, promote and sustain competition, protect consumers and ensure freedom of trade carried on by other participants in markets in India.

Its principal functions include:

  • Investigating and addressing anti-competitive agreements.
  • Preventing abuse of dominant position.
  • Reviewing combinations such as mergers, acquisitions and amalgamations.
  • Imposing penalties and issuing remedial directions under the Act.
  • Promoting competition advocacy and awareness.
  • Advising governments and statutory authorities on competition-policy issues when referred.
  • Developing institutional knowledge and market analysis.

The CCI's role is both corrective and preventive. It may intervene against established violations and review specified combinations before their implementation.

6. Anti-competitive agreements: Section 3

Section 3 prohibits agreements that cause or are likely to cause an appreciable adverse effect on competition (AAEC) within India. The provision covers agreements between enterprises, persons or associations of enterprises or persons, subject to the statutory framework.

6.1 Horizontal agreements

Horizontal agreements are arrangements between enterprises operating at the same level of the supply chain, such as competing manufacturers or competing service providers. Certain categories are presumed to have an AAEC under Section 3(3), subject to the statutory provisions.

These include agreements involving:

  • Price fixing, including direct or indirect determination of purchase or sale prices.
  • Limiting or controlling production, supply, markets, technical development, investment or provision of services.
  • Market allocation, including allocation of customers, geographical areas, products or sources of production.
  • Bid rigging or collusive bidding.

Cartels are particularly harmful because competing firms coordinate rather than independently determining their commercial conduct. The result may be higher prices, reduced output, inferior quality and diminished innovation.

6.2 Vertical agreements

Vertical agreements operate between enterprises at different stages of the supply chain, such as manufacturers, distributors, wholesalers and retailers. Section 3(4) covers specified arrangements including tie-in arrangements, exclusive supply agreements, exclusive distribution agreements, refusal to deal and resale price maintenance.

Vertical restraints are not automatically unlawful. Their competitive effects must be assessed under the statutory AAEC framework. Some arrangements may improve distribution efficiency, protect investment or support quality standards; others may foreclose competitors or restrict consumer choice.

6.3 Hub-and-spoke arrangements

Coordination may occur through an intermediary that facilitates information exchange or coordination among competitors. The 2023 amendment broadened the treatment of certain arrangements involving persons or enterprises that are not themselves engaged in identical or similar trade, where they participate or intend to participate in furtherance of an agreement among competitors.

This is relevant to digital platforms, trade associations, common suppliers and other intermediaries that may facilitate coordinated conduct.

7. Abuse of dominant position: Section 4

Dominance refers to a position of strength enjoyed by an enterprise in the relevant market that enables it to operate independently of competitive forces or affect competitors or consumers in its favour.

Dominance itself is not prohibited. The law prohibits its abuse. This distinction is important because a firm may become dominant through innovation, efficiency, investment or superior products. Competition law intervenes when the dominant position is used in ways prohibited by Section 4.

Abusive conduct may include:

  • Imposing unfair or discriminatory conditions in the purchase or sale of goods or services.
  • Imposing unfair or discriminatory prices, including predatory pricing within the statutory meaning.
  • Limiting or restricting production, markets or technical or scientific development to the prejudice of consumers.
  • Denial of market access in specified circumstances.
  • Making contracts subject to supplementary obligations that have no connection with the subject of the contracts.
  • Using dominance in one relevant market to enter into or protect another relevant market in specified circumstances.

Assessment requires defining the relevant market, examining market power, considering entry barriers and evaluating the conduct's effects. Market share is relevant but not conclusive by itself.

8. Relevant market and assessment of market power

The relevant market is generally defined through the relevant product market and relevant geographic market. This allows the CCI to identify the competitive constraints faced by an enterprise.

The relevant product market may be determined with reference to characteristics of goods or services, prices, intended use, consumer preferences and the ability of consumers to switch to alternatives.

The relevant geographic market considers the area in which conditions of competition are sufficiently homogeneous and distinguishable from neighbouring areas.

Factors relevant to assessing dominance include market share, size and resources, economic power, vertical integration, dependence of consumers, entry barriers, countervailing buyer power and market structure.

Digital markets create additional analytical challenges. Network effects, data advantages, multi-sided platforms, switching costs, default settings and control over access to users may influence market power. A market that appears to offer free services may still involve competition over attention, data, quality and innovation.

9. Regulation of combinations: Sections 5 and 6

Combinations include specified acquisitions of control, shares, voting rights or assets, and mergers or amalgamations that cross the thresholds prescribed under the Act and rules. The objective is to prevent transactions that cause or are likely to cause an AAEC in the relevant market in India.

Combination review is a preventive process. The CCI examines the likely competitive effects of a proposed transaction, including whether it may create or strengthen market power, eliminate important competitive constraints or facilitate coordinated conduct.

9.1 Thresholds and deal-value criterion

The Act provides asset and turnover thresholds for combinations, supplemented by rules and notifications. The 2023 amendment introduced a deal-value threshold as an additional criterion, subject to the statutory conditions and prescribed value.

The deal-value criterion is particularly relevant to transactions involving digital and technology businesses whose current assets or turnover may not adequately reflect their competitive significance. A business with limited current revenue may nevertheless possess valuable data, technology, intellectual property or a strategically important user base.

9.2 Review timeline

The 2023 amendment reduced the statutory outer timeline for the CCI to form its prima facie opinion on a notified combination and the overall review period, subject to the Act's procedural provisions. The revised framework seeks to improve certainty and speed while retaining substantive scrutiny.

Parties must comply with the applicable notification and standstill requirements. Completing a notifiable combination before obtaining the required approval may result in consequences under the Act.

9.3 Remedies

The CCI may approve a combination, prohibit it where the statutory test is met, or approve it subject to modifications. Modifications may include structural or behavioural remedies designed to address identified competitive concerns.

10. Investigation and adjudication process

Information concerning an alleged contravention may be received by the CCI from a person, consumer or association, or a reference may be made by the Central Government, a State Government or a statutory authority. The Commission may also act suo motu within the statutory framework.

Where the Commission forms a prima facie opinion that a contravention may have occurred, it may direct the Director General to investigate. The DG gathers documents, records statements and examines relevant evidence in accordance with the Act.

The investigation report is considered by the CCI. Parties must receive a fair opportunity to respond to material relied upon. The Commission then determines whether the statutory provisions have been contravened and what directions or penalties are appropriate.

The process must observe natural justice, reasoned decision-making and procedural fairness. Competition investigations can have major commercial consequences, making clear findings and transparent reasoning essential.

11. Powers and remedies

Where the CCI finds a contravention, it may issue directions to discontinue the anti-competitive conduct, prohibit repetition, modify agreements, impose penalties and issue other orders authorised by the Act.

In appropriate cases, the Commission may direct division of an enterprise enjoying a dominant position, subject to the statutory requirements. It may also order remedies in combination proceedings.

The CCI may impose penalties for contraventions of the Act and for specified procedural violations. The 2023 amendment changed the penalty framework to include the concept of global turnover derived from all products and services of an enterprise, subject to the statutory provisions and the applicable penalty guidelines.

Penalty assessment must be proportionate and based on legally relevant factors. Deterrence is important, but predictable and reasoned enforcement is also necessary for regulatory legitimacy.

12. Settlement, commitments and leniency

The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 introduced settlement and commitment mechanisms for specified cases involving alleged contraventions of Section 3(4) or Section 4.

A commitment mechanism allows an enterprise to offer commitments at the stage and in the manner prescribed by law, with the objective of addressing competition concerns without completing the full adjudicatory process. A settlement mechanism allows eligible parties to propose a settlement after the statutory investigation stage, subject to the prescribed framework.

These mechanisms are not a general substitute for enforcement. They are designed to secure faster market correction and reduce litigation in eligible matters. The CCI retains the statutory role of evaluating whether the proposal is appropriate and whether the legal requirements are met.

The Act also strengthened the leniency framework through Leniency Plus. An enterprise already cooperating in relation to one cartel may disclose information about another cartel and may receive an additional reduction in penalty in accordance with the statutory and regulatory framework.

Leniency mechanisms help uncover cartels that are difficult to detect because participants have incentives to conceal coordination. Their success depends on credible confidentiality protections and predictable treatment of applicants.

13. Competition advocacy

Competition advocacy involves promoting a culture of competition among businesses, public authorities, policymakers and consumers. It includes awareness, training, policy advice and examination of the competitive implications of government decisions.

Public procurement, licensing, technical standards, exclusive concessions and regulatory barriers can affect market entry and competition. Government action may unintentionally create entry barriers or favour particular suppliers.

The CCI may advise governments and statutory authorities on competition issues when referred to it under the Act. Competition advocacy can encourage competition-neutral policy design without requiring enforcement proceedings in every case.

14. Appellate oversight and judicial review

The National Company Law Appellate Tribunal (NCLAT) is the appellate forum for appeals against specified CCI orders under the Competition Act. Further appeal lies to the Supreme Court in accordance with the Act.

The appellate structure ensures that the CCI's decisions remain subject to legal scrutiny. Review may concern jurisdiction, interpretation of the Act, evidentiary standards, procedural fairness and the proportionality of remedies.

Competition adjudication requires a balance between regulatory expertise and judicial accountability. Courts should respect specialised economic analysis where legally sound, while ensuring that statutory powers are exercised within the law.

15. Relationship with sector regulators

Competition regulation often intersects with sectoral regulation. Telecom, electricity, banking, insurance, securities, transport and digital markets may be governed by specialised regulators that possess technical expertise and sector-specific responsibilities.

The Competition Act contains provisions for references and coordination between the CCI and statutory authorities. The objective is to avoid inconsistent regulation while preserving the distinct role of competition law.

Sector regulators may address licensing, service quality, prudential standards, consumer protection and technical obligations. The CCI focuses on competition concerns under the Competition Act. A sector regulator's existence does not automatically exclude competition law, although statutory context and jurisdiction must be examined carefully.

In Competition Commission of India v. Bharti Airtel Ltd. (2018), the Supreme Court considered the relationship between the CCI and the telecom regulator in a dispute involving sector-specific technical issues. The judgment illustrates the importance of institutional coordination and sequencing where specialist regulatory questions arise.

16. Digital markets and emerging competition issues

Digital markets can exhibit strong network effects: a service may become more valuable as more users join. Large platforms may also benefit from data accumulation, economies of scale, control over distribution and the ability to operate across multiple sides of a market.

Potential concerns include self-preferencing, tying, discriminatory access, restrictions on interoperability, exclusive arrangements, data-related advantages and practices that make switching difficult.

Digital competition analysis must distinguish legitimate innovation and efficiency from conduct that excludes competitors or harms the competitive process. A large user base is not itself proof of abuse.

The Competition (Amendment) Act, 2023 and the CCI's evolving enforcement framework address some challenges through the deal-value threshold, broader treatment of certain agreements and enhanced investigative and penalty mechanisms. The effectiveness of these measures depends on technical capacity and timely evidence gathering.

17. Challenges facing the CCI

  • Complex economic evidence: Competition cases require market definition, counterfactual analysis and assessment of competitive effects.
  • Digital market speed: Market structures and business models can change faster than traditional enforcement timelines.
  • Information asymmetry: Regulators may lack access to internal business data and platform algorithms.
  • Regulatory overlap: Multiple regulators may have legitimate interests in the same market conduct.
  • Procedural delay: Long investigations and litigation can reduce the effectiveness of remedies.
  • Penalty predictability: Enterprises require clarity on the methodology and proportionality of penalties.
  • Market concentration: Some sectors have high entry barriers, network effects or infrastructure constraints.
  • Public procurement: Bid rigging and collusion may be difficult to detect where procurement data are fragmented.
  • Institutional capacity: The Commission needs economists, data scientists, lawyers and sector specialists.

18. Reform agenda

  • Strengthen economic, legal and digital-market expertise within the Commission and investigation machinery.
  • Improve coordination protocols with sector regulators and public procurement authorities.
  • Develop transparent and predictable approaches to penalty calculation and remedial design.
  • Strengthen detection of bid rigging through data analytics and procurement transparency.
  • Improve the timeliness of investigations while preserving due process.
  • Publish accessible market studies and competition advocacy guidance.
  • Strengthen the capacity to investigate algorithmic coordination and platform conduct.
  • Ensure that merger review is sufficiently fast without sacrificing substantive assessment.
  • Promote competition-neutral government procurement and licensing frameworks.
  • Evaluate enforcement outcomes and publish reasoned explanations of significant decisions.

19. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

The CCI is central to India's transition towards a market economy in which enterprise is encouraged but anti-competitive conduct is constrained. Its mandate is not to protect inefficient firms from competition; it is to protect the competitive process and the interests of consumers and market participants.

The Competition Act, 2002 provides a framework for addressing anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominance and combinations. The 2023 amendment modernised several aspects of enforcement, particularly for digital markets and faster resolution of eligible cases.

The effectiveness of the CCI depends on technical expertise, institutional independence, procedural fairness, coordination with sector regulators and timely enforcement. Competition policy must also be integrated into government procurement, licensing and industrial policy so that public action does not unnecessarily restrict market entry.

21. Key terms

Appreciable adverse effect on competition: A significant adverse impact on competition assessed under the statutory framework.

Cartel: An association of producers, sellers, distributors, traders or service providers that, by agreement, limits, controls or attempts to control production, distribution, sale, price or trade in goods or services.

Dominant position: A position of strength in a relevant market enabling an enterprise to operate independently of competitive forces or affect competitors or consumers in its favour.

Relevant market: The market defined by reference to relevant product and geographic dimensions for competition analysis.

Combination: Specified acquisitions, mergers and amalgamations that fall within the statutory thresholds and framework.

Leniency: A mechanism through which qualifying cartel participants may receive reduced penalties for making disclosures and cooperating with the regulator.
