1. Securities markets and public confidence

Securities markets connect investors who supply capital with companies and governments that require funds. They support capital formation, investment, risk-sharing and economic development. A well-functioning securities market allows savings to be channelled towards productive uses and gives investors opportunities to diversify their portfolios.

These markets depend on trust. Investors must receive reliable information, intermediaries must follow fair practices, trading must be orderly and market manipulation must be prevented. Insider trading, fraudulent disclosures, price manipulation and misuse of client assets can undermine confidence and discourage participation.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) is the principal statutory regulator of India's securities market. Its mandate is to protect investors, promote the development of the securities market and regulate that market.

2. Evolution of securities-market regulation

India's securities market historically operated under a framework that included the Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956 and the regulatory supervision of the Central Government. The expansion of capital markets, growth of public investment and concerns about market irregularities created a need for a specialised regulator.

SEBI was constituted as a non-statutory body on 12 April 1988 through a government resolution. The Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992 gave it statutory status. The Act came into force on 30 January 1992.

Subsequent securities legislation and amendments strengthened SEBI's authority over stock exchanges, intermediaries, mutual funds, depositories, listed entities and market conduct.

SEBI is a statutory body, not a constitutional body. Its powers derive from the SEBI Act, 1992 and other legislation that assigns it regulatory functions.

3. Legal framework

SEBI's regulatory responsibilities arise from a group of statutes. The principal laws include:

  • Securities and Exchange Board of India Act, 1992: Establishes SEBI and provides its core powers, functions and enforcement framework.
  • Securities Contracts (Regulation) Act, 1956: Regulates securities contracts and stock exchanges and provides a framework for recognised exchanges.
  • Depositories Act, 1996: Provides the legal framework for depositories and the holding and transfer of securities in dematerialised form.
  • Companies Act, 2013: Contains provisions relevant to companies, securities issuance, corporate governance and disclosures, with responsibilities shared among statutory authorities.

SEBI also issues regulations, circulars, directions and guidelines within the authority conferred by law. Important regulatory areas include listing obligations, issue of capital, takeover regulation, insider trading, mutual funds, alternative investment funds and market intermediaries.

4. Composition and management of SEBI

Section 4 of the SEBI Act provides for the composition of the Board. The statutory structure includes:

  • A Chairperson.
  • Two members from among officials of the Central Government ministries dealing with finance and administration of the Companies Act, as provided by the statute.
  • One member from among officials of the Reserve Bank of India.
  • Five other members appointed by the Central Government, of whom at least three must be whole-time members.

The Central Government appoints the members in accordance with the Act. The Board exercises general superintendence, direction and management of SEBI's affairs. The Chairperson has powers of general superintendence and direction subject to the Act and regulations.

The composition reflects a combination of regulatory expertise and institutional coordination with the government and the central bank. At the same time, effective securities regulation requires professional independence, specialised knowledge and transparent decision-making.

5. Core mandate and functions

Section 11 of the SEBI Act states the Board's duty to protect the interests of investors in securities and to promote the development of, and regulate, the securities market.

Its functions include:

  • Regulating the business of stock exchanges and other securities markets.
  • Registering and regulating brokers, merchant bankers, underwriters, registrars, investment advisers and other intermediaries.
  • Registering and regulating depositories, participants, custodians and credit-rating agencies.
  • Regulating mutual funds, collective investment schemes and specified investment vehicles.
  • Promoting and regulating self-regulatory organisations where authorised.
  • Prohibiting fraudulent and unfair trade practices in securities markets.
  • Prohibiting insider trading.
  • Regulating substantial acquisition of shares and takeovers.
  • Calling for information, conducting inspections, inquiries and audits.
  • Promoting investor education and training of market intermediaries.
  • Conducting research and undertaking measures necessary for the development and regulation of securities markets.

SEBI therefore performs regulatory, developmental and protective functions. These functions are interdependent: market development without investor confidence is unsustainable, while excessive regulation without regard to innovation can reduce market efficiency.

6. Quasi-legislative, quasi-executive and quasi-judicial character

SEBI is often described as exercising three broad types of powers.

6.1 Quasi-legislative functions

SEBI makes regulations under the authority of the SEBI Act and other applicable laws. These regulations prescribe standards for market intermediaries, disclosure, trading, listing, takeovers, mutual funds and other activities.

Regulations must remain within the parent statute and comply with applicable legal requirements. They cannot override the Act or exceed the authority delegated by Parliament.

6.2 Quasi-executive functions

SEBI registers intermediaries, conducts inspections, calls for information, investigates suspected violations and issues directions. These functions involve administration and enforcement of the securities-market framework.

6.3 Quasi-judicial functions

SEBI conducts adjudicatory proceedings and passes orders under its statutory powers. It may determine violations, impose monetary penalties where authorised and issue directions to protect investors and the securities market.

Combining these functions within one institution requires safeguards. Clear separation of investigative and adjudicatory responsibilities, reasoned orders, disclosure of material relied upon and appellate review help maintain procedural fairness.

7. Investor protection

Investor protection is central to SEBI's statutory mandate. Investors may face risks arising from incomplete disclosures, misleading claims, fraud, insider trading, market manipulation, conflicts of interest and misuse of funds by intermediaries.

SEBI seeks to protect investors through disclosure requirements, registration and supervision of intermediaries, market surveillance, enforcement action and investor education.

Investor protection does not mean guaranteeing returns or eliminating investment risk. Securities investments remain exposed to market, credit, liquidity and business risks. Regulation seeks to ensure that investors receive fair treatment and material information and that markets operate in accordance with law.

Investor education is important because retail participation has expanded through digital platforms and simplified access to trading. Investors need to understand risk, diversification, fees, leverage, suitability and the difference between regulated investment products and fraudulent schemes.

8. Regulation of stock exchanges and market infrastructure

Stock exchanges provide organised platforms for trading securities. They support price discovery, liquidity, transparency and the transfer of securities between investors.

SEBI regulates recognised stock exchanges and market infrastructure institutions within the statutory framework. Its oversight covers governance, trading systems, risk management, surveillance, clearing and settlement arrangements and market integrity.

Market infrastructure must be resilient because operational failure can affect large numbers of investors and financial institutions. Cybersecurity, business continuity, disaster recovery and effective internal controls are therefore important regulatory priorities.

SEBI also regulates clearing corporations and depositories through the relevant legal framework. These institutions perform functions essential to the safe settlement and holding of securities.

9. Regulation of market intermediaries

Intermediaries connect issuers, investors and trading platforms. They include stock brokers, merchant bankers, registrars, investment advisers, portfolio managers, underwriters, custodians, depository participants and other entities specified under law.

Registration requirements help ensure that intermediaries meet prescribed standards of eligibility, competence, financial soundness and conduct.

Regulatory obligations may include:

  • Fit-and-proper criteria and professional competence.
  • Capital adequacy and financial safeguards.
  • Maintenance of records and audit trails.
  • Disclosure of fees, conflicts of interest and material information.
  • Segregation and protection of client assets.
  • Prevention of misuse of confidential information.
  • Risk-management and cybersecurity arrangements.
  • Grievance redressal and compliance systems.

Intermediary regulation is particularly important because investors often rely on agents and platforms to execute transactions or provide advice. Misconduct at this level can undermine market confidence even where the underlying issuer is sound.

10. Regulation of public issues and disclosures

Companies raise capital through public issues, rights issues, preferential issues and other permitted methods. Investors require accurate information about the issuer, its financial position, risks, use of proceeds and terms of the securities being offered.

SEBI's issue-related regulations prescribe disclosure, eligibility and procedural requirements for public offers and other capital-raising activities.

The objective is not to guarantee the commercial success of an issue. It is to ensure that investors receive material information and that securities are issued through fair and transparent processes.

Disclosure-based regulation places responsibility on issuers and intermediaries to provide accurate and complete information. Enforcement against false or misleading disclosures is essential for market integrity.

11. Insider trading

Insider trading involves trading in securities while in possession of unpublished price-sensitive information, in violation of the applicable legal framework. It undermines the principle that investors should compete on a fair basis and can damage confidence in securities markets.

The SEBI (Prohibition of Insider Trading) Regulations, 2015 provide the principal regulatory framework. They govern trading by insiders, handling and communication of unpublished price-sensitive information, trading plans and compliance obligations of listed companies and intermediaries.

Unpublished price-sensitive information generally concerns information relating to a company or its securities that is not generally available and that, upon becoming generally available, is likely to materially affect the price of securities.

Examples may include financial results, dividends, changes in capital structure, mergers, acquisitions, significant expansion plans and other material events specified by the regulations.

Effective enforcement requires examination of trading patterns, communication records, access controls and the relationship between persons possessing information and those who trade.

12. Fraudulent and unfair trade practices

Market manipulation distorts price discovery and can cause investors to make decisions based on artificial market signals. The SEBI (Prohibition of Fraudulent and Unfair Trade Practices relating to Securities Market) Regulations, 2003 provide a framework for addressing specified fraudulent and unfair conduct.

Prohibited conduct may include creating false or misleading appearances of trading, manipulating prices, disseminating misleading information and engaging in fraudulent transactions in violation of the regulations.

Modern forms of manipulation may involve coordinated trading, circular transactions, deceptive online communications, misleading recommendations and misuse of social media to influence prices.

SEBI's surveillance capacity must therefore adapt to high-frequency trading, algorithmic systems, digital communication and cross-platform conduct.

13. Takeovers and substantial acquisition of shares

Corporate control may change through acquisitions, open offers, mergers or other transactions. Takeover regulation seeks to protect shareholders, ensure fair treatment and provide transparency when control or substantial voting rights change.

The SEBI (Substantial Acquisition of Shares and Takeovers) Regulations, 2011 prescribe disclosure obligations and open-offer requirements in specified circumstances.

Open-offer rules provide public shareholders an opportunity to exit in circumstances where an acquirer crosses prescribed thresholds or acquires control, subject to the regulations.

Takeover regulation must balance shareholder protection with efficient corporate restructuring and the ability of enterprises to raise capital and reorganise ownership.

14. Mutual funds and collective investment schemes

Mutual funds pool money from investors and invest it in securities or other permitted assets according to the scheme's stated objectives. They provide access to diversified portfolios and professional management.

SEBI regulates mutual funds through registration requirements, disclosure norms, governance standards, investment restrictions, valuation requirements and safeguards concerning trustees and asset management companies.

Collective investment schemes are separately addressed under Section 11AA of the SEBI Act and the applicable regulations. The legal character of a scheme depends on the statutory criteria and not merely the label used by its promoter.

Regulation aims to protect investors from unauthorised pooling of funds, misleading promises and misuse of collective investments. It also seeks to promote responsible growth of professionally managed investment products.

15. Depositories and dematerialisation

The Depositories Act, 1996 provides the legal framework for holding securities in electronic form through depositories. Dematerialisation has reduced dependence on physical certificates and improved the efficiency of transfer and settlement.

Depositories maintain electronic records of securities holdings, while depository participants provide services to investors. SEBI regulates depositories and participants within the statutory framework.

Electronic holdings improve efficiency but create risks involving cyberattacks, unauthorised access, operational failure and identity fraud. Strong authentication, audit trails, data security and grievance mechanisms are therefore essential.

16. Listing obligations and corporate governance

Listed companies are subject to continuing disclosure and governance obligations. The SEBI (Listing Obligations and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2015 provide a framework for disclosures, board governance, related-party transactions, shareholder rights and other obligations.

Corporate governance requirements seek to address information asymmetry between promoters, management, controlling shareholders and public investors.

Important areas include independent directors, audit committees, related-party transaction safeguards, timely disclosure of material events, financial reporting and protection of minority shareholders.

Good governance improves market confidence and can reduce the risk of fraud, diversion of resources and conflicts of interest. Formal compliance, however, must be supported by genuine board oversight and an organisational culture of accountability.

17. Enforcement powers

SEBI has investigative and enforcement powers under the SEBI Act. It may call for information, inspect records, conduct inquiries and audits, issue directions and initiate adjudicatory proceedings within the statutory framework.

Section 11B provides power to issue directions in the interests of investors or the securities market. Section 11(4) provides specified measures, including suspension of trading in securities, restraint from accessing the securities market and other measures in the circumstances prescribed by law.

SEBI may impose monetary penalties under the relevant provisions of the Act through the prescribed adjudicatory process. The Act also contains provisions relating to prosecution and recovery of amounts due.

Enforcement must be proportionate, evidence-based and procedurally fair. Market regulation requires both deterrence and predictability; arbitrary or inconsistent enforcement can undermine the confidence it seeks to protect.

18. Appeals and Securities Appellate Tribunal

The Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) is a specialised appellate tribunal established under the securities-law framework. It hears appeals against specified orders of SEBI and other financial regulators as provided by law.

The SAT provides an important mechanism for reviewing regulatory decisions. It can examine legal interpretation, procedural fairness, evidentiary findings and the exercise of statutory powers.

An appeal against an order of the SAT lies to the Supreme Court on a question of law under the statutory framework.

The appellate structure strengthens accountability by ensuring that specialised regulatory decisions are subject to independent judicial scrutiny.

19. Investor grievance redressal

Investor grievance redressal is an important part of market regulation. Investors may face problems relating to non-receipt of securities, transfer, dividends, refunds, intermediary misconduct, unauthorised transactions and other failures.

SEBI has developed mechanisms for lodging and tracking investor complaints, including the SCORES platform. The platform facilitates submission and monitoring of complaints against entities within SEBI's regulatory jurisdiction.

Online grievance systems improve accessibility and create a record of regulatory complaints. They do not replace every legal remedy, and some disputes may require action before an exchange, an intermediary's grievance mechanism, SAT or a court, depending on the issue.

Effective redressal requires time-bound responses, meaningful resolution rather than mere closure, clear escalation procedures and accountability for repeat failures.

20. SEBI and other financial regulators

India follows a sectoral model of financial regulation. Different authorities regulate different parts of the financial system:

  • RBI: Banking, monetary policy, payment systems and specified financial institutions.
  • SEBI: Securities markets, listed entities and securities-market intermediaries.
  • IRDAI: Insurance sector regulation.
  • PFRDA: Pension-sector regulation.
  • IFSCA: Financial services and institutions operating in International Financial Services Centres within its statutory jurisdiction.

Some financial products and institutions may have overlapping features. Coordination is required to prevent regulatory arbitrage, close supervisory gaps and ensure consistent treatment of risks.

The Financial Stability and Development Council provides a platform for coordination among financial-sector regulators and the government. It does not replace the statutory powers of the individual regulators.

21. Judicial decisions and regulatory principles

SEBI v. Shri Ram Mutual Fund (2006): The Supreme Court considered statutory penalty provisions and explained that the nature of the statutory obligation and penalty framework must guide the assessment of liability.

Clariant International Ltd. v. SEBI (2004): The Supreme Court examined SEBI's regulatory role and the importance of statutory procedures and investor protection in securities-market regulation.

SEBI v. Rakhi Trading Pvt. Ltd. (2018): The Supreme Court considered market manipulation and the role of artificial transactions in creating misleading market signals.

SEBI v. Sahara India Real Estate Corporation Ltd. (2012): The Supreme Court examined public fundraising and the application of securities regulation to an issue of securities to a large number of persons. The decision illustrates the importance of substance over labels in determining the regulatory character of an issue.

N. Narayanan v. Adjudicating Officer, SEBI (2013): The Supreme Court emphasised the importance of accurate corporate disclosures and accountability in securities markets.

These decisions demonstrate that investor protection requires both substantive standards and effective enforcement. They also underline the importance of procedural fairness and statutory limits on regulatory power.

22. Challenges facing SEBI

22.1 Market complexity

Financial products and trading strategies have become increasingly complex. Regulators must understand derivatives, algorithmic trading, structured products, alternative investment funds and new digital distribution channels.

22.2 Retail investor vulnerability

Growing retail participation is positive for capital-market development but can expose inexperienced investors to speculative behaviour, excessive leverage, misleading advice and online fraud.

22.3 Insider trading and information asymmetry

Detecting misuse of unpublished price-sensitive information is difficult when information moves through informal networks, digital communications and complex corporate structures.

22.4 Market manipulation

Manipulative activity may use layered transactions, social media, coordinated trading or complex instruments. Surveillance systems must adapt continuously.

22.5 Regulatory overlap

Financial products may involve banking, insurance, securities and pension features. Unclear boundaries can create gaps or duplication.

22.6 Enforcement capacity

Investigations may involve large volumes of digital evidence, complex financial structures and multiple jurisdictions. Timely enforcement requires specialised staff and advanced analytics.

22.7 Corporate governance

Formal compliance cannot fully prevent promoter misconduct, conflicts of interest or weak board oversight. Regulatory standards must be supported by effective internal governance.

22.8 Cybersecurity and operational resilience

Market infrastructure is increasingly dependent on technology. Cyber incidents, outages and data breaches can affect investors and the stability of trading and settlement systems.

23. Reform agenda

  • Strengthen data analytics, market surveillance and technological expertise.
  • Improve transparency and predictability in enforcement and penalty decisions.
  • Ensure that investigation and adjudication follow fair and clearly documented procedures.
  • Strengthen disclosure quality and corporate-governance oversight in listed companies.
  • Improve investor education on risk, leverage, diversification and fraud prevention.
  • Enhance grievance redressal and track actual resolution rather than procedural closure.
  • Improve coordination with RBI, IRDAI, PFRDA and other regulators.
  • Strengthen cybersecurity and business-continuity requirements for market infrastructure.
  • Adapt regulation to algorithmic trading, digital investment platforms and new financial products.
  • Maintain proportionate regulation that protects investors without unnecessarily discouraging innovation and capital formation.

24. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

SEBI is an important institution for market integrity, investor confidence and capital formation. Its statutory mandate combines investor protection, market development and regulation. It exercises quasi-legislative, quasi-executive and quasi-judicial functions within the framework of the SEBI Act and related securities legislation.

The growth of retail participation, digital trading and complex financial products has increased the need for strong surveillance, disclosure, intermediary regulation and investor education. At the same time, regulation must remain proportionate and procedurally fair to sustain innovation and efficient capital markets.

SEBI's effectiveness depends on professional capacity, regulatory credibility, transparent enforcement, sound corporate governance and coordination with other financial regulators. Its ultimate contribution is to ensure that capital markets remain trustworthy channels for savings and investment.

26. Key terms

Primary market: The market in which securities are issued to investors for the first time or through further issues.

Secondary market: The market in which existing securities are traded between investors.

Insider trading: Trading in securities in violation of the legal restrictions relating to unpublished price-sensitive information.

Market manipulation: Conduct that creates artificial or misleading market activity, prices or appearances of trading.

Dematerialisation: Conversion of physical securities into electronic form held through a depository system.

Disclosure-based regulation: A regulatory approach requiring issuers and intermediaries to provide material and accurate information to investors.

Market infrastructure institution: An institution such as a stock exchange, clearing corporation or depository that supports the functioning of securities markets.
