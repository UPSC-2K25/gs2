1. Meaning and Constitutional Context

Human rights are basic rights and freedoms inherent in every person by virtue of human dignity. They protect individuals against arbitrary power and provide conditions for equality, liberty, participation and a life of dignity.

India's human rights framework is rooted in the Constitution, particularly Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy and the institutional commitment to constitutional remedies. Statutory human rights commissions supplement this framework by investigating alleged violations, reviewing safeguards, promoting awareness and recommending corrective action.

The National Human Rights Commission (NHRC) and State Human Rights Commissions (SHRCs) are established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. They are statutory bodies, not constitutional courts. Their role is principally investigative, recommendatory, advisory and promotional.

2. Constitutional Foundations of Human Rights

  • Article 14: Equality before the law and equal protection of the laws.
  • Article 15: Prohibition of discrimination on specified grounds, with constitutionally permitted special provisions.
  • Article 19: Protection of specified freedoms, subject to constitutionally permitted reasonable restrictions.
  • Article 21: Protection of life and personal liberty. Judicial interpretation has expanded its content to include conditions necessary for a life with dignity.
  • Articles 22 and 23: Safeguards concerning arrest and detention, and prohibition of trafficking and forced labour.
  • Articles 25–30: Protection of religious freedom and cultural and educational rights.
  • Articles 32 and 226: Constitutional remedies before the Supreme Court and High Courts.
  • Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 45 and 46: Directive Principles supporting social justice, legal aid, humane working conditions, education and protection of disadvantaged groups.

Fundamental Rights are judicially enforceable, while Directive Principles are not independently enforceable in court but guide governance and interpretation. Human rights commissions operate within this wider constitutional setting.

3. International Human Rights Framework

India's human rights commitments are also shaped by international instruments and participation in multilateral institutions.

  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948: Sets out a common standard of human rights, including civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights. It is a declaration, not a treaty.
  • International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, 1966: Addresses rights such as life, liberty, fair trial, expression, religion and political participation.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, 1966: Covers work, health, education, social security and an adequate standard of living.
  • Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women: Establishes international obligations concerning discrimination against women.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child: Protects children's civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities: Promotes equality, accessibility, inclusion and participation of persons with disabilities.
  • Convention against Torture: India signed the Convention in 1997 but has not ratified it. This distinction is important when discussing India's international obligations.

International treaties do not automatically replace domestic legislation. Their domestic implementation depends on constitutional provisions, legislation and judicial interpretation. In Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997), the Supreme Court used international conventions consistent with fundamental rights and not inconsistent with domestic law to develop guidelines addressing workplace sexual harassment in the absence of legislation.

4. Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993

The Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993 provides the statutory framework for the NHRC, SHRCs and Human Rights Courts. The Act has been amended, notably in 2006 and 2019. The current institutional framework should therefore be studied with the amended law rather than only the original 1993 text.

The Act defines human rights as rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual, guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in international covenants and enforceable by courts in India.

The 2019 amendment made significant changes to the composition and tenure framework of the Commission, widened the eligibility pool for certain appointments and strengthened the representation of women in the membership structure.

5. National Human Rights Commission: Composition

The NHRC is established by the Central Government under Section 3 of the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993.

Its statutory composition includes:

  • A Chairperson who has been the Chief Justice of India or a Judge of the Supreme Court.
  • One Member who is, or has been, a Judge of the Supreme Court.
  • One Member who is, or has been, the Chief Justice of a High Court.
  • Three Members appointed from among persons having knowledge of, or practical experience in, matters relating to human rights, with at least one being a woman.

The Act also provides for deemed membership of the heads of specified national commissions for the limited purposes set out in the statute. These include the national commissions dealing with Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, women, minorities, backward classes and protection of child rights, as well as the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities.

The Chairperson and Members are appointed by the President on the recommendation of a committee headed by the Prime Minister. The statutory committee includes the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Union Home Minister, the Leaders of Opposition in both Houses of Parliament and the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The Act also provides for the participation of the Deputy Chairman when the statutory conditions relating to the office of the Leader of Opposition are met.

The 2019 amendment permits a person who has been a Judge of the Supreme Court to be appointed as Chairperson, in addition to a former Chief Justice of India. It also modified the eligibility and tenure provisions.

6. Tenure, Removal and Independence

Under the amended Act, the Chairperson and Members hold office for a term of three years or until attaining the age of seventy years, whichever is earlier, and are eligible for reappointment subject to the statutory framework.

The President may remove the Chairperson or a Member on the grounds and through the procedure specified in Section 5. Removal for proved misbehaviour or incapacity requires an inquiry by the Supreme Court following a reference by the President. The Act also specifies other grounds for removal, such as insolvency, conviction involving moral turpitude, engagement in paid employment outside official duties, infirmity of mind or body, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, or abuse of office rendering continuation detrimental to public interest.

These safeguards seek to protect members from arbitrary removal. However, institutional independence also depends on transparent appointments, adequate resources, professional staff, timely vacancies and freedom to investigate without improper influence.

7. Powers and Functions of the NHRC

Section 12 sets out the principal functions of the Commission. These include:

  1. Inquiring, on its own initiative or on a petition, into complaints of human rights violations, abetment or negligence by a public servant in preventing such violations.
  2. Intervening in court proceedings involving allegations of human rights violations, with the approval of the concerned court.
  3. Visiting jails and other institutions under State control where persons are detained or lodged for treatment, reformation or protection, and making recommendations.
  4. Reviewing constitutional and legal safeguards for human rights and recommending measures for their effective implementation.
  5. Reviewing factors, including acts of terrorism, that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights and recommending remedial measures.
  6. Studying treaties and other international instruments on human rights and making recommendations for their effective implementation.
  7. Undertaking and promoting research, spreading human rights literacy and encouraging awareness through publications, seminars and other means.
  8. Encouraging the efforts of non-governmental organisations and institutions working in the field of human rights.

Under Section 13, the Commission has specified powers of a civil court while inquiring into complaints, including summoning witnesses, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits and requisitioning public records. It may also use other procedures authorised by law.

8. Nature and Limits of NHRC Recommendations

The NHRC is not a criminal court and does not ordinarily prosecute offenders or issue binding judicial decrees. Under Section 18, after an inquiry, it may recommend payment of compensation or damages, initiation of prosecution or other suitable action, and may approach the Supreme Court or a High Court for appropriate directions, orders or writs.

The concerned government or authority is required to send its comments on the report and indicate action taken or proposed within the period specified by the Act. The Commission may publish its report along with the government's comments and action taken or proposed.

This recommendatory character is a central institutional limitation. It allows the Commission to expose violations and press for accountability, but implementation often depends on the executive authority concerned.

9. State Human Rights Commissions

Section 21 enables State Governments to constitute SHRCs to exercise the functions assigned under the Act in relation to matters within the State's jurisdiction.

The Chairperson and Members are appointed by the Governor on the recommendation of a committee consisting of the Chief Minister, Speaker of the Legislative Assembly, Home Minister of the State and the Leader of Opposition in the Legislative Assembly. Where the State has a Legislative Council, the statutory provisions also include the Chairman and Leader of Opposition in that Council in the manner specified by the Act.

The SHRC's jurisdiction is limited by the Act. It may inquire into human rights matters relating to subjects in the State List and Concurrent List, subject to the statutory restrictions. It cannot inquire into a matter already being inquired into by the NHRC or another commission duly constituted under law.

State Commissions are important because many human rights concerns arise in areas such as policing, prisons, public health, local administration, land acquisition and delivery of welfare services. Their effectiveness depends on timely appointments, regional accessibility, investigation capacity and cooperation from State authorities.

10. Human Rights Courts and Other Institutions

Section 30 allows the State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to specify a Court of Session in each district as a Human Rights Court for the speedy trial of offences arising out of human rights violations. The State Government may also specify a Special Public Prosecutor for each such court under Section 31.

Human Rights Courts are not a substitute for the NHRC or SHRC. Commissions investigate and recommend action within their statutory powers; courts adjudicate criminal liability and provide judicial remedies according to law.

Other institutions contribute to the human rights framework, including the National Commission for Women, National Commission for Protection of Child Rights, National Commissions for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, National Commission for Minorities and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities. Their mandates may overlap in subject matter, but their legal foundations and powers differ.

11. Landmark Judicial Decisions

  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): Expanded the understanding of Article 21 by requiring that a procedure depriving a person of life or liberty be fair, just and reasonable.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979): Recognised the importance of speedy trial as part of Article 21 and exposed the problem of prolonged detention of undertrial prisoners.
  • D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997): Laid down safeguards against custodial abuse and arbitrary arrest, strengthening protection of life and liberty.
  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): Used international human rights principles to formulate workplace sexual-harassment safeguards in the absence of legislation.
  • People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2003): Addressed the relationship between civil liberties and State power, reinforcing the importance of constitutional safeguards.

12. Major Challenges and Criticism

  • Recommendatory powers: Governments may delay or inadequately implement recommendations.
  • Armed forces jurisdiction: Section 19 creates a special procedure for complaints involving members of the armed forces. The Commission may seek a report from the Central Government and make recommendations, but its ordinary inquiry powers are restricted.
  • One-year limitation: Under Section 36(2), the Commission shall not inquire into a matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the alleged act constituting the human rights violation is said to have been committed.
  • Vacancies and capacity: Delayed appointments, inadequate staff and dependence on external investigative agencies weaken timely inquiry.
  • Investigation concerns: Dependence on government agencies for investigation may create concerns about independence, especially where the alleged violation involves the same administration.
  • Overlap and coordination: Multiple commissions can create jurisdictional confusion and fragmented follow-up.
  • Limited public awareness: Vulnerable communities may lack information, legal assistance or access to complaint mechanisms.
  • Implementation deficit: Compensation or corrective recommendations do not always lead to systemic reform or prosecution where warranted.

13. Reforms and Way Forward

  1. Fill vacancies promptly through transparent, merit-based appointment procedures.
  2. Provide adequate independent investigation staff, forensic support and regional offices.
  3. Strengthen follow-up mechanisms so that governments provide reasoned responses and comply with recommendations.
  4. Improve coordination among the NHRC, SHRCs, specialised commissions, police oversight bodies and courts.
  5. Make complaint procedures accessible in regional languages and through secure digital systems.
  6. Strengthen prison monitoring, custodial safeguards and independent inquiry into allegations of abuse.
  7. Publish disaggregated data, annual reports and compliance information to enable public and legislative scrutiny.
  8. Promote human rights education among police, prison officials, civil servants, local bodies and educational institutions.
  9. Review statutory limitations and procedural barriers while preserving safeguards against stale or unsubstantiated claims.

14. Prelims-Oriented Facts

  • The NHRC and SHRCs are statutory bodies under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993.
  • The 2019 amendment changed the composition and tenure framework of the NHRC and SHRCs.
  • The NHRC Chairperson is appointed by the President; the SHRC Chairperson is appointed by the Governor.
  • The NHRC can inquire into complaints, visit institutions, review safeguards, promote research and recommend remedial action.
  • The NHRC's recommendations are not equivalent to binding judgments of a constitutional court.
  • Section 19 prescribes a special procedure for matters involving the armed forces.
  • Section 30 provides for Human Rights Courts, subject to the statutory process.
  • Section 36(2) imposes a one-year bar on inquiry into an alleged violation after the relevant date.

15. Mains Perspective and Conclusion

Human rights commissions bridge the gap between constitutional ideals and administrative practice. They provide an institutional channel for complaints, monitor conditions in custodial institutions, promote awareness and place violations in the public domain. Their contribution is particularly important for people who face barriers in accessing ordinary judicial remedies.

However, a commission's credibility depends on more than the number of complaints registered or disposed of. Its independence, investigative capacity, accessibility, quality of recommendations and the extent of government compliance are decisive.

Conclusion: Human rights commissions should be strengthened as independent, accessible and professionally capable institutions. Their recommendations must translate into remedies for victims and systemic changes in administration, policing, prisons and public service delivery.
