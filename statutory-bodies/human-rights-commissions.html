1. Human rights: constitutional and institutional foundations

Human rights are claims and freedoms inherent in human dignity. They protect individuals and groups against arbitrary power and require the State to respect, protect and, in appropriate circumstances, fulfil conditions necessary for a dignified life. In India, the constitutional framework is principally found in the Preamble, Fundamental Rights, Directive Principles of State Policy and the institutional remedies available through constitutional courts.

Part III protects civil and political rights through enforceable guarantees. Articles 14 and 21 have been interpreted to require fairness, non-arbitrariness and protection against unlawful deprivation of life and personal liberty. Articles 15 and 16 address discrimination and equality of opportunity; Articles 19 protect specified freedoms; Articles 23 and 24 prohibit particular forms of exploitation; and Articles 25 to 30 protect religious and cultural interests.

Part IV recognises social and economic dimensions of dignity. Articles 38, 39, 39A, 41, 42, 43, 45, 46 and 47 guide the State in pursuing social justice, legal aid, humane working conditions, education, protection of weaker sections and public health. Although Directive Principles are not directly enforceable in the same manner as Fundamental Rights, they inform constitutional interpretation and public policy.

Human rights commissions supplement courts and administrative institutions. They provide an accessible forum for inquiry, monitor safeguards, identify patterns of abuse, recommend relief and advise governments. They do not replace criminal courts, civil courts, constitutional remedies or the responsibility of police and executive authorities to prevent and investigate violations.

2. International framework and the Paris Principles

The international human-rights framework includes the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948, the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, 1966, the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, 1966, and specialised conventions addressing discrimination, torture, children, women, persons with disabilities and migrant workers.

India is a party to several major international human-rights instruments. International commitments inform domestic policy and interpretation, but their enforceability in Indian courts depends on constitutional principles, domestic legislation and the nature of the treaty obligation. Article 51(c) directs the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations; Article 253 empowers Parliament to make laws for implementing treaties and international agreements.

The Paris Principles, adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1993, set standards for national human rights institutions. They emphasise a broad mandate, independence, pluralism, adequate powers, adequate resources, accessibility, cooperation with civil society and engagement with international human-rights mechanisms.

Accreditation by the Global Alliance of National Human Rights Institutions is a peer-review assessment of compliance with the Paris Principles. “A status” indicates compliance and “B status” indicates partial compliance. Accreditation is not a substitute for domestic legal authority; it is an assessment of institutional independence, credibility and effectiveness.

3. National Human Rights Commission: statutory basis

The National Human Rights Commission was established under the Protection of Human Rights Act, 1993. The Act provides for the NHRC, State Human Rights Commissions and Human Rights Courts. The Commission's mandate is to protect and promote human rights as defined by the Act, namely rights relating to life, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual guaranteed by the Constitution or embodied in international covenants and enforceable by courts in India.

The Protection of Human Rights (Amendment) Act, 2019 modified the composition and tenure framework, among other provisions. The amended Act should be read as the operative statutory basis; older descriptions of composition and tenure may be inaccurate.

4. Composition and appointment of the NHRC

Section 3 of the Protection of Human Rights Act provides for the Commission's composition. The Chairperson is a person who has been the Chief Justice of India or a Judge of the Supreme Court. The judicial members include one person who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court and one person who is or has been the Chief Justice of a High Court. The Act also provides for three members drawn from persons with knowledge of or practical experience in human-rights matters, of whom at least one must be a woman.

The Chairpersons of specified national commissions are deemed members for the discharge of functions specified in Section 12(b) to 12(j). These include the national commissions for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, Backward Classes, Minorities, Women and Protection of Child Rights, as well as the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities, as provided in the amended law.

The President appoints the Chairperson and members on the recommendation of a statutory committee. The committee includes the Prime Minister as Chairperson, the Speaker of the Lok Sabha, the Union Home Minister, the leaders of opposition in both Houses and the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha. The statutory scheme also provides for the participation of the Deputy Chairman of the Rajya Sabha in the selection committee.

The Secretary-General is the chief executive officer of the Commission and exercises administrative and financial powers subject to the statutory framework and the control of the Chairperson. The headquarters are at New Delhi, with provision for offices elsewhere subject to the Act.

4.1 Tenure and removal

The 2019 amendment reduced the tenure framework to three years or until the age of seventy, whichever is earlier, subject to the provisions governing reappointment. The Act specifies grounds and procedures for removal, including removal by the President on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity after an inquiry by the Supreme Court in the manner prescribed by the statute. Other specified grounds, such as insolvency, paid employment outside official duties, infirmity of mind or body, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, or conviction involving moral turpitude, are governed by the Act.

Security of tenure is intended to protect independent decision-making. It must be accompanied by transparent appointments, adequate pluralism and accountability for performance.

5. Functions of the NHRC: Section 12

Section 12 gives the Commission a broad promotional and protective mandate. Its functions include:

  • Inquiring, on its own initiative or on a petition, into complaints of violation of human rights or abetment or negligence in preventing such violation by a public servant.
  • Intervening in court proceedings involving allegations of human-rights violations, with the approval of the court.
  • Visiting jails and other institutions under State control where persons are detained or lodged for treatment, reformation or protection, and making recommendations to the government.
  • Reviewing constitutional and legal safeguards for human-rights protection and recommending measures for effective implementation.
  • Reviewing factors, including acts of terrorism, that inhibit the enjoyment of human rights and recommending remedial measures.
  • Studying international treaties and instruments on human rights and making recommendations for their effective implementation.
  • Undertaking and promoting research in the field of human rights.
  • Spreading human-rights literacy and promoting awareness through publications, media, seminars and other means.
  • Encouraging the efforts of non-governmental organisations and institutions working in the field of human rights.

This combination of inquiry, review, research and awareness functions makes the NHRC more than a complaint-handling office. It is also expected to identify systemic failures and recommend structural reform.

6. Inquiry powers and procedure

6.1 Powers of a civil court

Under Section 13, while inquiring into complaints, the Commission has specified powers of a civil court under the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. These include summoning and enforcing the attendance of witnesses, examining them on oath, requiring discovery and production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, requisitioning public records and issuing commissions for examination of witnesses or documents.

The Commission may also require information from a person, subject to the statutory provisions. Proceedings before it are treated as judicial proceedings for specified purposes under the law. These powers facilitate fact-finding but do not convert the Commission into a criminal court with general power to convict and sentence offenders.

6.2 Investigation

Section 14 permits the Commission, with the approval of the appropriate government, to utilise the services of an officer or investigation agency of the Central or State Government. The Act also provides for its own investigation staff. The use of police personnel can create a perceived or actual conflict when allegations concern police misconduct. This makes independent investigation capacity and transparent safeguards particularly important.

6.3 Inquiry procedure

Section 17 sets out the procedure relating to complaints. The Commission may call for information or reports from the Central Government or a State Government or other authority. If the report is not received within the specified period, it may proceed with the inquiry on the basis of available material. If a report is received, the Commission may close the matter, proceed further or take other action in accordance with the Act.

Natural justice requires that persons whose conduct is under examination receive a fair opportunity to respond, subject to lawful procedural limits. The Commission must distinguish allegations, evidence, findings and recommendations in its orders.

7. Remedies and recommendations: Section 18

After completing an inquiry, the Commission may take steps authorised by Section 18. These include recommending payment of compensation or damages to the complainant or victim or members of the victim's family; recommending initiation of prosecution or other suitable action against the concerned person; recommending immediate interim relief; approaching the Supreme Court or a High Court for appropriate directions, orders or writs; and recommending further action to the government or authority concerned.

The Commission may also provide a copy of its inquiry report to the petitioner or the person concerned and publish the report together with the comments of the government or authority and the action taken or proposed. The statutory scheme requires the government or authority to indicate its comments and action within the period specified by the Act.

The NHRC's recommendations are generally recommendatory rather than equivalent to binding decrees of a constitutional court. Their influence depends on the quality of inquiry, public credibility, reasoned findings, legislative scrutiny and the willingness of authorities to implement them. A recommendation should not be presented as a conviction or a final judicial determination.

8. Special provision relating to armed forces: Section 19

Section 19 provides a special procedure where the alleged human-rights violation is committed by members of the armed forces. The Commission may seek a report from the Central Government and, after considering it, either refrain from proceeding or make recommendations to the government. The government is required to inform the Commission of the action taken within the statutory period, and the Commission may publish its report and recommendations along with the government's response.

This arrangement has been criticised because the Commission's direct investigative powers are more restricted in armed-forces cases than in ordinary cases. The issue is especially significant in areas affected by insurgency, counter-insurgency operations and special security laws. National security is a legitimate public concern, but it does not eliminate the need for independent scrutiny, effective remedies and accountability for unlawful conduct.

9. Limitations on jurisdiction

Section 36 restricts the Commission's jurisdiction in specified circumstances. It does not inquire into a matter that is pending before a State Commission or another commission duly constituted under law. It also cannot inquire into a matter after the expiry of one year from the date on which the act constituting the alleged human-rights violation is alleged to have been committed.

The one-year limitation can be difficult where victims face fear, displacement, stigma, lack of legal assistance or inability to access institutions. It can also complicate complaints involving continuing consequences. The statutory text and judicial interpretation must be applied carefully rather than assuming that every continuing effect automatically removes the limitation.

The Commission's jurisdiction is also shaped by the statutory definition of human rights, the requirement that the violation relate to a public servant or public authority in the manner contemplated by the Act, and the relationship between the Commission's inquiry and proceedings before courts or other commissions.

10. State Human Rights Commissions

Sections 21 onwards provide for State Human Rights Commissions. A State Government may constitute a State Commission to exercise the functions assigned under the Act in relation to matters within the State's jurisdiction. Its composition, appointment, tenure, removal, functions and powers are governed by the statutory scheme, with the 2019 amendments applying where relevant.

State Commissions are important because many human-rights violations arise in areas administered primarily by State governments: police, prisons, public hospitals, local administration, land acquisition, social welfare delivery and custodial institutions.

Their effectiveness varies with appointments, staff, investigative capacity, regional access, budgetary support and the cooperation of State departments. A State Commission without adequate personnel or timely access to records may be unable to conduct meaningful inquiries.

The division of functions between the NHRC and SHRCs should not lead to a gap in protection. Clear referral systems, information sharing and coordination are needed, while respecting the statutory limits on parallel inquiries.

11. Human Rights Courts

Section 30 enables a State Government, with the concurrence of the Chief Justice of the High Court, to specify a Court of Session as a Human Rights Court for each district to try offences arising out of violations of human rights. Section 31 provides for a Special Public Prosecutor for every Human Rights Court.

The statutory provision aims to provide a specialised criminal justice response. Its practical impact depends on notification of courts, appointment of prosecutors, awareness, coordination with ordinary criminal courts and the availability of evidence and witness protection. The mere existence of a provision does not establish that every district has a fully functioning specialised court.

12. Other national commissions and the wider protection architecture

The NHRC operates alongside specialised commissions created for particular groups or rights. The National Commission for Women, National Commission for Minorities, National Commission for Backward Classes, National Commission for Scheduled Castes, National Commission for Scheduled Tribes, National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities have distinct mandates under their respective laws.

These bodies can provide expertise and focused attention to structural discrimination. Their mandates may overlap where an individual belongs to more than one vulnerable group or where a violation implicates several rights. Coordination should prevent duplication without forcing a victim to navigate an unnecessarily fragmented system.

Constitutional commissions such as the NCSC and NCST have a different constitutional status from statutory commissions. Their powers and reporting arrangements arise from constitutional provisions, particularly Articles 338 and 338A. This distinction should be maintained when analysing institutional design.

13. Major human-rights concerns in India

13.1 Custodial violence and deaths

Custodial violence raises concerns under Articles 14 and 21 and engages the State's duty to prevent abuse by public officials. Effective response requires prompt registration and investigation, medical examination, preservation of CCTV and station records, independent post-mortem procedures, witness protection and prosecution where evidence supports it.

Commissions can monitor custodial deaths, seek reports, examine systemic practices and recommend compensation or institutional reform. Their work should be assessed by the quality of follow-up and accountability rather than the number of notices issued.

13.2 Prison conditions

Overcrowding, undertrial detention, inadequate healthcare, poor sanitation, violence and limited access to legal aid undermine dignity. The right to life under Article 21 extends to persons in custody. Prison reform requires coordination among prison departments, courts, legal services authorities, health agencies and human-rights institutions.

13.3 Discrimination and social exclusion

Discrimination based on caste, gender, disability, religion, ethnicity, language or other status can restrict access to education, housing, employment, justice and public services. Rights commissions should combine individual complaint resolution with examination of institutional practices and structural barriers.

13.4 Rights of women and children

Violence, trafficking, child labour, child marriage, sexual abuse and barriers to education require a coordinated protection framework. Specialised legislation and commissions must be supported by trained police, child-protection services, accessible courts, shelters, health services and rehabilitation.

13.5 Displacement and development projects

Land acquisition, infrastructure projects, mining, conservation measures and urban redevelopment may affect livelihood, housing, cultural rights and access to natural resources. Human-rights assessment should examine consultation, rehabilitation, compensation, environmental impacts and the position of affected communities.

13.6 Digital rights and surveillance

Digital systems can improve access to services but may also create risks involving privacy, profiling, exclusion, surveillance and opaque automated decisions. Rights institutions must understand the relationship between constitutional privacy, data protection, equality and due process.

14. Judicial principles and important decisions

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): The Supreme Court expanded the understanding of Article 21 by requiring a procedure depriving a person of life or personal liberty to be just, fair and reasonable, and not arbitrary.

D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997): The Court laid down safeguards concerning arrest and detention to reduce custodial abuse and strengthen accountability.

Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993): The Court recognised public-law compensation for violation of fundamental rights, particularly in the context of custodial death.

Joginder Kumar v. State of Uttar Pradesh (1994): The Court emphasised that arrest should not be routine and that safeguards are necessary to protect personal liberty.

People's Union for Democratic Rights v. Union of India (1982): The Court gave substantive effect to Article 23 and examined exploitation in labour conditions.

Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017): The Supreme Court recognised privacy as a fundamental right intrinsic to life and personal liberty and other freedoms. This has major implications for surveillance, data governance and dignity.

These judgments establish constitutional standards that human-rights commissions can draw upon while examining complaints and recommending systemic improvements. They do not enlarge the Commission's statutory jurisdiction beyond the Act.

15. Performance assessment and recurring institutional challenges

15.1 Recommendatory character

The Commission's reliance on recommendations can limit immediate enforcement. Governments may accept, partially implement or reject recommendations. Public reporting and legislative scrutiny are therefore essential to ensure that non-compliance is visible and explained.

15.2 Dependence on government machinery

Investigations may rely on State agencies, including police personnel. Where the allegation concerns the same agency, institutional independence and public confidence may be weakened. Independent investigators, forensic capacity and transparent case allocation can address this concern.

15.3 Vacancies and inadequate staffing

Vacancies in leadership and investigative posts reduce throughput and institutional continuity. A rights body should not be left without decision-making capacity for extended periods.

15.4 Narrow social representation

Pluralism requires more than formal eligibility. Appointment processes should encourage participation by women, minorities, persons with disabilities, representatives with lived experience and experts from different regions and disciplines.

15.5 Limited awareness and access

Victims may not know the Commission's jurisdiction, limitation period or procedure. Language barriers, distance, digital exclusion and fear of retaliation can prevent complaints. Regional outreach, accessible formats and legal assistance are important.

15.6 Weak follow-up data

Institutional reporting should distinguish complaints received, matters admitted, inquiries completed, recommendations issued, compensation recommended, prosecutions initiated and recommendations implemented. Aggregate totals without context may conceal delays or weak outcomes.

16. Reform agenda

  • Strengthen transparent and pluralistic selection procedures, with publicly accessible eligibility and vacancy information.
  • Provide adequate and predictable financial, investigative and regional resources.
  • Build an independent multidisciplinary investigation capacity, including forensic, medical, legal and social-science expertise.
  • Improve safeguards where public officials investigate allegations against their own departments.
  • Publish disaggregated data on custodial deaths, complaints, inquiries, recommendations and implementation.
  • Establish clear follow-up timelines and require reasoned responses to recommendations.
  • Improve coordination between the NHRC, SHRCs, specialised commissions, courts and legal services authorities.
  • Ensure effective access for persons with disabilities, linguistic minorities, rural communities and people in detention.
  • Strengthen Human Rights Courts where the statutory framework is operationalised, including prosecutors and witness support.
  • Align institutional practice with the Paris Principles on independence, pluralism, resources and cooperation with civil society.

17. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

Human-rights commissions are important components of India's accountability architecture because they offer specialised scrutiny beyond ordinary departmental processes. Their value lies in accessible inquiry, systemic review, public reporting and recommendations that can expose patterns of abuse.

Their effectiveness, however, depends on independence from the institutions they scrutinise, pluralistic membership, professional investigation, adequate resources and meaningful government response. A commission that can issue notices but cannot secure reliable evidence or follow up recommendations risks becoming a procedural rather than protective institution.

The appropriate reform approach is to strengthen investigative independence, improve transparency, protect complainants and witnesses, coordinate specialised institutions and ensure that constitutional courts remain accessible. Human-rights protection must ultimately be measured by prevention of abuse, accountability for violations and restoration of dignity.

19. Key terms

National Human Rights Institution: An independent public institution mandated to promote and protect human rights at the national level.

Paris Principles: International standards concerning the mandate, independence, pluralism, powers and effectiveness of national human-rights institutions.

Suo motu: Action initiated by an institution on its own motion without waiting for a formal complaint.

Custodial violence: Physical or mental abuse, torture or other unlawful treatment inflicted on a person in custody.

Public-law compensation: Monetary relief granted by constitutional courts for violation of fundamental rights, distinct from ordinary private-law damages.
