1. Environmental justice and specialised adjudication

Environmental disputes frequently involve complex scientific evidence, competing public interests and questions of long-term ecological risk. Industrial pollution, forest diversion, biodiversity loss, hazardous waste, mining, infrastructure development and water contamination may affect communities across administrative boundaries and generations.

Ordinary civil courts can adjudicate environmental disputes, but specialised environmental adjudication requires expertise in ecology, environmental engineering, public health, impact assessment and regulatory science. It also requires the capacity to balance economic development with environmental protection and constitutional rights.

The National Green Tribunal (NGT) was established to provide specialised and relatively speedy adjudication of environmental disputes. It combines judicial members with expert members and applies principles such as sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle.

The NGT is a statutory tribunal, not a constitutional court. Its jurisdiction is defined by the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010, and it operates within the wider constitutional framework of environmental protection.

2. Constitutional foundations of environmental protection

2.1 Article 21

Article 21 protects life and personal liberty. Judicial interpretation has recognised that the right to life includes the right to enjoy a clean and healthy environment. Pollution affecting air, water, public health and ecological conditions may therefore raise constitutional concerns.

2.2 Article 48A

Article 48A, a Directive Principle of State Policy, directs the State to protect and improve the environment and to safeguard forests and wildlife. It provides constitutional guidance for environmental legislation and public administration.

2.3 Article 51A(g)

Article 51A(g) makes it a fundamental duty of every citizen to protect and improve the natural environment, including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife, and to have compassion for living creatures.

2.4 Legislative competence

Environmental governance draws on several entries in the Seventh Schedule and on Parliament's legislative competence under constitutional provisions, including Article 253 for implementing international agreements. The 42nd Constitutional Amendment transferred forests and protection of wild animals and birds to the Concurrent List. Water and public health also involve important State responsibilities.

Environmental regulation is therefore a shared governance responsibility. The Union, States, local bodies and specialised authorities have interconnected roles.

3. Evolution of environmental adjudication in India

Environmental litigation in India developed through constitutional remedies, public interest litigation, statutory pollution-control authorities and specialised judicial procedures. The Supreme Court and High Courts played a major role in developing environmental principles through cases involving industrial hazards, river pollution, forest conservation and public health.

The increasing volume and technical complexity of environmental disputes demonstrated the need for a specialised forum. The Law Commission and other expert bodies examined institutional options. The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 created a specialised tribunal with jurisdiction over civil cases involving substantial environmental questions and appeals under specified environmental statutes.

The NGT consolidated aspects of environmental adjudication that had previously been spread across different forums. Its creation reflected a shift towards expert, multidisciplinary and principle-based environmental decision-making.

4. National Green Tribunal Act, 2010

4.1 Establishment

The National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 provides for the establishment of the NGT for effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection, conservation of forests and other natural resources, enforcement of environmental rights and relief and compensation for damage to persons and property.

4.2 Composition

The Tribunal consists of a Chairperson, Judicial Members and Expert Members. The Act provides for a minimum and maximum strength within the statutory framework. The multidisciplinary composition is intended to combine legal reasoning with scientific and technical expertise.

The Chairperson is a person who is or has been a Judge of the Supreme Court or Chief Justice of a High Court, subject to the statutory requirements. Judicial Members are drawn from persons with the specified judicial qualifications. Expert Members are selected from persons with the prescribed professional and technical experience in relevant fields such as environmental science, engineering, administration and related disciplines.

4.3 Benches and territorial reach

The NGT functions through its principal bench and zonal benches. Its bench structure is intended to improve geographical access to environmental adjudication. The location and functioning of benches are relevant to access for communities, industries and public authorities across a large country.

5. Jurisdiction of the NGT

5.1 Original jurisdiction: Section 14

Section 14 gives the NGT jurisdiction over all civil cases where a substantial question relating to the environment is involved, including enforcement of any legal right relating to the environment, and where the question arises out of the implementation of enactments listed in Schedule I to the Act.

A substantial question relating to the environment may arise where there is a direct violation of a specific statutory environmental obligation and the impact is significant, or where the environmental consequences affect the community at large or a wider geographical area. The statutory definition and judicial interpretation must be considered when determining jurisdiction.

The NGT does not possess unlimited jurisdiction over every dispute that has an environmental dimension. The case must fall within the statutory framework, including the scheduled enactments and the nature of the civil dispute.

5.2 Appeals: Section 16

Section 16 gives the NGT appellate jurisdiction over specified orders and decisions under the environmental laws listed in the provision. These include certain decisions under the Water Act, Air Act, Environment (Protection) Act, Forest (Conservation) Act, Biological Diversity Act and other enactments as specified by the Act.

The appeal must comply with the relevant statutory conditions, including limitation periods, standing requirements and the nature of the impugned order. Not every administrative action relating to the environment is automatically appealable to the NGT.

5.3 Relief and compensation: Section 15

Section 15 empowers the Tribunal to provide relief and compensation to victims of pollution and other environmental damage, restitution of property damaged and restitution of the environment for the areas within its jurisdiction.

This remedial power recognises that environmental harm may require more than an order stopping an unlawful activity. It may require restoration of ecosystems, compensation for affected people and remedial measures to prevent recurrence.

5.4 Limitation

Section 14 prescribes a limitation period of six months from the date on which the cause of action first arose, with a possible further period not exceeding sixty days where sufficient cause is shown. Section 15 provides a five-year limitation period for applications for relief, compensation or restitution, with a possible further period not exceeding sixty days for sufficient cause. Section 16 prescribes a period of thirty days for appeals, with a possible further period not exceeding sixty days.

Limitation rules are important because environmental harm may be gradual, cumulative or discovered only after technical investigation. Applicants must nevertheless comply with the statutory periods and explain any request for extension.

6. Schedule I environmental enactments

The NGT's jurisdiction is connected to the enactments listed in Schedule I to the 2010 Act. These include:

  • Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.
  • Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Cess Act, 1977, as reflected in the statutory schedule and subsequent legal changes.
  • Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980, now renamed the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, 1980.
  • Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981.
  • Environment (Protection) Act, 1986.
  • Public Liability Insurance Act, 1991.
  • Biological Diversity Act, 2002.

The precise scope of NGT jurisdiction must be checked against the current text of the Act and the relevant scheduled enactment. Amendments, repeals and changes in statutory names should not be assumed to expand or contract jurisdiction without examining the operative legal provisions.

7. Procedure and powers

The NGT is not bound by the procedure laid down in the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908. It is guided by principles of natural justice and has power to regulate its own procedure, subject to the Act and applicable rules.

For specified purposes, the Tribunal has powers of a civil court, including summoning persons, requiring production of documents, receiving evidence on affidavits, issuing commissions and reviewing its decisions within the statutory framework.

The Tribunal may call for expert reports, examine scientific evidence, require environmental monitoring and direct remedial measures. Its multidisciplinary composition allows technical issues to be assessed alongside legal principles.

Proceedings should remain fair to all parties. Industries, communities, public authorities and affected persons must have an opportunity to present evidence and respond to material relied upon by the Tribunal.

8. Environmental principles applied by the NGT

8.1 Sustainable development

Sustainable development seeks to reconcile economic and social development with ecological limits and the needs of future generations. It does not mean that every development project must be approved, nor does it require a complete halt to development. It requires decisions to consider environmental carrying capacity, social costs, mitigation and long-term consequences.

8.2 Precautionary principle

The precautionary principle requires preventive action where there is a credible risk of serious or irreversible environmental harm, even if scientific certainty is incomplete. It recognises that waiting for conclusive proof may make damage impossible to reverse.

The principle does not justify arbitrary restrictions. Decisions should be proportionate to the risk and supported by the best available scientific evidence, transparent reasoning and appropriate review.

8.3 Polluter pays principle

The polluter pays principle requires the person or entity responsible for pollution to bear the costs of preventing, controlling and remedying environmental damage. It may include compensation to affected persons and the cost of ecological restoration.

The principle is not merely punitive. It internalises environmental costs that would otherwise be imposed on communities and the public exchequer.

8.4 Inter-generational equity

Inter-generational equity recognises that natural resources and ecological systems must be managed in a manner that does not unfairly transfer environmental costs to future generations. It is relevant to forests, groundwater, biodiversity, climate risks and long-lived infrastructure.

8.5 Public trust doctrine

The public trust doctrine treats certain natural resources as resources that the State holds in trust for public use and ecological protection. Government authorities cannot treat such resources as ordinary private assets to be disposed of without regard to public and environmental interests.

9. Landmark judicial principles

Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996): The Supreme Court recognised the precautionary principle and polluter pays principle as essential features of sustainable development in Indian environmental law.

Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India (1996): The Court applied the polluter pays principle and emphasised that polluting industries must bear the cost of remedial measures.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (Oleum Gas Leak, 1987): The Supreme Court developed the principle of absolute liability for hazardous industries, strengthening accountability for industrial harm.

Municipal Council, Ratlam v. Vardhichand (1980): The Court emphasised public authorities' duty to address serious public nuisance and sanitation problems, demonstrating the connection between environmental conditions and public health.

T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India: The continuing forest litigation developed important principles relating to forest conservation and judicial oversight of environmental administration.

Hanuman Laxman Aroskar v. Union of India (2019): The Supreme Court emphasised reasoned environmental decision-making and the need for environmental impact assessment processes to be informed, transparent and lawful.

These cases form part of the broader legal environment within which the NGT operates. They are not all decisions of the Tribunal; many are Supreme Court judgments that establish principles applied in environmental adjudication.

10. Environmental Impact Assessment and regulatory review

Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) is a process for identifying and evaluating the likely environmental consequences of proposed projects before decisions are made. It can include screening, scoping, baseline data collection, impact prediction, public consultation, appraisal and post-clearance monitoring.

The EIA framework under the Environment (Protection) Act and the applicable notification is central to environmental governance. The NGT may examine the legality of environmental clearance decisions and the adequacy of compliance with statutory procedures where the dispute falls within its jurisdiction.

Environmental assessment should not be reduced to a procedural checklist. Its quality depends on credible baseline data, independent assessment, meaningful public participation, disclosure of relevant information, examination of alternatives and effective monitoring.

Public hearings can give affected communities an opportunity to raise concerns about pollution, displacement, livelihood impacts, water availability and ecological risks. Their views must be considered meaningfully rather than treated as a formality.

11. NGT and federal environmental governance

Environmental governance involves the Union Government, State Governments, pollution control boards, forest departments, local authorities and specialised agencies. The NGT provides an adjudicatory forum when disputes arise under the statutory framework.

Central and State Pollution Control Boards perform regulatory and monitoring functions under the Water Act and Air Act. The NGT does not replace these boards. It may examine their actions or failures where a dispute falls within its jurisdiction and may direct appropriate remedial action.

Local bodies have responsibilities relating to waste management, sanitation, water bodies and land use. Effective environmental protection therefore requires cooperation between adjudicatory institutions and the executive agencies that implement environmental law.

12. Appeals and judicial review

Section 22 of the NGT Act provides for an appeal to the Supreme Court against an award, decision or order of the Tribunal, subject to the statutory grounds and limitation framework. The Supreme Court's appellate role is an important safeguard against legal error.

Judicial review and appellate scrutiny ensure that the Tribunal acts within its jurisdiction, applies the correct legal principles, follows fair procedure and gives reasoned decisions. Specialisation does not remove the need for constitutional and legal accountability.

In practice, the quality of NGT adjudication depends on clear findings, proper evaluation of expert evidence, enforceable remedial directions and monitoring of compliance.

13. Challenges facing the NGT

13.1 Vacancies and capacity

Vacancies among judicial and expert members can delay hearings and reduce the Tribunal's ability to deal with technically complex disputes. Timely appointments and adequate support staff are essential.

13.2 Access and geographical reach

Environmental harm frequently affects rural, tribal and remote communities. Travel costs, legal complexity, language barriers and limited awareness may prevent affected people from accessing the Tribunal.

13.3 Enforcement of orders

A strong order is ineffective if regulators and project authorities do not implement it. Monitoring, compliance reporting, technical verification and consequences for non-compliance are necessary.

13.4 Scientific evidence

Environmental disputes involve uncertainty, cumulative impacts and complex causal relationships. The Tribunal requires independent expert support and transparent methods for assessing competing scientific claims.

13.5 Delay and case management

Specialised adjudication is intended to be expeditious, but high caseloads, repeated adjournments and incomplete regulatory records can cause delay. Better case management and early identification of technical issues can improve performance.

13.6 Jurisdictional boundaries

Not every environmental issue falls within the NGT's statutory jurisdiction. Disputes involving laws outside Schedule I or matters that are not civil cases within Section 14 may require other legal remedies. Clear public guidance is necessary.

13.7 Balancing development and ecological protection

The Tribunal must avoid both uncritical approval of development and indiscriminate prohibition. It should examine evidence, alternatives, mitigation, environmental limits and distribution of costs and benefits.

14. Reform agenda

  • Fill judicial and expert vacancies through transparent and timely appointments.
  • Strengthen independent scientific and technical research support.
  • Improve access through effective regional benches, digital procedures and multilingual assistance.
  • Establish systematic monitoring of compliance with environmental orders.
  • Improve coordination with pollution control boards and other environmental regulators.
  • Promote transparent EIA processes and meaningful public participation.
  • Develop consistent methods for assessing cumulative environmental impacts and ecological restoration costs.
  • Provide accessible information on jurisdiction, limitation and remedies.
  • Strengthen enforcement against repeat violations while ensuring proportionate and reasoned decisions.
  • Improve environmental data quality and public access to monitoring information.

15. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

The NGT represents a significant institutional development in environmental governance because it combines specialised adjudication with scientific expertise and established environmental principles. It helps convert constitutional commitments to environmental protection into legal remedies and enforceable directions.

Its effectiveness depends on independence, adequate membership, access for affected communities, quality of scientific evidence and implementation of its orders. A specialised tribunal cannot substitute for competent regulators or sound environmental administration.

India needs an integrated environmental justice framework in which prevention, transparent regulation, public participation, specialised adjudication and ecological restoration reinforce one another. The NGT is most effective when it is supported by capable institutions and a government that treats environmental compliance as a core public responsibility.

16. Prelims fact file

  • The NGT was established under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
  • It is a statutory tribunal, not a constitutional court.
  • Section 14 concerns original jurisdiction over substantial environmental questions within the statutory framework.
  • Section 15 concerns relief, compensation and restitution.
  • Section 16 concerns appeals under specified environmental enactments.
  • Section 20 requires the Tribunal to apply sustainable development, the precautionary principle and the polluter pays principle.
  • Section 22 provides for appeal to the Supreme Court.
  • The NGT is not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, but is guided by natural justice.
  • Its composition includes Judicial Members and Expert Members.
  • Article 48A is a Directive Principle of State Policy.
  • Article 51A(g) is a fundamental duty.
  • Article 21 has been judicially interpreted to include environmental dimensions of the right to life.

17. Key terms

Sustainable development: Development that reconciles present needs with environmental protection and the interests of future generations.

Precautionary principle: Preventive action in response to credible risks of serious or irreversible harm despite scientific uncertainty.

Polluter pays: The polluter bears the costs of prevention, control and remediation of environmental damage.

Environmental restitution: Restoration of damaged ecosystems and environmental conditions.

Environmental Impact Assessment: A process for identifying and evaluating the likely environmental consequences of proposed projects.
