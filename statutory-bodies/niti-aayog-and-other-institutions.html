1. Institutions beyond the conventional constitutional structure

Public administration in India involves institutions that do not all derive their authority from the Constitution or from a single category of legislation. Some are constitutional bodies, some are statutory authorities, some are executive institutions created through government resolutions, and others are advisory or coordinating mechanisms.

These institutions perform functions such as policy advice, intergovernmental coordination, economic planning, research, evaluation, capacity-building and programme monitoring. Their effectiveness depends on the clarity of their mandate, quality of evidence, institutional credibility and ability to influence implementation.

This chapter focuses on NITI Aayog and selected national institutions that contribute to policy coordination, development strategy and governance. Their legal status and powers must be distinguished carefully: an advisory institution does not acquire executive or statutory powers merely because it influences important government decisions.

2. Planning Commission: historical background

The Planning Commission was established in March 1950 through an executive resolution of the Government of India. It was not created by the Constitution or by an Act of Parliament.

Its principal role was to assess national resources, formulate plans for their effective use, determine development priorities and advise the government on economic planning. It became closely associated with India's Five-Year Plans and the centralised planning framework adopted after independence.

The Planning Commission played an important role in directing public investment, setting national development priorities and coordinating sectoral plans. Its approach reflected the economic and institutional conditions of the early decades after independence.

Over time, changes in the economy, growth of private investment, liberalisation, global integration and the increasing role of States generated debate about the suitability of centralised planning and the allocation of resources through a central planning institution.

3. Establishment of NITI Aayog

The Government of India established the National Institution for Transforming India (NITI Aayog) through an executive resolution on 1 January 2015. It replaced the Planning Commission.

NITI Aayog is neither a constitutional body nor a statutory body. It is an executive institution established by a government resolution. Its authority and functions arise from that institutional arrangement and subsequent government decisions.

The change reflected a shift away from the traditional centralised planning model towards a framework emphasising cooperative federalism, competitive federalism, strategic policy advice, innovation and outcome-oriented development.

NITI Aayog does not possess the same constitutional or statutory powers as a regulator, commission or tribunal. It generally performs advisory, coordinating, research and monitoring functions rather than exercising independent coercive authority over States or private entities.

4. Rationale for replacing the Planning Commission

The institutional transition was associated with several developments:

  • Economic transformation: The Indian economy had become more market-oriented and integrated with the global economy.
  • State-level diversity: States had different development needs, capacities and policy priorities.
  • Cooperative federalism: National development increasingly required partnership with States rather than a predominantly centralised approach.
  • Changing public finance: The Finance Commission's constitutional role and changes in fiscal arrangements altered the context in which central transfers and planning operated.
  • Outcome orientation: Greater attention was required for implementation, measurable outcomes and evidence-based evaluation.
  • Innovation: Policy design needed to respond to technology, entrepreneurship, demographic change and new economic opportunities.

The replacement did not mean that national development strategy became unnecessary. It changed the institutional approach through which strategic advice, coordination and evaluation were pursued.

5. Objectives and guiding principles of NITI Aayog

NITI Aayog's broad objectives include evolving a shared vision of national development priorities, fostering cooperative federalism, promoting strategic and long-term policy frameworks, encouraging innovation and improving monitoring and evaluation.

Its institutional approach is associated with the following principles:

  • Recognising the role of States in national development.
  • Encouraging bottom-up policy formulation.
  • Promoting evidence-based policy advice.
  • Supporting innovation and entrepreneurship.
  • Encouraging inter-sectoral and inter-ministerial coordination.
  • Monitoring development outcomes and identifying implementation gaps.
  • Building partnerships with research institutions, civil society and other stakeholders.

These objectives position NITI Aayog as a policy think tank and coordinating institution rather than a central authority that directly administers all development programmes.

6. Organisational structure

The institutional structure of NITI Aayog includes a Governing Council, a Chairperson, a Vice-Chairperson, full-time members, part-time members, ex officio members, special invitees and a Chief Executive Officer, in accordance with the executive arrangements notified by the government.

6.1 Governing Council

The Governing Council provides a forum for participation by State governments and Union Territories in discussions on national development priorities. Its composition includes the Chief Ministers of States and the Lieutenant Governors or administrators of Union Territories, along with other specified participants.

The Council is intended to strengthen cooperative federalism by creating a platform for discussion, coordination and exchange of policy experience.

6.2 Chairperson

The Prime Minister is the Chairperson of NITI Aayog. This provides a high-level political platform for national policy coordination.

6.3 Vice-Chairperson and members

The Vice-Chairperson and members contribute to strategic policy work, research, inter-sectoral coordination and institutional leadership. Their work may involve economic policy, social development, technology, infrastructure, governance and other national priorities.

6.4 Chief Executive Officer

The CEO is responsible for executive administration and implementation of the institution's work programme within the approved structure.

7. Cooperative and competitive federalism

Cooperative federalism involves the Union and States working together to formulate and implement policies. It recognises that many development outcomes depend on shared responsibilities, local implementation and coordination across levels of government.

NITI Aayog provides a platform for dialogue and policy exchange. Its work can help identify common priorities, share successful practices and encourage States to participate in national development strategies.

Competitive federalism seeks to encourage States and Union Territories to improve performance through comparison, public information and incentives. Rankings and indices can draw attention to gaps and create pressure for administrative improvement.

Competition can support innovation, but it must be designed carefully. States have different starting conditions, fiscal capacities, geography and demographic challenges. Rankings that ignore these differences may reward pre-existing advantages rather than genuine administrative improvement.

Cooperation and competition are therefore complementary. Cooperation helps address common problems, while fair comparison can encourage improvement and innovation.

8. Policy research and strategic planning

NITI Aayog undertakes policy research and provides advice on long-term development issues. Its work can include economic transformation, infrastructure, energy, agriculture, health, education, technology, sustainability and governance.

Strategic planning involves identifying long-term trends, assessing policy choices and examining the consequences of alternative development pathways. It differs from a centralised allocation system in which a single institution determines detailed expenditure plans for all sectors and States.

Policy advice must be supported by reliable data, transparent assumptions and consultation with affected stakeholders. Research should also recognise implementation constraints, fiscal limits and the distributional effects of policy choices.

9. Monitoring and evaluation

Monitoring tracks whether activities and outputs are being delivered. Evaluation examines whether interventions are effective, efficient, equitable and sustainable and whether observed changes can reasonably be linked to a programme.

NITI Aayog has undertaken monitoring and evaluation-related work, including the development of indicators, review of selected programmes and promotion of evidence-based policy assessment.

Outcome monitoring is important because expenditure and administrative activity do not automatically translate into improvements in health, education, livelihoods or living conditions.

Good evaluation should distinguish between inputs, outputs, outcomes and long-term impacts. It should also consider unintended consequences, regional differences and the experience of beneficiaries.

Evaluation findings are useful only when institutions act on them. A strong system should connect evidence to budget decisions, programme redesign, administrative accountability and public reporting.

10. Aspirational Districts Programme

The Aspirational Districts Programme was launched in 2018 to accelerate development in districts identified as requiring focused improvement. It emphasises convergence of government schemes, collaboration among stakeholders and competition based on measurable progress.

The programme focuses on broad development themes including health and nutrition, education, agriculture and water resources, financial inclusion and skill development, and basic infrastructure.

Its institutional approach seeks to improve coordination between the Union, State governments and district administration. District-level leadership is important because many development outcomes depend on local implementation and the effective use of existing schemes.

Performance assessment can create incentives for improvement, but it must account for baseline conditions, data quality and differences in local capacity. A ranking should be treated as a management tool rather than a complete measure of human development.

11. Aspirational Blocks Programme

The Aspirational Blocks Programme extends the development approach to sub-district administrative units. It recognises that significant inequalities can exist within districts and that block-level data can help identify underserved populations and locations.

The programme seeks to strengthen local planning, convergence and outcome monitoring. Its value lies in improving the ability of district and block administrations to identify service gaps and coordinate interventions.

Local data systems must be reliable, timely and sufficiently disaggregated. Without these safeguards, performance comparisons may not reflect the real experience of residents.

12. Indices, rankings and development measurement

NITI Aayog has contributed to the development and publication of indices and reports intended to measure progress across States, districts and sectors. Such instruments can support evidence-based policy and encourage public discussion of development performance.

Examples of areas covered by national indices and reports include health, innovation, sustainable development, school education, export preparedness and multidimensional poverty.

Indices have several potential benefits:

  • They make differences in performance more visible.
  • They encourage data collection and administrative attention.
  • They help identify areas where policy intervention may be required.
  • They can facilitate the exchange of good practices.
  • They create a common framework for reviewing progress.

However, composite indices involve choices about indicators, weights, data sources and aggregation. These choices can affect rankings. A single score may conceal inequalities within a State or district and may not capture the quality of public services.

Indices should therefore complement, not replace, detailed evaluation and local evidence.

13. NITI Aayog and sustainable development

The Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) provide a global framework for economic, social and environmental development. India has adopted national and subnational approaches to monitor progress towards these goals.

NITI Aayog has an important coordinating and monitoring role in relation to India's SDG framework. Its work includes tracking progress, promoting State-level engagement and encouraging integration of sustainable development priorities into public policy.

SDG monitoring highlights the interdependence of poverty reduction, health, education, gender equality, clean energy, employment, sustainable cities, climate action and institutional effectiveness.

Progress requires coordination among ministries, States, local governments, businesses and civil society. Data gaps, financing constraints and uneven administrative capacity remain important challenges.

14. NITI Aayog and innovation

Innovation is relevant to productivity, public-service delivery, employment and the ability of government institutions to respond to new challenges. NITI Aayog has undertaken initiatives intended to encourage innovation, entrepreneurship and the adoption of new technologies.

Innovation policy must address more than the creation of start-ups. It also involves research capacity, access to finance, skills, intellectual property, public procurement, infrastructure and the ability of smaller enterprises to participate.

Public-sector innovation should be assessed by its effect on citizens, including accessibility, affordability, reliability and inclusion. Technology is not automatically beneficial if it excludes people who lack devices, connectivity, literacy or accessible interfaces.

15. Atal Innovation Mission

The Atal Innovation Mission (AIM) was established under NITI Aayog to promote innovation and entrepreneurship. Its activities have included support for innovation ecosystems, school-level tinkering, incubation and entrepreneurship-related initiatives.

The programme reflects the view that innovation capacity should be developed across educational institutions, research organisations and businesses rather than being concentrated in a small number of established centres.

Its effectiveness should be assessed through the quality and sustainability of innovations, participation across regions and social groups, access to mentoring and finance, and the ability of supported initiatives to produce lasting outcomes.

16. National development and intergovernmental coordination

Many development challenges cross administrative and sectoral boundaries. Water management, urbanisation, public health, transport, energy transition and climate adaptation require coordination among multiple ministries and levels of government.

NITI Aayog can contribute by convening stakeholders, developing shared policy frameworks and identifying implementation bottlenecks. Its influence depends on the willingness of ministries and States to use its analysis and coordinate their work.

Because it is not a statutory command authority, NITI Aayog's effectiveness depends substantially on the quality of its advice, the legitimacy of its processes and its ability to build consensus.

17. Distinguishing NITI Aayog from constitutional and statutory bodies

Institutional classification is important for understanding legal authority and accountability.

  • Constitutional bodies derive their existence and core powers directly from the Constitution, such as the Finance Commission and Election Commission of India.
  • Statutory bodies are created by legislation and exercise powers assigned by Parliament or a State Legislature, such as SEBI, TRAI and the Central Information Commission.
  • Executive bodies are created through executive decisions, resolutions or administrative orders. NITI Aayog falls in this category.
  • Advisory institutions provide recommendations, research and policy advice. Their recommendations do not automatically have the force of law.

An institution may perform important national functions without being a constitutional or statutory body. Its legal status must be identified from the instrument that created it and the powers conferred upon it.

18. Other institutions contributing to national policy

India's policy ecosystem includes a number of institutions that provide research, advice, technical expertise, coordination and evaluation. Their roles differ, and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

18.1 Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister

The Economic Advisory Council to the Prime Minister (EAC-PM) is an advisory body constituted by the government to provide economic advice on issues referred to it or considered relevant to the national economy.

Its work may include macroeconomic trends, employment, growth, fiscal issues, investment, productivity and structural reforms. Its advice is not itself legislation or a binding executive direction.

18.2 Prime Minister's Science, Technology and Innovation Advisory Council

The Prime Minister's Science, Technology and Innovation Advisory Council (PM-STIAC) provides a platform for advice on science, technology and innovation priorities. Its work can help connect scientific expertise with national development needs.

Science and technology policy requires coordination among research institutions, universities, government departments, industry and funding bodies. Advisory mechanisms can identify strategic priorities, but implementation depends on the responsible ministries and institutions.

18.3 Capacity-building institutions

National capacity-building institutions support the professional development of public officials and the modernisation of public administration. Mission Karmayogi and the Capacity Building Commission are part of the broader effort to shift civil-service training towards role-based competencies and continuous learning.

Capacity-building is essential because policy design and implementation depend on the skills, incentives and organisational systems of public institutions.

18.4 Research and evaluation institutions

Research organisations, universities, independent evaluation institutions and government research bodies contribute evidence to policy decisions. Their credibility depends on methodological rigour, access to data, professional independence and transparent communication of findings.

19. NITI Aayog and public finance

NITI Aayog's role should not be confused with the constitutional functions of the Finance Commission. The Finance Commission is constituted under Article 280 of the Constitution and recommends the distribution of specified tax revenues between the Union and States and other matters referred to it within the constitutional framework.

NITI Aayog is an executive policy institution. It does not replace the Finance Commission's constitutional role in recommending tax devolution and grants under Article 280.

It is also important to distinguish policy advice from budgetary authority. Ministries and legislatures retain their respective responsibilities in the formulation, approval and implementation of public expenditure.

20. Accountability and institutional limitations

NITI Aayog's advisory status creates both flexibility and limitations. It can convene experts and propose policy frameworks without the procedural constraints associated with a statutory regulator. However, it cannot compel States or ministries to adopt every recommendation merely by virtue of its institutional position.

Its accountability is primarily through the executive framework, public reporting, parliamentary scrutiny of government policy and the administrative processes through which its recommendations are considered.

Important limitations include:

  • Advisory authority: Recommendations may not translate into implementation without political and administrative ownership.
  • Data quality: Weak or inconsistent data can undermine policy evaluation and rankings.
  • Federal balance: State participation must be meaningful rather than merely consultative.
  • Institutional overlap: Responsibilities may overlap with ministries, regulators and other policy bodies.
  • Implementation gap: Policy reports may not address the administrative constraints faced by frontline institutions.
  • Measurement limitations: Composite indicators can oversimplify complex development outcomes.
  • Continuity: Long-term policy initiatives require institutional stability across political and administrative cycles.

21. Reform priorities

  • Strengthen meaningful participation by States and local governments in national policy design.
  • Improve transparency of research methods, data sources and evaluation findings.
  • Build independent and credible outcome-evaluation capacity.
  • Ensure that rankings account for baseline conditions and regional differences.
  • Improve coordination between NITI Aayog, ministries, regulators and constitutional bodies.
  • Connect policy recommendations to clear implementation responsibilities and timelines.
  • Strengthen local-government capacity and use of district and block-level evidence.
  • Promote inclusive innovation and access to technology across regions and social groups.
  • Encourage transparent consultation with civil society, research institutions and affected communities.
  • Protect the distinction between advisory institutions and authorities that exercise statutory or constitutional powers.

22. Mains-oriented analytical synthesis

NITI Aayog represents a shift in India's development-policy architecture from centralised planning towards strategic advice, cooperative federalism, innovation and outcome monitoring. Its significance lies in its ability to convene governments, generate evidence, identify policy priorities and encourage better implementation.

Its executive and advisory status is both a source of flexibility and a limitation. It can influence policy through research, consensus-building and institutional coordination, but it does not possess the constitutional or statutory authority of bodies such as the Finance Commission or sectoral regulators.

Its effectiveness should therefore be assessed not by the number of reports or rankings it produces but by whether its work improves public policy, strengthens federal partnership, addresses regional inequalities and produces measurable improvements in citizens' lives.

24. Key terms

Cooperative federalism: Partnership between the Union and States in policy formulation and implementation.

Competitive federalism: A framework in which States are encouraged to improve performance through comparison, innovation and measurable outcomes.

Outcome budgeting: An approach that links public expenditure with measurable outputs and outcomes.

Monitoring: Continuous tracking of programme activities, outputs and progress.

Evaluation: Systematic assessment of a policy or programme's relevance, effectiveness, efficiency, equity and sustainability.

Executive resolution: A government decision or administrative instrument used to establish or organise an executive institution.

Policy think tank: An institution that undertakes research and analysis to inform public policy.
