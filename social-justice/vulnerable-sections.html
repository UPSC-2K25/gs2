1. Meaning of Vulnerability

Vulnerability refers to an increased risk of harm, deprivation, exclusion or inability to cope with social, economic, environmental or personal shocks. It can arise from discrimination, poverty, age, disability, illness, displacement, insecure work, social isolation or unequal access to public institutions.

Vulnerability is not an inherent weakness of an individual or community. It is often produced or intensified by social structures, unequal power, inaccessible institutions and inadequate protection. A person may experience several overlapping disadvantages at the same time.

Public policy must therefore move beyond a narrow list of beneficiary categories and identify the barriers that prevent people from exercising rights and accessing services.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Articles 14 and 15: Equality and protection against specified forms of discrimination.
  • Article 16: Equality of opportunity in public employment.
  • Article 17: Abolition of untouchability.
  • Article 21: Life and personal liberty, including the constitutional protection of dignity.
  • Articles 23 and 24: Prohibition of trafficking, forced labour and specified child labour.
  • Article 39: Principles concerning livelihoods, equal pay, distribution of resources and protection of workers and children.
  • Article 39A: Equal justice and free legal aid.
  • Article 41: Public assistance in specified circumstances within the State's economic capacity and development.
  • Article 46: Promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 47: Improvement of nutrition, standards of living and public health.

3. Scheduled Castes

Scheduled Castes are communities specified under Article 341 of the Constitution. The President specifies the communities for each State or Union Territory, and Parliament may include or exclude communities from the notified list by law. The constitutional framework recognises the historical and continuing effects of caste-based exclusion and untouchability.

Key safeguards

  • Article 17 abolishes untouchability.
  • Article 15 permits specified special provisions, including measures for socially and educationally backward classes and Scheduled Castes.
  • Article 16 permits specified reservation provisions in public employment.
  • Articles 330 and 332 provide for reservation of seats in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Article 338 establishes the National Commission for Scheduled Castes.
  • Article 335 addresses the claims of Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in services and posts, consistent with administrative efficiency.

Major concerns

  • Untouchability practices, caste-based violence and social exclusion.
  • Discrimination in housing, education, employment and access to common resources.
  • Manual scavenging and hazardous sanitation work.
  • Landlessness, insecure livelihoods and unequal access to credit and assets.
  • Underreporting of offences and delays in investigation, prosecution and rehabilitation.

4. Scheduled Tribes

Scheduled Tribes are communities specified under Article 342. The President specifies the tribes or tribal communities, or parts or groups within them, for each State or Union Territory; Parliament may alter the list by law.

Constitutional safeguards

  • Articles 15 and 16 permit specified protective and affirmative measures.
  • Articles 330 and 332 provide for legislative representation.
  • Article 338A establishes the National Commission for Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 244 and the Fifth and Sixth Schedules provide special administrative arrangements for specified tribal areas.
  • Article 275(1) provides for grants-in-aid for specified purposes, including development of Scheduled Tribes and administration of Scheduled Areas.
  • Article 46 directs the State to promote the educational and economic interests of Scheduled Tribes and protect them from social injustice and exploitation.

Major concerns

  • Displacement due to mining, infrastructure, conservation and other development projects.
  • Insecure land and forest rights and inadequate recognition of customary practices.
  • Limited access to quality health, education, transport and digital connectivity in remote areas.
  • Loss of language, culture and traditional knowledge.
  • Exploitation by intermediaries, debt bondage and distress migration.
  • Weak implementation of local self-governance and forest-rights legislation.

5. Other Backward Classes and Economically Weaker Sections

Other Backward Classes

Articles 15(4), 15(5) and 16(4) provide for specified measures for socially and educationally backward classes. Article 340 enables appointment of a commission to investigate the conditions of backward classes. Article 338B establishes the National Commission for Backward Classes.

The identification of backward classes, the distinction between central and State lists, the creamy-layer principle where applicable and the legal conditions for reservation must be considered in their respective constitutional and statutory contexts.

Economically Weaker Sections

The 103rd Constitutional Amendment inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6), enabling special provisions, including reservation, for economically weaker sections other than classes covered by the specified existing reservation provisions. The validity of the amendment was upheld by the Supreme Court in Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022).

EWS provisions are distinct from the constitutional framework for socially and educationally backward classes. Eligibility and implementation depend on the applicable rules and judicially interpreted constitutional provisions.

6. Women and Girls

Women and girls may face intersecting barriers arising from gender discrimination, caste, class, disability, location, age and social norms. Vulnerability may appear in unequal access to education and health, unpaid care burdens, violence, unsafe workplaces, property ownership and political participation.

Constitutional provisions

  • Articles 14, 15 and 16: Equality and non-discrimination.
  • Article 15(3): Permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 39(a) and 39(d): Livelihood and equal pay principles.
  • Article 42: Humane work conditions and maternity relief.
  • Article 51A(e): Fundamental Duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  • Articles 243D and 243T: Reservation of seats for women in Panchayats and Municipalities.

The Constitution (106th Amendment) Act, 2023 provides for reservation of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi, subject to the constitutional conditions concerning delimitation and the relevant census process. Its implementation should not be confused with the existing local-body reservation framework.

7. Children

Children require protection because of their age, dependence and evolving capacity. Their rights include survival, development, protection and participation.

  • Article 21A: Free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen.
  • Article 24: Prohibits employment of children below fourteen in factories, mines and other hazardous employment as constitutionally specified.
  • Article 39(e) and 39(f): Protect children against abuse and promote healthy development and dignity.
  • Article 45: Early childhood care and education for children below six years.
  • Article 15(3): Permits special provisions for children.

Major concerns include malnutrition, child labour, trafficking, abuse, school exclusion, child marriage, online exploitation, neglect and inadequate family-based care.

8. Persons with Disabilities

Disability inclusion is based on equality, dignity, accessibility, reasonable accommodation and participation. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 gives effect to important obligations under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

  • Recognises specified disabilities and provides a statutory framework for rights and entitlements.
  • Provides for reservation in government employment and higher education within the statutory framework.
  • Requires accessibility measures and non-discrimination.
  • Provides for high support needs and specified protections.
  • Establishes central and State-level institutional mechanisms and designated authorities.

Challenges include inaccessible buildings and transport, communication barriers, inadequate assistive technology, limited inclusive education, employment discrimination and inconsistent implementation of reasonable accommodation.

9. Older Persons

Older persons may face income insecurity, health problems, isolation, age discrimination, abuse and dependence on family or institutional care.

The Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007 provides a statutory framework for maintenance and welfare. Social security pensions, health services, accessible public spaces, community care and protection against abuse are also important.

Policy should recognise the diversity of older persons: some remain economically active and independent, while others require long-term care, disability support or protection from neglect and exploitation.

10. Religious and Linguistic Minorities

Minority status and protections operate through constitutional provisions, legislation and judicial interpretation. Articles 25–28 protect religious freedom, while Articles 29 and 30 protect specified cultural and educational rights.

Article 350A concerns facilities for instruction in the mother tongue at the primary stage for children belonging to linguistic minority groups. Article 350B provides for a Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities.

Challenges may include discrimination, unequal access to public services, educational disadvantage, insecurity, stereotyping and barriers to preserving language and culture. Policy responses must protect equal citizenship while respecting constitutional freedoms and institutional neutrality.

11. Migrant Workers, Informal Workers and the Urban Poor

Workers in informal employment often face irregular earnings, weak social security, unsafe working conditions, limited bargaining power and difficulty accessing entitlements across locations.

Internal migrants may encounter documentation barriers, portability gaps, language differences, inadequate housing and weak access to health care, education and public distribution systems.

Policy priorities include portable social protection, worker registration, safe housing, labour inspection, accessible grievance systems, affordable urban services and coordination between source and destination States.

12. Intersectionality and Multiple Disadvantages

Intersectionality explains how multiple forms of disadvantage can interact. A woman with a disability from a Scheduled Tribe community in a remote area may experience barriers that cannot be understood by examining gender, disability, tribal status or geography separately.

Policy design should therefore:

  • Use disaggregated data while protecting privacy and dignity.
  • Identify barriers at the point of service delivery.
  • Consult affected communities and representative organisations.
  • Provide reasonable accommodation and accessible communication.
  • Coordinate schemes rather than forcing citizens to navigate fragmented institutions.
  • Evaluate whether benefits reach the most disadvantaged within each broad category.

13. Delivery Challenges

  • Beneficiary identification systems may exclude eligible people or include ineligible persons.
  • Insufficient awareness of rights and entitlements can reduce access.
  • Documentation, language and digital authentication requirements may create barriers.
  • Frontline institutions may lack trained personnel, funds and accessible infrastructure.
  • Weak coordination can produce overlapping schemes and service gaps.
  • Social stigma and fear of retaliation may prevent people from reporting abuse.
  • Inadequate data can conceal the needs of small, mobile or geographically isolated groups.
  • Monitoring may focus on expenditure and enrolment rather than quality and outcomes.

14. Way Forward

  1. Adopt a dignity-based, rights-oriented approach rather than treating people only as welfare beneficiaries.
  2. Strengthen enforcement of anti-discrimination and protective laws.
  3. Improve portability and continuity of social protection for migrants and mobile populations.
  4. Make public infrastructure, information and digital services accessible by design.
  5. Strengthen community-level institutions and representation of affected groups.
  6. Improve legal aid, rehabilitation, counselling and victim-support systems.
  7. Use disaggregated evidence to identify intersectional disadvantage.
  8. Ensure adequate staffing, training and accountability at the frontline.
  9. Make grievance redressal simple, time-bound and safe for complainants.
  10. Evaluate policies by improvements in dignity, agency, access and actual well-being.

15. Revision Summary

Vulnerability is shaped by social structures, unequal capabilities and institutional barriers. The Constitution provides protections through equality, dignity, affirmative action, representation, social welfare principles and special safeguards. Effective policy must address both group-specific disadvantages and their intersections, with accessible services, enforceable rights, participation and accountable institutions.
