1. Concept and Principles of Child Rights

Child rights recognise children as independent rights-holders entitled to survival, health, nutrition, education, protection, development, participation and dignity. Children require special safeguards because of their age, evolving capacities and dependence on families and institutions.

The central principles are:

  • Best interests of the child: the child's welfare must be a primary consideration in decisions affecting them.
  • Non-discrimination: every child must enjoy rights without discrimination based on sex, caste, religion, disability, origin or other status.
  • Survival and development: public policy must support physical, mental, emotional, social and educational development.
  • Participation: children should be heard in matters affecting them, in a manner appropriate to age and maturity.
  • Protection: children must be safeguarded from violence, exploitation, neglect, trafficking and harmful practices.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws.
  • Article 15(3): permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 21: protection of life and personal liberty, interpreted to protect dignity and conditions necessary for development.
  • Article 21A: free and compulsory education for children aged 6–14 years.
  • Article 23: prohibits trafficking, begar and forced labour.
  • Article 24: prohibits employment of children below 14 years in factories, mines and other hazardous employment.
  • Article 39(e): requires protection of the health and strength of workers and protection against abuse of tender age.
  • Article 39(f): directs the State to ensure opportunities and facilities for children to develop in conditions of freedom and dignity and to protect childhood and youth against exploitation and moral and material abandonment.
  • Article 45: early childhood care and education for all children until they complete six years of age.
  • Article 47: duty of the State to raise nutrition levels and improve public health.
  • Article 51A(k): duty of a parent or guardian to provide educational opportunities to a child or ward aged 6–14 years.

3. International Framework

UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1989

The Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC) is the principal international treaty on children's rights. India ratified it in 1992. It recognises civil, political, economic, social and cultural rights of children.

Its four general principles are non-discrimination, the best interests of the child, the right to life, survival and development, and respect for the views of the child.

Other international instruments

  • Optional Protocols to the UNCRC address the involvement of children in armed conflict and the sale of children, child prostitution and child pornography.
  • ILO Convention No. 138 addresses minimum age for admission to employment.
  • ILO Convention No. 182 addresses the worst forms of child labour.
  • Sustainable Development Goals include targets on child mortality, education, child labour, violence and birth registration.

International obligations inform domestic policy and interpretation. Their domestic legal effect must be distinguished from the enforceability of constitutional and statutory provisions.

4. Right to Education

The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009 gives effect to Article 21A. It provides for free and compulsory elementary education for children aged 6–14 years, subject to the statutory framework.

Key features

  • Duty of the appropriate government and local authorities to ensure admission, attendance and completion of elementary education.
  • Norms relating to neighbourhood schools, pupil-teacher ratios, infrastructure and teacher qualifications.
  • Prohibition of capitation fees and screening procedures for admission, as provided by the Act.
  • Protection against physical punishment and mental harassment.
  • Special provisions for children who have not been admitted or have not completed elementary education.
  • Specified obligations concerning admission of children from weaker sections and disadvantaged groups in covered private unaided schools.
  • School Management Committees with responsibilities relating to school functioning and development plans.

The Act's no-detention framework has been amended. The current statutory position permits examinations in specified classes and allows States to adopt a re-examination and holding-back process subject to prescribed safeguards. Avoid relying on older descriptions of a universal no-detention rule.

Implementation challenges

  • Learning poverty and weak foundational literacy and numeracy.
  • Teacher vacancies, uneven teacher deployment and inadequate school infrastructure.
  • Dropout and transition barriers, especially among vulnerable children.
  • Disability-related accessibility gaps and inadequate inclusive education support.
  • Digital divide and unequal access to devices, connectivity and learning resources.
  • Weak monitoring of school quality and learning outcomes.

5. Child Labour and Exploitation

Child labour undermines education, health, safety and development. It is linked to household poverty, debt, migration, informal employment, social exclusion and weak enforcement.

Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986, as amended in 2016

  • The Act generally prohibits employment or work of children below 14 years, subject to limited statutory exceptions.
  • Adolescents aged 14–18 years are prohibited from working in the hazardous occupations and processes specified by law.
  • The law provides for penalties and rehabilitation-related measures.
  • Family enterprise exceptions and their conditions require careful reading; they do not create a general permission to exploit children or displace the child's right to education.

Related constitutional and statutory safeguards

  • Articles 23 and 24 prohibit trafficking, forced labour and specified child employment.
  • The Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976 addresses bonded labour.
  • The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015 provides a framework for children in need of care and protection and children in conflict with law.
  • The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 contains criminal provisions relevant to trafficking, exploitation and offences against children; the exact applicable offence and procedure must be checked against the facts and current law.

6. Child Sexual Abuse and Protection

Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012

The POCSO Act provides a child-specific legal framework for protection from sexual assault, sexual harassment and pornography-related offences. A child is a person below 18 years under the Act.

  • Defines and criminalises specified forms of sexual offences against children.
  • Provides for reporting of offences and associated statutory responsibilities.
  • Requires child-friendly procedures for recording statements and conducting trials.
  • Provides for Special Courts and safeguards relating to identity, privacy and the child's dignity.
  • Includes provisions concerning assistance, support and compensation in accordance with the law.

Implementation must balance mandatory legal duties with sensitive, non-intimidating treatment of the child. Repeated questioning, disclosure of identity, delays, inadequate counselling and poor coordination can compound trauma.

Online child safety

Digital risks include grooming, sexual exploitation, circulation of abusive material, cyberbullying, extortion, identity misuse and exposure to harmful content. Responses require child-sensitive reporting mechanisms, digital literacy, platform accountability, lawful investigation and support for victims.

7. Juvenile Justice System

The Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, as amended, provides the principal statutory framework for children in conflict with law and children in need of care and protection.

Two distinct categories

  • Child in conflict with law: a child alleged or found to have committed an offence before completing 18 years of age.
  • Child in need of care and protection: a child meeting the categories defined in the Act, including children who are abandoned, orphaned, missing, abused, exploited, trafficked or otherwise vulnerable.

Institutional mechanisms

  • Juvenile Justice Board (JJB): deals with children in conflict with law, applying child-friendly procedures and the statutory principles of rehabilitation and reintegration.
  • Child Welfare Committee (CWC): exercises authority concerning children in need of care and protection.
  • District Child Protection Units: coordinate and support child protection services at district level.
  • Child Care Institutions: provide institutional care where required, subject to registration, standards and oversight under the Act.
  • Special Juvenile Police Units and Child Welfare Police Officers: support child-sensitive policing and statutory processes.

Children aged 16–18 and heinous offences

The Act provides a preliminary assessment mechanism by the Juvenile Justice Board in specified cases involving children aged 16–18 alleged to have committed heinous offences. This is not an automatic transfer to adult trial. The statutory criteria, assessment, Board decision and safeguards must be followed. The child remains entitled to the protections prescribed by the Act.

8. Child Marriage

The Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006 defines a child by reference to age: a male below 21 years and a female below 18 years. It provides for prevention, injunctions, penalties and specified remedies relating to child marriages.

  • Child marriages are generally voidable at the option of the contracting party who was a child at the time of marriage, subject to statutory conditions and time limits.
  • The Act identifies circumstances in which a child marriage is void, including specified coercive, trafficking-related or other circumstances.
  • Child Marriage Prohibition Officers have responsibilities for prevention, awareness and enforcement.
  • Maintenance, residence and custody-related relief may be available under the Act in the circumstances it specifies.

Child marriage is connected to gender inequality, school discontinuation, poverty, insecurity and social norms. Effective prevention requires education retention, community engagement, adolescent health services, birth and marriage registration and accessible protection mechanisms.

9. Nutrition, Health and Early Childhood Development

Child development begins before birth and depends on maternal health, nutrition, early stimulation, safe environments and access to quality healthcare. Malnutrition can affect physical growth, cognitive development, learning and long-term productivity.

  • Integrated Child Development Services and Anganwadi services: provide a platform for supplementary nutrition, growth monitoring, preschool education and health-related referrals within the applicable programme framework.
  • POSHAN Abhiyaan and related nutrition initiatives: seek to improve nutrition outcomes through convergence, behaviour change and monitoring.
  • Universal immunisation: protects children against vaccine-preventable diseases.
  • National Health Mission: supports maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health services.
  • School health and nutrition interventions: connect education with health screening, nutrition and well-being.

Key concerns include stunting, wasting, underweight, anaemia, low birth weight, inadequate dietary diversity, unsafe water, poor sanitation and gaps in primary healthcare.

10. Institutional Framework

  • National Commission for Protection of Child Rights (NCPCR): statutory body under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005; examines safeguards, reviews laws and policies, inquires into violations and promotes child rights within its mandate.
  • State Commissions for Protection of Child Rights: perform corresponding functions at State level where established.
  • National Commission for Women: may address issues affecting women and girls within its statutory mandate.
  • Ministry of Women and Child Development: leads central policy and programme coordination on child protection and development.
  • Child Welfare Committees: decide matters concerning children in need of care and protection under the Juvenile Justice Act.
  • Juvenile Justice Boards: deal with children in conflict with law.
  • Child Helpline and district protection structures: provide access to emergency assistance and referral services under the applicable operational framework.
  • Police, prosecution, health services and courts: have complementary roles in protection, investigation, treatment and justice.

11. Major Child Protection and Welfare Programmes

  • Mission Vatsalya: supports child protection services and institutional and non-institutional care, with emphasis on family-based care, sponsorship, foster care, adoption-related systems and child protection structures.
  • Mission Saksham Anganwadi and POSHAN 2.0: addresses nutrition and early childhood care through the Anganwadi platform and related interventions.
  • Samagra Shiksha: supports school education through an integrated framework, including access, equity and quality.
  • PM POSHAN: provides nutritional support to eligible schoolchildren in covered government and government-aided schools under the scheme framework.
  • National Health Mission: supports child health services, immunisation and maternal and child healthcare.
  • Childline/Child Helpline services: provide channels for assistance and referral; the current administrative arrangement should be checked against official programme information.

Scheme names, guidelines, coverage and administrative arrangements may change. For current operational details, rely on the latest official ministry guidelines rather than older summaries.

12. Important Judicial Principles

  • Unni Krishnan, J.P. v. State of Andhra Pradesh (1993): contributed to the judicial development of the right to education, preceding the insertion of Article 21A.
  • M.C. Mehta v. State of Tamil Nadu (1996): addressed child labour and rehabilitation, including directions concerning children employed in hazardous industries.
  • Bandhua Mukti Morcha v. Union of India (1984): connected bonded labour, exploitation and dignity with constitutional protections.
  • Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017): addressed the criminal law protection of minor wives and the rights of girls under 18.
  • Suchita Srivastava v. Chandigarh Administration (2009): recognised reproductive autonomy under Article 21, relevant to the dignity and bodily autonomy of women and girls.

Judicial decisions should be read with the current statutory framework. Earlier cases may have interpreted provisions that have since been amended or replaced.

13. Major Challenges

  • Child labour, trafficking, bonded labour and exploitation in informal sectors.
  • Sexual abuse, online exploitation, bullying and violence within homes and institutions.
  • Child marriage, gender discrimination and adolescent pregnancy.
  • Malnutrition, anaemia, preventable disease and inadequate early childhood services.
  • School exclusion, poor learning outcomes, dropout and digital inequality.
  • Weak birth registration and gaps in reliable, disaggregated child protection data.
  • Inadequate staffing, infrastructure and training in child protection institutions.
  • Institutionalisation where family-based care and community support could be more appropriate.
  • Delays in investigation, trials, rehabilitation and compensation.
  • Insufficient coordination among police, education, health, labour and child protection authorities.

14. Way Forward

  1. Strengthen prevention: address poverty, school exclusion, discrimination and harmful social practices that expose children to risk.
  2. Invest in early childhood: improve maternal health, nutrition, preschool education and responsive caregiving.
  3. Ensure quality education: focus on learning outcomes, safe schools, inclusion and retention through secondary education.
  4. Improve child protection systems: strengthen CWCs, JJBs, district units, police capacity and specialised support services.
  5. Prioritise family-based care: use family strengthening, sponsorship, kinship and foster care where appropriate, with institutional care as a regulated measure of last resort.
  6. Make justice child-friendly: prevent repeated interviews, protect identity, ensure legal assistance and reduce delays.
  7. Strengthen online safety: improve digital literacy, reporting, lawful platform cooperation and child-sensitive support.
  8. Improve convergence: connect health, nutrition, education, labour, police and social welfare departments through clear referral systems.
  9. Use evidence responsibly: improve birth registration, disaggregated data, independent evaluation and privacy safeguards.
  10. Enable child participation: hear children's views in age-appropriate ways in schools, institutions and decisions affecting them.

15. Key Distinctions for Revision

  • Child in need of care and protection vs child in conflict with law: these are distinct statutory categories handled by different institutions under the Juvenile Justice Act.
  • Child labour vs adolescent labour: the Child and Adolescent Labour Act creates different legal rules for children below 14 and adolescents aged 14–18.
  • POCSO vs Juvenile Justice Act: POCSO addresses sexual offences against children; the Juvenile Justice Act covers care, protection, rehabilitation and children in conflict with law.
  • Article 21A vs Article 45: Article 21A covers free and compulsory education for ages 6–14; Article 45 concerns early childhood care and education below six years.
  • Protection vs participation: safeguarding children must not erase their right to be heard in matters affecting them.
  • Institutional care vs family-based care: institutional placement is not automatically the best solution; decisions must follow the child's best interests and statutory safeguards.

16. Revision Framework

For any child rights issue, structure analysis around: constitutional guarantee; international commitment; applicable statute; responsible institution; prevention and response mechanism; implementation gap; best interests of the child; and reform.

Connect survival → health and nutrition → education → protection → participation → development and dignity.
