1. Meaning of Hunger, Nutrition and Food Security

Hunger refers to the physical condition associated with insufficient food intake. Undernourishment is a sustained condition in which habitual food consumption is insufficient to provide the dietary energy needed for a normal, active and healthy life.

Nutrition is broader than the quantity of food consumed. It concerns the intake and utilisation of energy, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals and other nutrients required for growth, health and functioning.

Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical, social and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food that meets dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life.

  • Availability: Adequate food supply through domestic production, imports and stocks.
  • Access: Physical and economic ability to obtain food.
  • Utilisation: Ability to absorb and use nutrients, influenced by diet quality, sanitation, safe water and healthcare.
  • Stability: Reliable availability, access and utilisation over time, including during shocks.

2. Forms of Malnutrition

  • Undernutrition: Inadequate intake or absorption of nutrients and energy.
  • Stunting: Low height for age, generally reflecting chronic undernutrition and repeated illness.
  • Wasting: Low weight for height, often associated with acute undernutrition.
  • Underweight: Low weight for age, which can reflect both chronic and acute undernutrition.
  • Micronutrient deficiencies: Inadequate intake of essential vitamins and minerals, including iron, iodine, vitamin A and others.
  • Overnutrition: Excessive energy intake and related conditions such as overweight and obesity.
  • Double burden of malnutrition: Coexistence of undernutrition and overnutrition within a population, household or individual across the life course.

Malnutrition can begin before birth and continue across the life cycle. Maternal nutrition, infant feeding, childhood illness, adolescent health and household food access are interconnected.

3. Constitutional and Legal Framework

  • Article 21: The right to life and dignity has been interpreted in ways relevant to access to food and conditions necessary for survival.
  • Article 39(a): Directs the State to secure adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally.
  • Article 39(e) and (f): Protect the health and strength of workers and support the healthy development of children.
  • Article 41: Provides for public assistance in specified circumstances, subject to the State's economic capacity and development.
  • Article 42: Provides for humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 47: Makes improvement of nutrition, living standards and public health a primary duty of the State.
  • Article 48: Directs the State to organise agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines and take steps for preserving and improving specified livestock.

Food security is also supported by statutory entitlements and public programmes, particularly the National Food Security Act, 2013.

4. National Food Security Act, 2013

The National Food Security Act, 2013 seeks to provide food and nutritional security by ensuring access to adequate quantity of quality food at affordable prices so that people may live with dignity.

Coverage and entitlements

  • The Act provides for coverage of up to 75 per cent of the rural population and up to 50 per cent of the urban population under the targeted public distribution system, subject to the statutory framework.
  • Eligible persons belonging to priority households are entitled to 5 kilograms of foodgrains per person per month under the Act.
  • Antyodaya Anna Yojana households are entitled to 35 kilograms of foodgrains per household per month.
  • The Act provides for foodgrains at the statutory issue prices specified in the Act, subject to amendments and applicable government decisions.
  • Eligible pregnant women and lactating mothers are entitled to meals and maternity benefit provisions, subject to the Act and applicable rules.
  • Children in the prescribed age groups are entitled to age-appropriate meals through covered child nutrition and school meal arrangements.

Governance and accountability

  • Central and State governments have defined responsibilities for procurement, allocation, distribution and implementation.
  • State governments identify eligible households within the coverage framework.
  • The Act provides for grievance redressal mechanisms, including District Grievance Redressal Officers and State Food Commissions.
  • It provides for social audits and transparency measures.
  • It recognises the eldest woman of an eligible household, aged eighteen years or above, as the head of household for ration card purposes, subject to the statutory framework.

Foodgrain distribution under the Act is one part of food security. It does not by itself ensure dietary diversity, adequate sanitation, healthcare or freedom from micronutrient deficiencies.

5. Public Distribution System

The Public Distribution System is a major instrument for providing subsidised foodgrains to eligible households through a network of fair price shops.

Targeted Public Distribution System

The Targeted Public Distribution System operates through identification of eligible households and distribution of foodgrains according to statutory entitlements and government rules.

Antyodaya Anna Yojana

Antyodaya Anna Yojana targets the poorest eligible households and provides a higher household-level foodgrain entitlement under the applicable framework.

One Nation One Ration Card

The One Nation One Ration Card system supports portability of eligible foodgrain entitlements across participating States and Union Territories, particularly benefiting migrant workers and mobile households.

Operational issues

  • Exclusion of eligible households due to outdated lists or documentation barriers.
  • Leakages, diversion and quality concerns in supply chains.
  • Uneven access to fair price shops in remote and difficult terrain.
  • Biometric authentication failures and connectivity problems.
  • Limited dietary diversity where food security is interpreted narrowly as grain access.
  • Storage, transport and procurement costs.

6. Procurement, Buffer Stocks and Foodgrain Management

Foodgrain procurement and buffer stocking support price stability, farmer incentives and the public distribution system. The Food Corporation of India plays a major role in procurement, storage, movement and distribution of foodgrains in coordination with State agencies.

  • Minimum Support Price: An administered price announcement intended to provide price assurance for specified crops; actual access to procurement varies across crops and regions.
  • Procurement: Purchase of foodgrains by public agencies under applicable arrangements.
  • Buffer stocks: Stocks maintained for public distribution, food security and market intervention purposes.
  • Decentralised procurement: Enables State agencies to procure, store and distribute foodgrains within the applicable system.
  • Storage and logistics: Warehousing, scientific storage, transportation and inventory management reduce losses and improve reliability.

Policy must balance farmer price support, consumer affordability, fiscal sustainability, nutritional diversity and environmental sustainability.

7. Nutrition Programmes Across the Life Cycle

Saksham Anganwadi and POSHAN 2.0

POSHAN 2.0 provides an integrated framework for nutrition support, including services for children, adolescent girls and pregnant and lactating women in the covered categories. Anganwadi services combine supplementary nutrition, growth monitoring, early childhood care and education, health-related referral and community mobilisation.

PM POSHAN

PM POSHAN provides cooked meals to eligible children in covered schools. It aims to improve nutrition, support attendance and strengthen children's participation in education.

Mission Saksham Anganwadi

The programme seeks to strengthen selected Anganwadi infrastructure and service delivery, including early childhood development and nutrition services.

Other important interventions

  • National Health Mission: Supports maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health services.
  • Pradhan Mantri Matru Vandana Yojana: Provides maternity benefit support to eligible women subject to the scheme's current eligibility conditions.
  • National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme: Addresses iodine deficiency through prevention and monitoring measures.
  • Food fortification initiatives: Seek to improve micronutrient intake through specified fortified food vehicles, subject to applicable standards and programme design.
  • Breastfeeding promotion: Encourages early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, followed by appropriate complementary feeding alongside continued breastfeeding.

Programme eligibility, benefits and implementation details may be revised. Consult current official guidelines when citing specific entitlements.

8. Maternal, Infant and Young Child Nutrition

The first 1,000 days—from conception to a child's second birthday—are a critical period for growth and development. Nutritional deprivation during this period can have lasting consequences for health, learning and productivity.

  • Improve maternal nutrition before and during pregnancy.
  • Ensure antenatal care, appropriate supplementation and safe delivery.
  • Promote early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months.
  • Introduce adequate and safe complementary foods from around six months while continuing breastfeeding.
  • Monitor child growth and respond promptly to wasting and severe acute malnutrition.
  • Improve immunisation, safe water, sanitation and treatment of childhood infections.
  • Strengthen counselling and household support for infant and young child feeding.

9. Food Safety and Nutrition Quality

Food security requires safe and nutritious food, not merely sufficient calories. Food contamination, adulteration and unsafe handling can undermine nutritional outcomes and public health.

  • Food Safety and Standards Authority of India: Performs regulatory functions under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006.
  • Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006: Provides the legal framework for food safety and regulation in India.
  • Food fortification: Can help address specific micronutrient deficiencies when appropriately designed and monitored.
  • Dietary diversity: Access to pulses, vegetables, fruits, dairy, eggs and other nutritious foods supports balanced diets.
  • Food labelling and consumer awareness: Help consumers make informed decisions about food quality and nutrition.

Fortification should complement, not replace, dietary diversity, access to nutritious foods and broader public health measures.

10. Agricultural Systems and Nutrition Security

Agriculture policy affects nutrition through food availability, household incomes, crop diversity, prices and environmental sustainability.

  • Encourage crop diversification, including pulses, millets, fruits and vegetables.
  • Strengthen local procurement and supply chains for nutritious foods.
  • Improve irrigation efficiency, soil health and climate-resilient agriculture.
  • Support small and marginal farmers, women farmers and agricultural labourers.
  • Reduce post-harvest losses through storage, processing and market infrastructure.
  • Promote kitchen gardens, community nutrition initiatives and local food systems.

Food policy should avoid equating national foodgrain availability with household-level nutrition security. Access, affordability, diet quality and utilisation are equally important.

11. Hunger Measurement and Nutrition Indicators

  • Prevalence of undernourishment: Estimates the proportion of a population whose habitual dietary energy consumption is insufficient.
  • Stunting: Low height for age among children and a marker of chronic undernutrition.
  • Wasting: Low weight for height and a marker of acute undernutrition.
  • Underweight: Low weight for age.
  • Low birth weight: Birth weight below 2,500 grams, an important indicator of maternal and newborn health.
  • Anaemia: A condition involving low haemoglobin, often associated with iron deficiency but also caused by other nutritional, infectious and genetic factors.
  • Dietary diversity: Measures the range of food groups consumed and provides insight into diet quality.

Different surveys and indices use different samples, reference periods, indicators and methods. Comparisons should be made only after checking definitions and data quality.

12. Major Challenges

  • Persistent child undernutrition, wasting, stunting and micronutrient deficiencies.
  • Unequal access to nutritious diets because of poverty and food price volatility.
  • Maternal undernutrition and anaemia affecting intergenerational health.
  • Double burden of malnutrition, with undernutrition and obesity coexisting.
  • Weak sanitation, unsafe water and disease burdens that reduce nutrient absorption.
  • Leakages, exclusion errors and delivery gaps in food distribution systems.
  • Regional, gender, caste and tribal disparities in nutrition outcomes.
  • Climate change, droughts, floods and supply-chain disruptions affecting food availability and prices.
  • Insufficient dietary diversity and over-reliance on a narrow range of foodgrains.
  • Coordination gaps between health, women and child development, agriculture, education and food distribution departments.

13. Reform Priorities

  1. Life-cycle nutrition: Integrate maternal, infant, child, adolescent and adult nutrition interventions.
  2. Dietary diversity: Improve access to pulses, millets, vegetables, fruits and locally appropriate nutritious foods.
  3. Strengthen Anganwadis: Improve infrastructure, workforce, growth monitoring, counselling and referral systems.
  4. Improve PDS delivery: Reduce exclusion, improve portability and strengthen transparency and grievance redressal.
  5. Convergent planning: Link nutrition with health, sanitation, safe water, education and women's empowerment.
  6. Maternal support: Improve antenatal care, maternity entitlements, breastfeeding support and maternal nutrition.
  7. Food systems: Support climate-resilient agriculture, local procurement, storage and reduced food waste.
  8. Targeted response: Identify and promptly treat severe acute malnutrition and other high-risk conditions.
  9. Evidence-based monitoring: Use reliable, disaggregated data and evaluate programme outcomes.
  10. Community participation: Strengthen nutrition awareness, local monitoring and women's participation in food decisions.

14. International Framework

  • SDG 2: Seeks to end hunger, achieve food security, improve nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture.
  • Food and Agriculture Organization: Works on food security, agriculture, nutrition and sustainable food systems.
  • World Food Programme: Provides food assistance and supports emergency food security interventions.
  • World Health Organization: Provides guidance on nutrition, maternal and child health and prevention of malnutrition.
  • UNICEF: Supports child nutrition, maternal health and early childhood development.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, Article 11: Recognises the right to an adequate standard of living, including adequate food, and the fundamental right to be free from hunger.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child, Article 24: Includes obligations relating to child health, nutrition and access to adequate nutritious foods.

15. Prelims Knowledge and Mains Analysis

Prelims focus

  • Four dimensions of food security.
  • Definitions of stunting, wasting, underweight and undernourishment.
  • National Food Security Act coverage and entitlements.
  • Distinction between TPDS, Antyodaya Anna Yojana and One Nation One Ration Card.
  • Roles of FCI, FSSAI and relevant nutrition institutions.
  • Objectives of POSHAN 2.0, PM POSHAN and related programmes.
  • International organisations and SDG 2.

Mains focus

Analyse food security as a combination of availability, access, utilisation and stability. Connect agricultural policy, public distribution, maternal and child nutrition, sanitation, healthcare, gender equality and climate resilience. Distinguish foodgrain security from nutrition security.
