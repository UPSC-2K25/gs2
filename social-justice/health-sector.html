1. Meaning and Scope of Public Health

Public health concerns the organised efforts of society to prevent disease, prolong life and promote health through collective action. It extends beyond hospitals and clinical treatment to include nutrition, sanitation, safe water, housing, education, occupational safety, environmental protection and social security.

Health is both an intrinsic human capability and an instrument of social and economic development. Ill-health can reinforce poverty through lost income, high out-of-pocket expenditure, reduced educational attainment and intergenerational disadvantage.

  • Preventive care: vaccination, screening, health education and risk reduction.
  • Promotive care: nutrition, physical activity, mental well-being and healthy environments.
  • Curative care: diagnosis, treatment, medicines, surgery and emergency services.
  • Rehabilitative and palliative care: recovery, disability support, pain relief and dignified end-of-life care.
  • Public health preparedness: surveillance, outbreak response, laboratories and emergency coordination.

2. Constitutional and Legal Framework

The Constitution does not expressly enumerate a separate fundamental right titled “right to health”. Its protection emerges from the interpretation of fundamental rights, Directive Principles and the distribution of legislative and executive responsibilities.

  • Article 21: The right to life has been interpreted to include conditions necessary for a dignified life, including access to health-related protections.
  • Article 14: Equality before law requires non-arbitrary public health decisions and fair access to public services.
  • Article 15(3): Enables special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 15(4) and 15(5): Enable special provisions for socially and educationally disadvantaged classes and specified communities in educational contexts.
  • Article 21A: Education supports health literacy and long-term social determinants of health.
  • Article 23: Protection against trafficking and forced labour has implications for occupational and reproductive health.
  • Article 24: Prohibits employment of children below fourteen years in factories, mines and hazardous employment.
  • Article 39(e) and 39(f): Direct the State to protect workers' health and strength and ensure children develop in conditions of freedom and dignity.
  • Article 41: Directs the State, within its economic capacity, to provide public assistance in cases including sickness and disablement.
  • Article 42: Provides for just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 47: Makes improvement of public health a primary duty of the State and directs attention to nutrition and living standards.
  • Article 48A: Environmental protection is closely connected with prevention of disease and health risks.
  • Article 51A(g): Places a fundamental duty on citizens to protect and improve the natural environment.

Federal distribution: Public health and sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries are principally State subjects under Entry 6 of the State List. Population control and family planning are in the Concurrent List (Entry 20A), as are several related areas such as prevention of food adulteration and drugs and poisons. Union action also operates through national programmes, centrally sponsored schemes, regulation, research, disease surveillance and inter-State coordination.

3. Right to Health: Judicial Understanding

  • State of Punjab v. Mohinder Singh Chawla (1997): The Supreme Court recognised that the right to health is integral to the right to life and that the State has a constitutional obligation to provide medical facilities.
  • Paschim Banga Khet Mazdoor Samity v. State of West Bengal (1996): Failure of government hospitals to provide timely emergency treatment was held to violate Article 21. The judgment emphasised the State's obligation to organise an adequate emergency medical system.
  • Consumer Education and Research Centre v. Union of India (1995): Connected workers' health and medical care with the right to life and dignity.
  • Parmanand Katara v. Union of India (1989): Emphasised the duty of doctors to provide immediate medical aid to preserve life, without avoidable procedural delay.

These decisions strengthen constitutional accountability, but effective realisation of health rights still depends on institutional capacity, financing, accessible facilities and enforceable service standards.

4. Health System Architecture

Primary, secondary and tertiary care

  • Primary care: First-contact, continuous and comprehensive services, including prevention, basic treatment, maternal and child health, screening and referral.
  • Secondary care: Specialist services generally provided through community health centres, sub-district hospitals and district hospitals.
  • Tertiary care: Advanced specialist treatment, medical education, complex surgery and research facilities.

A strong primary-care system reduces avoidable hospitalisation, improves early diagnosis and helps ensure that expensive tertiary facilities are not overwhelmed by conditions manageable at lower levels.

Public and private providers

India's health system includes public facilities, private hospitals and clinics, charitable institutions, non-governmental organisations, community health workers and traditional systems of medicine. Private providers play a significant role in service delivery, but uneven regulation, affordability and quality make effective public stewardship essential.

Human resources and community institutions

  • ASHA workers connect communities with health services, awareness, referrals and public programmes.
  • Auxiliary Nurse Midwives and other frontline workers support maternal, child and primary healthcare.
  • Anganwadi workers contribute to nutrition, early childhood care and convergence with health services.
  • Medical officers, nurses, specialists, pharmacists, laboratory personnel and public health managers sustain the wider system.

5. Major Policy Frameworks and Programmes

National Health Policy, 2017

The policy seeks to move towards universal access to quality healthcare without financial hardship. It emphasises preventive and promotive care, primary healthcare, better public investment, quality standards, access to medicines and stronger health-system governance.

National Health Mission

The National Health Mission, comprising the National Rural Health Mission and National Urban Health Mission, seeks to improve equitable access to affordable and quality healthcare. Its major components include strengthening health infrastructure, maternal and child health, disease control, community participation and health-system capacity.

Ayushman Bharat

  • Ayushman Arogya Mandirs: The expanded primary healthcare platform seeks to provide comprehensive primary healthcare closer to communities, including preventive, promotive, basic curative, rehabilitative and palliative services.
  • Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY): Provides eligible beneficiary families with health assurance for specified secondary and tertiary hospitalisation, subject to scheme rules and package conditions.

Primary healthcare and hospitalisation cover address different parts of the health system. Insurance or assurance for hospital treatment cannot substitute for accessible primary care, adequate public hospitals or affordable outpatient medicines.

Other important interventions

  • Universal Immunisation Programme: Provides vaccination against specified vaccine-preventable diseases.
  • Mission Indradhanush: Seeks to improve immunisation coverage among children and pregnant women who have been missed or partially covered.
  • National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme: Supports prevention, diagnosis, treatment, surveillance and community participation in TB control.
  • National AIDS Control Programme: Focuses on prevention, testing, treatment, care and reduction of stigma associated with HIV.
  • Pradhan Mantri Ayushman Bharat Health Infrastructure Mission: Seeks to strengthen public health infrastructure, surveillance, laboratories and preparedness for health emergencies.
  • Pradhan Mantri Bhartiya Janaushadhi Pariyojana: Promotes access to quality generic medicines at affordable prices through dedicated outlets.
  • National Mental Health Programme: Supports mental healthcare services, awareness and integration of mental health into the broader health system.
  • eSanjeevani: A national telemedicine service intended to improve access to consultations, including for people in underserved areas.

Programme eligibility, benefits, implementation guidelines and names may change. For operational decisions, consult the latest official scheme guidelines and ministry notifications.

6. Public Health Regulation and Institutions

  • Ministry of Health and Family Welfare: Leads national health policy, central programmes and coordination.
  • Directorate General of Health Services: Provides technical advice and supports public health functions of the Union government.
  • Indian Council of Medical Research: Supports biomedical and health research.
  • National Centre for Disease Control: Supports disease surveillance, outbreak investigation and public health preparedness.
  • Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation: Performs central regulatory functions concerning drugs, cosmetics and specified medical devices under applicable law.
  • National Medical Commission: Regulates medical education and professional standards under its statutory framework.
  • Food Safety and Standards Authority of India: Regulates food safety under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006.
  • State health departments: Plan and deliver a large share of health services and administer State-level institutions.

Effective regulation requires coordination among Union and State authorities, professional regulators, local governments and independent oversight mechanisms.

7. Social Determinants and Health Inequality

Health outcomes are shaped by income, caste, gender, disability, geography, occupation, education, housing, sanitation, nutrition and environmental conditions. Unequal exposure to risks and unequal access to care produce avoidable health disparities.

  • Gender: Maternal health, reproductive autonomy, anaemia, violence and unequal household access to nutrition and care.
  • Children: Immunisation, nutrition, neonatal care, safe water and early diagnosis.
  • Older persons: Non-communicable diseases, long-term care, rehabilitation and accessible geriatric services.
  • Persons with disabilities: Accessible facilities, assistive devices, rehabilitation and non-discriminatory treatment.
  • Tribal and remote communities: Geographic barriers, language, cultural context, workforce shortages and under-served facilities.
  • Urban poor and migrants: Mobility, insecure housing, documentation barriers and limited continuity of care.

Health equity requires proportionate attention to groups facing greater barriers, rather than merely providing identical services to all.

8. Financing and Financial Protection

Health financing involves raising revenue, pooling risks and purchasing services. A system dependent on direct household payments can push families into debt or poverty, particularly when serious illness requires prolonged treatment.

  • Increase predictable public financing and improve the efficiency of expenditure.
  • Strengthen risk pooling and financial protection against catastrophic health expenditure.
  • Ensure affordable access to essential medicines, diagnostics and outpatient care.
  • Improve purchasing arrangements, provider payment systems and audit of publicly financed services.
  • Strengthen public facilities so that insurance schemes do not merely transfer public funds to poorly regulated providers.

Insurance coverage is only one dimension of universal health coverage. Availability, accessibility, acceptability, quality and financial protection must be considered together.

9. Major Challenges

  • Unequal distribution of doctors, nurses, specialists and facilities across regions.
  • Shortages of public health personnel, diagnostic capacity and essential medicines.
  • High household expenditure, especially on outpatient treatment and medicines.
  • Fragmented service delivery and weak referral links between levels of care.
  • Growing burden of non-communicable diseases alongside persistent communicable diseases.
  • Malnutrition, anaemia, maternal and neonatal health risks and preventable childhood illness.
  • Weak disease surveillance, data interoperability and emergency preparedness in some areas.
  • Quality variation, inadequate regulation and information asymmetry in healthcare markets.
  • Mental health stigma and limited access to affordable, community-based services.
  • Environmental health risks, antimicrobial resistance and climate-sensitive disease patterns.

10. Reform Priorities

  1. Primary healthcare first: Build capable, adequately staffed primary facilities with reliable medicines, diagnostics and referral systems.
  2. Public health capacity: Invest in epidemiology, laboratories, surveillance, health data and emergency response.
  3. Human resources: Improve recruitment, rural retention, training, working conditions and multidisciplinary public health education.
  4. Financial protection: Reduce out-of-pocket expenditure through public provision, essential medicine access and effective risk pooling.
  5. Quality and accountability: Enforce transparent standards, patient rights, clinical protocols and grievance redressal.
  6. Cooperative federalism: Strengthen Union-State coordination while allowing States to adapt delivery to local needs.
  7. Convergence: Link health with nutrition, sanitation, drinking water, education, housing and environmental policy.
  8. Digital inclusion: Use digital health systems with informed consent, privacy safeguards, cybersecurity and alternatives for people with limited connectivity.
  9. Community participation: Strengthen local health committees, social accountability and patient feedback.
  10. One Health approach: Coordinate human, animal and environmental health systems to address zoonotic disease and antimicrobial resistance.

11. International Framework

  • WHO: Provides global public health leadership, technical guidance and coordination.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights: Article 12 recognises the right to the highest attainable standard of physical and mental health.
  • Convention on the Rights of the Child: Article 24 concerns children's right to health and healthcare.
  • Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities: Article 25 addresses health without discrimination on the basis of disability.
  • Sustainable Development Goal 3: Seeks to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages, including universal health coverage.
  • Alma-Ata and Astana declarations: Emphasise primary healthcare as central to health for all.

12. Prelims Knowledge and Mains Analysis

Prelims focus

  • Constitutional provisions relating to health, nutrition, labour and environmental protection.
  • State List and Concurrent List responsibilities relevant to health.
  • Distinction between primary, secondary and tertiary care.
  • Objectives and institutional roles under major health programmes.
  • Functions of ICMR, NCDC, CDSCO, FSSAI and the National Medical Commission.
  • International health instruments and SDG 3.

Mains focus

Analyse health as a constitutional, developmental and governance concern. A balanced answer should connect public financing, primary healthcare, federal coordination, social determinants, human resources, regulation and measurable health outcomes. Avoid treating insurance expansion alone as equivalent to universal health coverage.

13. Revision Framework

  • Constitution: Articles 21, 39, 41, 42, 47 and 48A; State List Entry 6; Concurrent List subjects.
  • Judiciary: Paschim Banga, Parmanand Katara, Mohinder Singh Chawla and Consumer Education and Research Centre.
  • System: Primary, secondary and tertiary care; public-private mix; frontline workforce.
  • Programmes: NHM, Ayushman Bharat, PM-JAY, immunisation, disease control and health infrastructure.
  • Analysis: Equity, financial protection, quality, federalism, social determinants and preparedness.
