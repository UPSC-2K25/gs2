1. Meaning and Dimensions of Poverty

Poverty is a condition in which individuals or households lack the resources and capabilities required to attain an acceptable standard of living. It involves inadequate income and consumption, but also deprivation in health, education, nutrition, housing, sanitation, security and social participation.

Poverty is therefore both a material condition and a denial of substantive freedoms. Its causes include unequal access to assets, insecure employment, low productivity, discrimination, ill-health, indebtedness, social exclusion and vulnerability to economic or environmental shocks.

  • Income poverty: Inadequate income to meet essential needs.
  • Consumption poverty: Household consumption expenditure below a defined poverty threshold.
  • Multidimensional poverty: Overlapping deprivation in indicators such as health, education and living standards.
  • Chronic poverty: Persistent deprivation over an extended period, often transmitted across generations.
  • Transient poverty: Temporary deprivation caused by shocks such as illness, job loss, crop failure or disasters.
  • Relative poverty: Deprivation assessed in relation to prevailing living standards and social participation in a society.
  • Vulnerability to poverty: Risk of falling into poverty because of insecure livelihoods, weak savings or exposure to shocks.

2. Poverty, Inequality and Social Exclusion

Poverty and inequality are related but distinct. Poverty concerns deprivation below a threshold or across specified dimensions, while inequality concerns how resources, opportunities and outcomes are distributed across individuals or groups.

Economic growth can reduce poverty without necessarily reducing inequality. Conversely, redistribution can improve equality while poverty persists if overall access to income and essential services remains inadequate.

  • Social exclusion: Denial of access to institutions, opportunities and public services because of caste, tribe, gender, disability, religion, location or other forms of disadvantage.
  • Intergenerational poverty: Poor health, nutrition and educational outcomes can reduce opportunities for the next generation.
  • Spatial poverty: Deprivation may be concentrated in rural regions, tribal areas, drought-prone districts, urban informal settlements or remote communities.
  • Gendered poverty: Unequal asset ownership, unpaid care work, labour-market barriers and limited control over household resources can intensify women's deprivation.

3. Constitutional Framework for Poverty Reduction

  • Article 14: Equality before law and equal protection of laws.
  • Article 15: Prohibits specified forms of discrimination and permits special provisions for disadvantaged groups, women and children.
  • Article 16: Guarantees equality of opportunity in public employment, subject to constitutional provisions for reservation.
  • Article 21: Protects life and personal liberty and has been interpreted to encompass dignity and conditions necessary for meaningful life.
  • Article 21A: Provides for free and compulsory education for children aged six to fourteen years.
  • Article 23: Prohibits trafficking, begar and other similar forms of forced labour.
  • Article 38: Directs the State to promote welfare and minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities.
  • Article 39(a): Directs the State to secure adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally.
  • Article 39(b) and (c): Address distribution of material resources to serve the common good and prevention of concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment.
  • Article 41: Directs the State, within its economic capacity and development, to provide for work, education and public assistance in specified cases.
  • Article 43: Supports a living wage and conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life.
  • Article 46: Promotes the educational and economic interests of weaker sections, particularly Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes.
  • Article 47: Makes improvement of nutrition, living standards and public health a primary duty of the State.

These provisions establish a constitutional basis for poverty reduction through social protection, equal opportunity, livelihood support, public services and redistribution-oriented policy.

4. Measuring Poverty in India

Consumption-based poverty estimates

Traditional poverty estimation in India has relied substantially on household consumption expenditure surveys and poverty lines. A poverty line is a threshold used to classify individuals or households according to a defined minimum level of consumption or resources.

Different expert groups have proposed methodologies for estimating poverty. Their approaches have differed in the consumption basket, price adjustments, reference periods, treatment of expenditure and rural-urban differences.

  • Alagh Task Force (1979): Developed an early calorie-linked poverty estimation approach based on consumption expenditure.
  • Lakdawala Expert Group (1993): Continued the consumption-based approach, using State-specific poverty lines and price indices.
  • Tendulkar Expert Group (2009): Recommended changes in the poverty estimation methodology, including a common consumption basket approach and revised treatment of expenditure patterns.
  • Rangarajan Expert Group (2014): Proposed a different poverty line methodology using separate normative and behaviourally determined components.

These are historical expert methodologies, not interchangeable measures. A poverty estimate must always be read with its reference year, survey data and methodology. There is no single universally accepted poverty line that captures every form of deprivation.

Multidimensional Poverty Index

The global Multidimensional Poverty Index developed by UNDP and the Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative assesses overlapping deprivation in three dimensions:

  • Health: Indicators include nutrition and child mortality.
  • Education: Indicators include years of schooling and school attendance.
  • Standard of living: Indicators include cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing and assets.

The global MPI uses a deprivation threshold across weighted indicators to identify multidimensionally poor people. National MPI adaptations may use a locally tailored indicator framework, so national and global estimates should not be assumed to be identical.

The NITI Aayog's National Multidimensional Poverty Index uses a national framework to track deprivation and inform policy. Changes in measured multidimensional poverty should be interpreted in relation to the selected indicators, data periods and methodology.

5. Poverty, Growth and Employment

Economic growth can reduce poverty by expanding employment, raising incomes, increasing productivity and generating public revenue. However, the poverty-reducing impact of growth depends on its sectoral composition, employment intensity, distribution of opportunities and access to essential services.

  • Employment-intensive growth: Expands productive jobs, particularly for low-income households.
  • Agricultural productivity: Improves rural incomes through better irrigation, technology, markets, storage and diversification.
  • Manufacturing and services: Can absorb labour and create higher-productivity employment when skills and infrastructure are adequate.
  • Micro, small and medium enterprises: Support entrepreneurship, local employment and economic diversification.
  • Infrastructure: Reduces transaction costs and improves access to markets, education and health services.
  • Redistribution and public services: Ensure that growth translates into improved capabilities and living standards.

Growth without adequate employment, fair wages and social protection may leave substantial sections of the population vulnerable.

6. Rural Poverty and Livelihood Security

Rural poverty is linked to landlessness, small and fragmented holdings, low agricultural productivity, seasonal employment, indebtedness, inadequate irrigation, weak market access and vulnerability to climatic shocks.

Major interventions

  • Mahatma Gandhi National Rural Employment Guarantee Act, 2005: Establishes a legal framework for a demand-driven guarantee of up to 100 days of wage employment in a financial year to eligible rural households whose adult members volunteer for unskilled manual work, subject to the Act.
  • Deendayal Antyodaya Yojana – National Rural Livelihoods Mission: Promotes self-help groups, financial inclusion, livelihood diversification and community institutions among rural poor households.
  • Pradhan Mantri Awaas Yojana–Gramin: Supports eligible rural households in obtaining pucca housing, subject to scheme criteria.
  • Pradhan Mantri Gram Sadak Yojana: Improves rural road connectivity and access to services and markets.
  • PM-KISAN: Provides income support to eligible landholding farmer families subject to scheme rules and exclusions.
  • National Social Assistance Programme: Provides social assistance through specified pension and family benefit components for eligible beneficiaries.

Employment guarantees, income support, rural infrastructure and self-help group institutions address different aspects of rural deprivation and should be viewed as complementary interventions.

7. Urban Poverty and Informal Livelihoods

Urban poverty is shaped by insecure employment, high housing costs, informal settlements, inadequate sanitation, lack of social security and barriers to accessing public services. Migrants and informal workers may face additional difficulties because entitlements and service delivery are often tied to location or documentation.

  • DAY-NULM: Seeks to reduce urban poverty through livelihood opportunities, self-employment, skill development and support for urban street vendors and vulnerable communities under its framework.
  • PM SVANidhi: Provides working-capital support to eligible street vendors through a scheme-based credit framework.
  • Affordable housing initiatives: Seek to improve access to adequate housing through different urban and rural programmes.
  • One Nation One Ration Card: Supports portability of eligible foodgrain entitlements under the public distribution system for beneficiaries moving across locations.

Urban poverty policy must integrate housing, water, sanitation, public transport, healthcare, education, skills, social security and secure livelihoods.

8. Social Protection and Poverty Reduction

Social protection reduces vulnerability and helps households manage risks across the life cycle. It can be contributory, non-contributory, universal or targeted, depending on programme design.

  • Food security: Public distribution and nutrition programmes reduce food deprivation.
  • Income support: Transfers and pensions support eligible households and vulnerable individuals.
  • Employment support: Wage employment and livelihood programmes provide income opportunities.
  • Health protection: Public health services and health assurance reduce the financial consequences of illness.
  • Housing and basic services: Housing, sanitation, drinking water and electricity programmes improve living standards.
  • Insurance and pensions: Social security arrangements help manage risks associated with death, disability, accidents and old age.

Targeting can direct resources to those most in need, but inaccurate exclusion, documentation barriers and outdated beneficiary lists can deny support to eligible households. Universal access to essential services can reduce these risks.

9. Poverty and Vulnerable Groups

  • Scheduled Castes: Social discrimination, occupational segregation, landlessness and unequal access to quality services can deepen deprivation.
  • Scheduled Tribes: Geographic isolation, displacement, forest rights issues and limited access to public services can contribute to multidimensional poverty.
  • Women: Unequal asset ownership, unpaid care work, wage gaps and restricted labour-market opportunities increase economic vulnerability.
  • Children: Poverty affects nutrition, education, health, safety and future opportunities.
  • Persons with disabilities: Additional costs, inaccessible infrastructure and discrimination can increase poverty risks.
  • Older persons: Income insecurity, health expenditure and dependence on family support can produce deprivation.
  • Minorities and migrants: Unequal access, discrimination, mobility and documentation barriers may limit opportunities and services.

Disaggregated data and context-specific policy are necessary because poverty does not affect all groups in the same way.

10. Institutions and Governance

  • NITI Aayog: Supports policy coordination, development strategy and monitoring, including national multidimensional poverty work.
  • Ministry of Rural Development: Administers major rural livelihood, employment, housing and social assistance programmes.
  • Ministry of Housing and Urban Affairs: Leads important urban development and poverty-related programmes.
  • Ministry of Consumer Affairs, Food and Public Distribution: Oversees foodgrain distribution and related food security functions.
  • State governments: Implement many programmes, identify beneficiaries, deliver services and adapt interventions to local needs.
  • Local governments: Support local planning, beneficiary identification, service delivery and community monitoring within their powers.
  • Self-help groups and community organisations: Strengthen collective action, financial inclusion, livelihoods and local accountability.

11. Major Challenges

  • Different poverty measures can produce different estimates and policy interpretations.
  • Income and consumption data may not capture the full extent of deprivation or vulnerability.
  • Informal employment, low wages and underemployment limit income security.
  • Regional disparities persist in infrastructure, public services and livelihood opportunities.
  • Climate change, disasters, crop losses and health shocks can push vulnerable households into poverty.
  • Exclusion and inclusion errors in beneficiary identification reduce programme effectiveness.
  • Fragmented schemes and weak convergence can create gaps and duplication.
  • Urban migrants may face barriers to portability and continuity of entitlements.
  • Gender inequality and unpaid care work constrain economic participation.
  • Weak local capacity, delayed payments and poor grievance redressal reduce delivery quality.

12. Reform Priorities

  1. Productive employment: Promote labour-intensive manufacturing, services, rural non-farm activity and viable enterprises.
  2. Human capabilities: Improve nutrition, healthcare, education and foundational skills to break intergenerational poverty.
  3. Reliable measurement: Use timely household data and transparent methodologies, combining income, consumption and multidimensional indicators.
  4. Social protection floors: Ensure accessible minimum protection against food insecurity, illness, disability, old age and livelihood shocks.
  5. Portability: Make entitlements accessible to migrants and mobile workers across State and district boundaries.
  6. Women's economic empowerment: Expand access to assets, credit, childcare, safe transport and decent employment.
  7. Climate resilience: Strengthen livelihood diversification, water security, disaster preparedness and adaptive social protection.
  8. Community institutions: Empower self-help groups, local governments and community organisations in planning and monitoring.
  9. Effective delivery: Improve payment timeliness, transparency, grievance redressal and independent social audits.
  10. Convergence: Coordinate employment, housing, food, health, education, sanitation and financial inclusion interventions.

13. International Framework

  • SDG 1: End poverty in all its forms everywhere.
  • SDG 2: End hunger, achieve food security and improved nutrition and promote sustainable agriculture.
  • SDG 8: Promote sustained, inclusive and sustainable economic growth, full and productive employment and decent work for all.
  • SDG 10: Reduce inequality within and among countries.
  • International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights: Recognises rights relating to work, social security, adequate living standards, food, housing, health and education.
  • ILO social protection framework: Promotes social security and nationally defined social protection floors.
  • World Bank poverty measurement: Provides international poverty estimates and analytical tools, using its own defined methodologies and thresholds.

14. Prelims Knowledge and Mains Analysis

Prelims focus

  • Distinction between income, consumption and multidimensional poverty.
  • Historical poverty estimation expert groups and their broad approaches.
  • Dimensions and indicators of the MPI.
  • Constitutional provisions relating to livelihood, equality and welfare.
  • Objectives and statutory basis of MGNREGA.
  • Rural and urban livelihood and social protection programmes.
  • Roles of NITI Aayog and relevant Union ministries.

Mains focus

Analyse poverty as a multidimensional and structural problem. Connect growth, employment, human capabilities, inequality, social protection and governance. Distinguish poverty reduction from vulnerability reduction, and assess whether programmes create durable livelihood opportunities or only provide short-term relief.

  • Concepts: Absolute, relative, chronic, transient and multidimensional poverty.
  • Measurement: Poverty lines, expert groups and MPI.

  • Urban interventions: DAY-NULM, PM SVANidhi and portability of entitlements.
