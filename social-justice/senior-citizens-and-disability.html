1. Understanding Ageing and Disability

Ageing and disability are distinct but sometimes overlapping dimensions of social vulnerability. Older persons may face declining health, reduced income, social isolation, inadequate care and age discrimination. Persons with disabilities may encounter physical, communication, institutional, attitudinal and digital barriers that restrict equal participation.

Neither group should be viewed only through a welfare lens. A rights-based approach recognises autonomy, dignity, accessibility, participation, independent living and equal citizenship.

Important concepts

  • Active ageing: enabling people to participate in social, economic, cultural and civic life as they grow older.
  • Age-friendly environment: accessible housing, transport, public spaces, health services and community support.
  • Disability inclusion: removal of barriers and provision of reasonable accommodation so persons with disabilities can participate on an equal basis.
  • Universal design: designing products, environments and services to be usable by all people to the greatest extent possible.
  • Independent living: enabling persons with disabilities to exercise choice and control over their lives and support arrangements.
  • Care economy: paid and unpaid activities that support people who require assistance, including older persons and persons with disabilities.

2. Constitutional Foundations

  • Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws.
  • Article 15: prohibition of discrimination on specified grounds; Article 15(3) permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment.
  • Article 21: protection of life and personal liberty, interpreted to include dignity and conditions necessary for a meaningful life.
  • Article 23: prohibition of trafficking, begar and forced labour.
  • Article 38: promotion of a social order informed by justice and reduction of inequalities.
  • Article 39(e): protection of the health and strength of workers and against abuse of tender age.
  • Article 41: within the State's economic capacity and development, provision for the right to work, education and public assistance in cases including old age, sickness and disablement.
  • Article 42: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 46: promotion of educational and economic interests of weaker sections.
  • Article 47: improvement of nutrition, standard of living and public health.

Article 41 is particularly relevant to social assistance for old age and disablement, but it is a Directive Principle and is expressly qualified by the State's economic capacity and development.

3. Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016

The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (RPwD Act) gives effect to India's obligations under the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. It replaced the earlier 1995 legislation and expanded the recognised categories of specified disabilities.

Core principles and protections

  • Equality and non-discrimination.
  • Respect for individual dignity, autonomy and freedom to make choices.
  • Accessibility in the built environment, transport, information and communication technologies and public services.
  • Protection against abuse, violence and exploitation.
  • Inclusive education and appropriate support in educational institutions.
  • Equal opportunity in employment and non-discrimination by government establishments.
  • Social security, healthcare, rehabilitation and community participation.
  • Access to justice and procedural accommodation.

Specified disabilities

The Act recognises 21 specified disabilities, including blindness, low vision, hearing impairment, locomotor disability, intellectual disability, mental illness, autism spectrum disorder, cerebral palsy, muscular dystrophy, chronic neurological conditions, specific learning disabilities, multiple sclerosis, speech and language disability, thalassemia, haemophilia, sickle cell disease, multiple disabilities and other categories specified in the Schedule.

The statutory definition and certification rules should be consulted for the exact scope of each category. A person's rights under the Act should not be reduced to whether they qualify for a particular benefit threshold.

Benchmark disability and reservations

  • A person with benchmark disability is generally defined by reference to not less than 40% of a specified disability, as certified by the competent authority, subject to the Act.
  • Section 32 provides for reservation of not less than 5% of seats in government and government-aided higher educational institutions for persons with benchmark disabilities, subject to statutory provisions.
  • Section 34 provides for not less than 4% reservation in government establishments for persons with benchmark disabilities across specified categories, subject to the statutory framework.

Institutional mechanisms

  • Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities at the Union level.
  • State Commissioners for Persons with Disabilities.
  • Central and State Advisory Boards on Disability.
  • Special courts and designated authorities under the statutory framework.

4. Rehabilitation, Accessibility and Inclusive Education

Disability policy must move from a charity-based model to a social and human-rights model. The social model highlights that disability-related exclusion is produced not only by an individual's impairment but also by inaccessible environments, discriminatory attitudes and institutional barriers.

  • Accessibility: accessible buildings, footpaths, transport, toilets, websites, public information and emergency services.
  • Reasonable accommodation: necessary and appropriate modifications that do not impose a disproportionate or undue burden, enabling equal participation in particular circumstances.
  • Inclusive education: accessible classrooms, trained teachers, assistive devices, learning support and reasonable accommodation.
  • Assistive technology: mobility aids, hearing and communication devices, accessible software and other tools that enable autonomy.
  • Community-based rehabilitation: support delivered through local health, education and social protection systems, close to the person's home.
  • Independent living: personal assistance, accessible housing, community support and choice over care arrangements.

The Accessible India Campaign (Sugamya Bharat Abhiyan) seeks to improve accessibility in the built environment, transport and information and communication ecosystems. Implementation requires measurable standards, audits, maintenance and accountability rather than one-time construction.

5. Mental Health and Psychosocial Disability

Mental health is a component of overall health and well-being. Mental illness and psychosocial disability can be associated with stigma, discrimination, exclusion from employment and barriers to healthcare. A rights-based approach requires informed consent, dignity, privacy, community support and protection against degrading treatment.

The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 recognises the right to access mental healthcare and treatment from services funded or provided by the appropriate government, subject to the Act. It includes provisions concerning advance directives, nominated representatives, informed consent, confidentiality and the Mental Health Review Boards.

The Act also decriminalised attempted suicide for the purposes specified in its Section 115, creating a presumption of severe stress and requiring care, treatment and rehabilitation rather than punishment, subject to the statutory text.

Mental health services should be integrated with primary healthcare, schools, workplaces, community organisations and social protection systems. Institutionalisation should not substitute for accessible community-based care.

6. Rights and Welfare of Senior Citizens

Population ageing raises questions of income security, healthcare, long-term care, housing, social participation and intergenerational solidarity. Older persons are not a homogeneous group: gender, disability, caste, income, location, family structure and access to pensions shape their experiences.

Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act, 2007

  • Provides a legal framework for maintenance of parents and senior citizens by children or specified relatives in the circumstances covered by the Act.
  • Provides for Maintenance Tribunals and Appellate Tribunals.
  • Addresses abandonment and neglect through statutory provisions.
  • Contains provisions concerning protection of life and property of senior citizens.
  • Provides for measures relating to old-age homes and medical support within the statutory framework.

The Act's definition of senior citizen generally refers to a person who has attained 60 years of age. State rules and implementation arrangements are relevant to the operation of the Act.

Other relevant legal protections

  • Constitutional protections under Articles 14 and 21 apply to older persons as individuals.
  • The Code of Criminal Procedure historically contained a maintenance provision for parents; the corresponding current procedural provision should be checked under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023.
  • Property, succession, pension, healthcare and consumer laws may be relevant depending on the specific issue.

7. Social Security and Support for Older Persons

  • National Social Assistance Programme (NSAP): includes the Indira Gandhi National Old Age Pension Scheme for eligible older persons from poor households, subject to programme criteria and State supplementation.
  • Atal Pension Yojana: a contributory pension scheme designed primarily for workers in the unorganised sector who meet its eligibility requirements.
  • Pradhan Mantri Vaya Vandana Yojana: a pension-oriented scheme for senior citizens that was time-bound for new subscriptions; its historical features should not be mistaken for current enrolment availability.
  • Rashtriya Vayoshri Yojana: provides eligible senior citizens with age-related assistive living devices under its scheme guidelines.
  • Ayushman Bharat and public health programmes: provide health-service and financial-protection mechanisms, with eligibility and coverage determined by current guidelines.
  • State pension and welfare programmes: vary in eligibility, amount and coverage across States.

Distinguish tax-funded social assistance from contributory pension arrangements and insurance. They have different eligibility conditions, financing structures and risk-sharing models.

8. Institutional and Community Support

  • Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment: central policy and programme responsibilities relating to senior citizens and disability affairs.
  • Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities: leads disability-related policy and programme coordination.
  • Chief Commissioner and State Commissioners for Persons with Disabilities: monitor safeguards and address matters within the RPwD Act.
  • Maintenance Tribunals: adjudicate maintenance applications under the 2007 Act.
  • Local governments: can support accessible public spaces, community care, local services and social participation.
  • Health and social care institutions: provide primary healthcare, rehabilitation, palliative care, assistive services and long-term support.
  • Community organisations and civil society: support peer networks, legal awareness, helplines, caregiver support and outreach.

9. International Framework

  • UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (UNCRPD): India ratified the Convention in 2007. It promotes dignity, autonomy, non-discrimination, accessibility, inclusion and participation.
  • UN Principles for Older Persons, 1991: identify principles including independence, participation, care, self-fulfilment and dignity.
  • Madrid International Plan of Action on Ageing, 2002: provides a policy framework addressing older persons and development, health and supportive environments.
  • Sustainable Development Goals: the commitment to leave no one behind includes older persons and persons with disabilities across education, health, work, inequality and accessible communities.

Unlike the UNCRPD, the UN Principles for Older Persons and the Madrid Plan are not treaties. Their normative and policy significance should be distinguished from binding treaty obligations.

10. Major Challenges

  • Inadequate and uneven pension coverage, low benefit levels and irregular payments.
  • High out-of-pocket health expenditure and limited geriatric, rehabilitation and long-term care services.
  • Social isolation, neglect, abuse, property disputes and age discrimination.
  • Inaccessible buildings, transport, public toilets, digital services and emergency infrastructure.
  • Shortage of trained special educators, therapists, caregivers and sign-language or communication support.
  • Limited employment opportunities and workplace accommodation for persons with disabilities.
  • Stigma surrounding mental illness and inadequate community-based mental healthcare.
  • Fragmented schemes, weak awareness, complicated certification and administrative barriers.
  • Unequal access in rural, remote, tribal and underserved areas.
  • Gendered vulnerability, including older women with limited property rights, pensions or family support.

11. Way Forward

  1. Strengthen social security: improve pension adequacy, portability, coverage and timely payment while addressing the needs of informal workers.
  2. Build integrated care systems: connect primary healthcare, geriatric care, rehabilitation, mental health, palliative care and community support.
  3. Implement accessibility standards: audit public buildings, transport, digital platforms and public services, with timelines and accountability.
  4. Promote inclusive education and employment: provide reasonable accommodation, assistive technology, trained personnel and accessible workplaces.
  5. Support caregivers: expand respite care, counselling, training and community-based support for unpaid caregivers.
  6. Protect autonomy: prevent abuse and neglect while respecting the choices, privacy and legal capacity of older persons and persons with disabilities.
  7. Improve local delivery: strengthen district-level coordination, accessible grievance systems and outreach in underserved areas.
  8. Improve data and planning: use reliable age-, sex-, disability- and location-disaggregated data with privacy safeguards.
  9. Promote participation: include affected persons and their representative organisations in policy design, monitoring and evaluation.

12. Key Distinctions for Revision

  • Impairment vs disability: impairment concerns a condition or difference; disability-related exclusion also arises from interaction with environmental and social barriers.
  • Benchmark disability vs specified disability: specified disability is a category recognised under the Act; benchmark disability is a statutory threshold relevant to specified benefits and reservations.
  • Social assistance vs contributory pension: assistance is generally funded through public expenditure, while contributory pensions depend on contributions and scheme rules.
  • Maintenance vs comprehensive social security: maintenance law provides a specific remedy; it does not replace universal access to healthcare, pensions and community care.
  • Accessibility vs reasonable accommodation: accessibility is generally a system-wide design obligation; reasonable accommodation responds to individual circumstances.

13. Revision Framework

For any issue concerning older persons or persons with disabilities, analyse: constitutional guarantee; applicable rights legislation; responsible institution; accessibility and service delivery; social security; participation and autonomy; enforcement gap; and reform.

Connect dignity → equality → accessibility → autonomy → social security → participation.
