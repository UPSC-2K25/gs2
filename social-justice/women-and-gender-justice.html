1. Meaning and Scope of Gender Justice

Gender justice means ensuring equal rights, dignity, opportunities, security, resources and decision-making power irrespective of gender. It addresses both direct discrimination and structural inequalities embedded in family relations, labour markets, public institutions, social norms and access to property.

It is broader than formal legal equality. It requires substantive equality, recognition of unpaid care work, freedom from violence, bodily autonomy, economic independence, political participation and equal access to public services.

  • Equality: equal legal status and protection against discrimination.
  • Equity: measures that address unequal starting conditions and structural barriers.
  • Autonomy: ability to make decisions about education, work, marriage, health and personal life.
  • Representation: meaningful participation in political, economic and institutional decision-making.
  • Safety and dignity: protection from violence, exploitation, harassment and degrading treatment.

2. Constitutional Framework

  • Preamble: justice, liberty, equality and dignity of the individual.
  • Article 14: equality before law and equal protection of laws.
  • Article 15(1): prohibition of discrimination on grounds including sex.
  • Article 15(3): permits special provisions for women and children.
  • Article 16: equality of opportunity in public employment.
  • Article 21: life and personal liberty, including dignity, privacy and decisional autonomy.
  • Article 23: prohibition of trafficking and forced labour.
  • Article 39(a): adequate means of livelihood for men and women equally.
  • Article 39(d): equal pay for equal work for men and women.
  • Article 39(e): protection of workers' health and strength, including women.
  • Article 42: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
  • Article 51A(e): duty to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  • Articles 243D and 243T: reservation of seats for women in Panchayats and Municipalities.

3. Constitutional Amendments and Political Representation

73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendments

These amendments provide for reservation of not less than one-third of seats for women in Panchayats and Municipalities, including seats reserved for women belonging to SC and ST categories. States may provide for greater reservation. The provisions have expanded women's descriptive representation in local government, although effective participation depends on capacity, resources, social conditions and institutional support.

106th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2023

The Constitution (One Hundred and Sixth Amendment) Act, 2023 provides for reservation of approximately one-third of seats for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Legislative Assembly of the National Capital Territory of Delhi, including within seats reserved for SCs and STs. Its operation is linked to a delimitation exercise undertaken after the relevant census is published, as specified in the amendment. It should not be described as an immediately implemented reservation in current elected Houses without checking the applicable constitutional and legal position.

The amendment does not provide a corresponding reservation in the Rajya Sabha or State Legislative Councils.

5. Violence Against Women

Violence may occur in homes, workplaces, public spaces, educational institutions, online environments and conflict situations. It includes physical, sexual, psychological and economic abuse, trafficking, stalking, harassment, harmful practices and technology-facilitated abuse.

Domestic violence

The Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005 recognises physical, sexual, verbal, emotional and economic abuse within the statutory framework. It provides remedies such as protection orders, residence orders, monetary relief, custody orders and compensation. Protection Officers, service providers and Magistrates have roles under the Act.

Workplace sexual harassment

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 requires Internal Committees at covered workplaces and Local Committees at district level for specified establishments and complaints. The framework includes employer duties, complaint procedures, inquiry and confidentiality safeguards.

Criminal justice response

Criminal law addresses specified offences, investigation, prosecution and punishment. A survivor-centred response also requires timely medical assistance, privacy, legal aid, witness protection where appropriate, sensitive investigation and access to rehabilitation.

6. Economic Empowerment and the Care Economy

Women's economic empowerment depends on access to paid work, equal opportunity, decent working conditions, productive assets, credit, technology, markets and control over earnings. Labour force participation alone does not reveal job quality, wage equality, security or autonomy.

  • Recognise and reduce unpaid care and domestic work through public childcare, elder care, health services and basic infrastructure.
  • Improve access to land, housing, inheritance, credit, digital finance and productive assets.
  • Strengthen safe transport, sanitation, workplace facilities and maternity protections.
  • Support women in agriculture, informal work, entrepreneurship, cooperatives and formal employment.
  • Address occupational segregation, wage gaps, discrimination in recruitment and barriers to career progression.

The Code on Social Security, 2020 and other labour codes are relevant to social protection and working conditions, but their operative provisions must be assessed against commencement notifications and implementation status. Do not assume enactment alone means every provision is in force.

7. Health, Education and Reproductive Rights

Health and nutrition

Gender justice requires access to quality primary healthcare, reproductive and maternal health services, menstrual health, mental healthcare, nutrition and freedom from coercion. Maternal mortality, anaemia, adolescent health and barriers to healthcare are linked to poverty, education, sanitation and women's decision-making power.

Reproductive autonomy

Reproductive health policy must respect privacy, informed consent, dignity and access to lawful healthcare. The Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, as amended in 2021, provides the statutory framework for termination within specified conditions and procedures. The law should be read alongside constitutional principles and relevant judicial decisions.

Education

Girls' education is affected by household poverty, early marriage, safety, sanitation, distance, care responsibilities, disability and digital access. Retention, transition to secondary education, learning outcomes and safe institutional environments are as important as enrolment.

8. Institutional Framework

  • National Commission for Women: statutory body under the National Commission for Women Act, 1990; reviews safeguards, examines complaints and advises on policy within its mandate.
  • State Commissions for Women: address women's rights and complaints at State level under applicable laws.
  • Ministry of Women and Child Development: central ministry responsible for policy and programmes concerning women and children.
  • Internal and Local Committees: workplace sexual harassment redressal institutions under the 2013 Act.
  • Protection Officers: facilitate implementation of the Domestic Violence Act.
  • Legal Services Authorities: provide legal services and assistance to eligible persons under the Legal Services Authorities Act, 1987.
  • Local governments: enable political participation, local planning and community-level service delivery.

Commissions and committees have different legal powers. A commission's recommendations or inquiry powers should not be confused with the binding adjudicatory powers of a court.

9. Important Judicial Developments

  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997): laid down workplace sexual harassment guidelines in the absence of legislation, drawing on constitutional guarantees and international commitments; the framework preceded the 2013 Act.
  • Shayara Bano v. Union of India (2017): invalidated instant triple talaq through the majority opinions, raising questions of equality, dignity and gender justice.
  • Joseph Shine v. Union of India (2018): struck down the adultery offence under the former Indian Penal Code, rejecting a framework that treated women as subordinate to husbands.
  • Independent Thought v. Union of India (2017): read down the marital rape exception under the former IPC in relation to wives below 18 years, harmonising criminal law with child protection.
  • Secretary, Ministry of Defence v. Babita Puniya (2020): addressed permanent commission for women officers in the Army and rejected stereotypical assumptions about women's capabilities.
  • Lt. Col. Nitisha v. Union of India (2021): examined indirect discrimination and structural barriers in the evaluation of women Army officers.
  • Suchita Srivastava v. Chandigarh Administration (2009): recognised reproductive choice as part of personal liberty under Article 21.

These decisions illustrate the movement from formal non-discrimination toward substantive equality, dignity, autonomy and scrutiny of institutional stereotypes.

10. International Commitments

  • Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women (CEDAW): international framework addressing discrimination against women. India ratified it in 1993, subject to its stated reservations and declarations.
  • Beijing Declaration and Platform for Action, 1995: global framework for advancing women's equality and empowerment.
  • Sustainable Development Goal 5: achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls.
  • ILO conventions: relevant standards address equal remuneration, discrimination, maternity protection and violence and harassment in the world of work; India's ratification status must be checked convention by convention.

International commitments inform policy and interpretation but must be distinguished from directly enforceable domestic statutory provisions.

11. Major Challenges

  • Patriarchal norms, son preference and unequal control over household decisions.
  • Gender-based violence, under-reporting, low conviction rates and weak survivor support.
  • Low or unequal access to decent paid work, assets, inheritance and financial resources.
  • Disproportionate unpaid care responsibilities and inadequate childcare infrastructure.
  • Unequal access to health, nutrition, education, sanitation and safe transport.
  • Under-representation in higher political, corporate and administrative leadership.
  • Intersectional barriers affecting women from SC, ST, minority, migrant, rural and disability communities.
  • Digital gender divide, online abuse and technology-enabled surveillance or harassment.
  • Weak enforcement, fragmented institutions and limited gender-disaggregated data.

12. Way Forward

  1. Strengthen prevention and response to violence: improve policing, forensic capacity, survivor support, legal aid, shelter and time-bound justice.
  2. Expand economic agency: promote decent employment, equal opportunity, asset ownership, entrepreneurship and social security.
  3. Redistribute care responsibilities: invest in childcare, elder care, public health and infrastructure and encourage shared household responsibilities.
  4. Improve political participation: build capacity, ensure meaningful local authority and prepare institutions for effective implementation of legislative reservation.
  5. Make public services gender-responsive: use gender budgeting, safety audits, accessible facilities and sex-disaggregated outcome indicators.
  6. Promote education and social norm change: strengthen school retention, comprehensive age-appropriate education and community engagement.
  7. Address intersectionality: design services for women facing multiple and overlapping forms of disadvantage.
  8. Strengthen institutional accountability: ensure adequately resourced commissions, functional complaint committees and transparent reporting.

13. Key Distinctions for Revision

  • Equality vs equity: equal treatment may be insufficient where structural barriers create unequal starting points.
  • Women's representation vs substantive representation: presence in institutions does not automatically ensure influence over decisions.
  • Protection vs empowerment: protection from harm is essential, but empowerment also requires autonomy, resources and agency.
  • Workforce participation vs decent work: employment data must be assessed alongside wages, security, working conditions and unpaid care.
  • Gender-neutral vs gender-responsive policy: identical rules can have unequal effects where social conditions differ.

14. Revision Framework

For a gender justice issue, organise analysis under: constitutional guarantee; structural inequality; applicable statute; responsible institution; implementation gap; judicial interpretation; intersectional impact; and reform.

Connect equality → dignity → autonomy → economic agency → representation → substantive justice.
